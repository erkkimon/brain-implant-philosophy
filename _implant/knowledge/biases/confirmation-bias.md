# Confirmation bias

Family: judgement. Written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
every classification and interpretation below is attributed. Excerpts in
`raw/` under the file names given.

## Also known as

- *Myside bias* — the form in which people produce or recall reasons for
  the side they favour and not the other; Nickerson reports the term from
  Baron (1991, 1995) and Perkins et al. (1983, 1991)
  ([Nickerson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175), p. 178;
  excerpt: `raw/nickerson-1998-confirmation-bias-definition.md`).
- *Biased assimilation* — the interpretive form: the same evidence read as
  supporting opposed prior views
  ([Lord, Ross & Lepper 1979](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.11.2098),
  as reported by Nickerson 1998, p. 187).
- *Positive test strategy* — not a synonym but the name Klayman & Ha give
  to what they argue much of the evidence actually shows (below).
- *Motivated reasoning* — a neighbouring term ([Kunda 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480))
  for the case in which the partiality serves a wish; Nickerson's definition
  deliberately covers the *unwitting* case only.

## The effect as claimed

The descriptive definition the literature converges on is Nickerson's:

> "Confirmation bias, as the term is typically used in the psychological
> literature, connotes the seeking or interpreting of evidence in ways
> that are partial to existing beliefs, expectations, or a hypothesis in
> hand." ([Nickerson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175), p. 175)

Nickerson separates it from deliberate advocacy: the term "refers usually
to unwitting selectivity in the acquisition and use of evidence" (p. 175),
and it "does not necessarily mean completely ignoring the counterindicative
information but means being less receptive to it than to supportive
information" (p. 178). The same review opens by wondering whether the bias
"by itself, might account for a significant fraction of the disputes,
altercations, and misunderstandings that occur among individuals, groups,
and nations" (p. 175) — the widest claim made for it, and Nickerson's own.

The canonical study is [Wason 1960](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470216008416717)
(excerpt: `raw/wason-1960-2-4-6-abstract.md`). Participants were shown a
number triplet and asked to discover the experimenter's rule by proposing
further triplets, each answered yes or no. Per Nickerson's report, given
2-4-6 "people were likely to come up with the hypothesis successive even
numbers and then proceed to test this hypothesis by generating additional
sets of successive even numbers" (p. 179), while the rule was any ascending
sequence. Wason's abstract reports: "Six out of 29 subjects reached the
correct conclusion without previous incorrect ones, 13 reached one
incorrect conclusion, nine reached two or more incorrect conclusions, and
one reached no conclusion." Wason frames the task as a test of enumerative
versus eliminative induction; the abstract does not use the phrase
"confirmation bias".

## Evidence

- **Canonical study.** Wason 1960, N = 29; the headline is the count above,
  not an effect size — the paper predates that convention.
- **Interpretive form.** Lord, Ross & Lepper 1979 gave proponents and
  opponents of capital punishment the same two studies (one supporting
  each side); Nickerson's report is that "two people with initially
  conflicting views can examine the same evidence and both find reasons
  for increasing the strength of their existing opinions" (p. 187). The
  primary text was not read for this page; the claim rests on Nickerson's
  report.
- **Reviews.** Nickerson 1998 is a narrative review of several dozen
  paradigms; Mercier & Sperber 2011 write that "it is one of the most
  studied biases in psychology" and that "while there is some individual
  variation, it seems that everybody is affected to some degree,
  irrespective of factors like general intelligence or open mindedness"
  (p. 63, citing Stanovich & West 2007, 2008; excerpt:
  `raw/mercier-sperber-2011-argumentative-theory.md`).
- **Replication record.** The 2-4-6 finding has been reproduced across the
  hypothesis-testing literature Klayman & Ha review; what is disputed is
  not whether people behave that way but what the behaviour *is* (next
  section). No registered multi-site replication of the 1979 study is
  cited on this page; that is a gap in this page, not a finding about the
  effect.

**Evidence grade: moderate.** The behaviour in the canonical tasks is
reproduced; the downgrade is that the field disagrees about whether the
behaviour is a bias (Klayman & Ha), what its function is (Mercier &
Sperber), and that the interpretive-form evidence here is second-hand.
Grades follow [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).

## Boundary conditions

- **Task structure.** Klayman & Ha's analysis makes the outcome depend on
  the relation between the hypothesised rule and the true rule: a positive
  test strategy "can be a very good heuristic for determining the truth or
  falsity of a hypothesis under realistic conditions. It can, however, lead
  to systematic errors or inefficiencies"
  ([Klayman & Ha 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.94.2.211), p. 211;
  excerpt: `raw/klayman-ha-1987-positive-test-strategy.md`). Wason's task
  is built so that the hypothesised rule is a subset of the true one, the
  case in which positive tests cannot falsify.
- **Social context.** Mercier & Sperber: "When one is alone or with people
  who hold similar views, one's arguments will not be critically evaluated.
  This is when the confirmation bias is most likely to lead to poor
  outcomes" — whereas "in arguments among people who disagree but have a
  common interest in the truth" it "contributes to an efficient form of
  division of cognitive labor" (p. 65).
- **Deliberate vs unwitting.** By Nickerson's definition the term does not
  cover conscious one-sided case-building (p. 175); a lawyer's brief is
  advocacy, not the bias.

## Interpretations and critique

- **A flaw of hypothesis testing** — the received reading Nickerson
  reviews, on which people "tend to test those cases that have the best
  chance of verifying current beliefs rather than those that have the best
  chance of falsifying them" (Klayman & Ha's summary of the view, p. 211).
- **A positive test strategy, not a preference for confirmation** —
  Klayman & Ha 1987: "many phenomena labeled 'confirmation bias' are
  better understood in terms of a general positive test strategy … This
  strategy is not equivalent to confirmation bias in the first sense"
  (p. 211). Nickerson accepts the reinterpretation for the Wason task
  (p. 200) while keeping the term for the broader family.
- **A feature of reasoning's function** — Mercier & Sperber 2011: "For
  standard theories of reasoning, the confirmation bias is no more than a
  flaw of reasoning. For the argumentative theory, however, it is a
  consequence of the function of reasoning and hence a feature of
  reasoning when used for the production of arguments" (p. 63). Their
  commentators — De Neys, Poletiek, Stupple & Ball, Wolfe — are reported
  by the authors as having "reservations about the prevalence and
  robustness of this bias".
- **Adaptive-behaviour readings of the neighbouring selection task** —
  Nickerson reports Cosmides (1989) and others as holding that performance
  "is indicative of behavior that has proved to be adaptively effective in
  analogous real-world situations" (p. 186). This concerns the Wason
  *selection* task, a different paradigm; it is listed because the same
  ecological argument is made against calling either a bias.

## How to spot it

Markers the sources give:

- Tests proposed only from inside the hypothesis — every probe an
  instance the hypothesis predicts, none an instance it forbids (the 2-4-6
  pattern; Nickerson p. 179).
- Asymmetric scrutiny — counter-evidence met with "seek to discredit it or
  to explain it away" while supporting evidence passes unexamined
  (Nickerson p. 178).
- One-sided reason lists — reasons for the favoured side recalled or
  produced, reasons for the other side not, though the person can produce
  them when explicitly asked (Nickerson's report of Perkins et al., p. 178).
- Both sides strengthened by the same evidence — the biased-assimilation
  signature (Lord, Ross & Lepper 1979 via Nickerson p. 187).
- In text: a claim followed only by supporting instances; a source's
  counter-cases absent rather than answered.

## Debiasing

- **Consider the opposite.** Nickerson: people "could be encouraged to
  attempt to identify reasons why the complement of the hypothesis in hand
  might be true. Again, the evidence provides reason for optimism that the
  approach can work" (p. 211, citing Anderson 1982, Anderson & Sechler
  1986, and [Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.47.6.1231)).
  The same intervention is the one with published support against
  [anchoring](anchoring.md).
- **Change the social context.** The argumentative theory's own
  prescription follows from its boundary condition: reasoning "among
  people who disagree but have a common interest in the truth" (Mercier &
  Sperber p. 65). This is a theoretical implication the authors draw, not
  a tested intervention on this page.
- Nickerson adds a caution: where apparent biases are "the results of
  efforts to avoid certain types of decision errors", debiasing "might not
  be desirable" if the errors avoided are the costlier ones (p. 211).

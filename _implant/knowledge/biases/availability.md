# Availability heuristic

Family: judgement (frequency and probability). Written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
every classification is attributed. Excerpts in `raw/` under the file
names given.

## Also known as

- *Availability bias* — the name for the error the heuristic produces; the
  source paper names the *heuristic* and calls its errors "systematic
  biases" ([Tversky & Kahneman 1973](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90033-9),
  reprint p. 164; excerpt: `raw/tversky-kahneman-1973-availability-definition.md`).
- *Ease of retrieval* — the mechanism Schwarz et al. argue actually carries
  the effect ([Schwarz et al. 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.61.2.195);
  excerpt: `raw/schwarz-et-al-1991-ease-of-retrieval.md`).
- *Vividness / salience effects* and *the availability cascade* are
  neighbouring terms not treated on this page.

## The effect as claimed

The definition is Tversky & Kahneman's:

> "A person is said to employ the availability heuristic whenever he
> estimates frequency or probability by the ease with which instances or
> associations could be brought to mind. To assess availability it is not
> necessary to perform the actual operations of retrieval or construction.
> It suffices to assess the ease with which these operations could be
> performed" (1973, reprint p. 164).

The same page states both halves of the claim in the authors' voice:
"Availability is an ecologically valid clue for the judgment of frequency
because, in general, frequent events are easier to recall or imagine than
infrequent ones. However, availability is also affected by various factors
which are unrelated to actual frequency. … Consequently, the use of the
availability heuristic leads to systematic biases" (p. 164). The bias, on
the source's own account, is what happens when ease and frequency come
apart — fame, recency, vividness, ease of construction — not a claim that
the heuristic is generally wrong.

The canonical demonstration is Study 8: recorded lists of 39 names in which
"fame and frequency were inversely related in all lists" (19 famous women
with 20 less famous men, or the reverse). "Among the 99 subjects who
compared the frequency of men and women in the lists, 80 erroneously
judged the class consisting of the more famous names to be more frequent
(p < .001, by sign test)" (p. 175). Study 3 is the K-words question: "Is
it more likely that the word starts with a K, or that K is its third
letter? … It is certainly easier to think of words that start with a K
than of words where K is in the third position" (p. 166).

## Evidence

- **Canonical studies.** Ten studies, approximately 1500 participants in
  total per the reprint's footnote 2 (Oregon, Stanford, Israeli high
  schools); Study 8 N = 99 for the frequency judgement.
- **Field application.** [Lichtenstein, Slovic, Fischhoff, Layman & Combs 1978](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.551)
  (excerpt: `raw/lichtenstein-et-al-1978-lethal-events.md`) had people
  judge the frequency of 41 causes of death. The abstract reports "(a) a
  tendency to overestimate small frequencies and underestimate larger
  ones, and (b) a tendency to exaggerate the frequency of some specific
  causes and to underestimate the frequency of others, at any given level
  of objective frequency", traced "to a number of possible sources,
  including disproportionate exposure, memorability, or imaginability",
  and adds that "subjects were unable to correct for these sources of bias
  when specifically instructed to avoid them" (p. 551).
- **Mechanism study.** Schwarz et al. 1991 asked people to recall six or
  twelve examples of their own assertive behaviour and then rate their
  assertiveness; per the indexed abstract, the effect of what was recalled
  "held only for those subjects who were asked to recall six examples …
  which they found easy", and reversed at twelve, which they found
  difficult; the effect was eliminated when the difficulty could be
  misattributed to an external cause (Experiment 3). Only the abstract was
  read for this page.
- **Replication record.** No registered multi-site replication of Study 8
  or of the 1991 study is cited on this page; that is a gap in this page.
  The 1973 findings are among the most-cited in the heuristics programme,
  which is a fact about citation, not about replication.

**Evidence grade: moderate.** The laboratory demonstrations are original
and consistent across paradigms and the field study points the same way;
the downgrade is the absence, on this page, of a pre-registered
replication and the fact that the mechanism study is cited from its
abstract. Grades follow
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).

## Boundary conditions

- **Ease and frequency aligned.** Where the easily recalled *is* the
  frequent, the heuristic gives the right answer; this is the authors' own
  "ecologically valid" case (p. 164). The bias needs a wedge — fame,
  publicity, recency, personal exposure, ease of construction.
- **Experienced ease, not count.** On Schwarz et al.'s reading, recalling
  *more* instances can *lower* the judgement if the recall felt hard;
  interventions that make people list many instances can therefore
  backfire (indexed abstract, Experiments 1–2).
- **Misattribution removes it.** When the felt difficulty is blamed on
  something else, the effect disappears (Experiment 3) — which is a
  finding about the mechanism and also a lever (below).
- **Instruction does not remove it.** Lichtenstein et al.: told to avoid
  the bias, participants "were unable to correct" (p. 551).

## Interpretations and critique

- **A heuristic with systematic errors** — the source view, above.
- **An ease-of-retrieval experience used as information** — Schwarz et
  al. 1991 reframe the mechanism from "how many instances come to mind"
  to "how it feels to bring them to mind"; the 1973 definition already
  speaks of "the ease with which" rather than the number, so the 1991
  work is offered as a sharpening, on its authors' account.
- **Ecological-rationality readings** — Gigerenzer's programme treats
  heuristics as adapted tools whose accuracy depends on the environment
  ([Gigerenzer 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.103.3.592), on
  Kahneman & Tversky's norms; excerpt:
  `raw/gigerenzer-1996-narrow-norms.md`); on that reading the K-words and
  fame studies are environments built to break a tool that is usually
  right. This page reports the disagreement; it does not settle it.
- **Risk-perception literature** — Lichtenstein et al. present the
  lethal-events result as bearing on "the management of societal
  hazards" (p. 551); the later "availability cascade" and "dread risk"
  literatures build on it and are not covered here.

## How to spot it

Markers the sources give:

- A frequency or probability judgement made without a count, where a
  count exists (the K-words case: the corpus is checkable).
- The judged-frequent class is the more *famous*, *recent*, *vivid* or
  *publicised* one (Study 8; Lichtenstein et al.'s "dramatic events …
  publicized disproportionately", p. 552).
- Rare-but-dramatic causes rated above common-but-quiet ones (the "quiet
  killers" pattern, p. 552).
- In text: "you hear about it all the time", "I can think of several
  cases", "everyone knows someone who" — recall offered as a base rate.
- Reversal on effort: a judgement that changes when the person is asked
  to list more instances than come easily (the 6-vs-12 signature).

## Debiasing

- **Replace recall with a count.** The direct move: if the class can be
  enumerated or its base rate looked up, do that instead of assessing
  ease (the 1973 paper's own contrast between an "objective procedure for
  enumerating instances" and the heuristic, p. 175). The
  [estimate-a-credence](https://github.com/erkkimon/brain-implant-cognitive-tools/blob/main/_implant/skills/estimate-a-credence.md) skill in the cognitive-tools
  implant is built to force this step.
- **Attribute the ease.** Schwarz et al.'s Experiment 3: when the
  experienced difficulty is credited to an external cause, the effect
  disappears. As an intervention this is a theoretical implication of the
  mechanism, not a tested debiasing procedure on this page.
- **What does not work on the evidence here:** telling people to avoid the
  bias (Lichtenstein et al., p. 551); asking for *more* examples (Schwarz
  et al., where it reversed the judgement rather than correcting it).

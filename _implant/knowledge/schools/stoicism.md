# Stoicism

All citations on this page are to Marion Durand, "Stoicism", SEP (Fall
2024). Excerpt: `raw/sep-stoicism-fall-2024-school-and-doctrines.md`.
Durand cites ancient texts by their Long & Sedley number (e.g. "Stobaeus,
63B").

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Founded** "around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium" (§1.1).
- **Name.** "The name derives from the porch (stoa poikilê) in the Agora
  at Athens decorated with mural paintings, where the first generation of
  Stoic philosophers congregated and lectured" (preamble).
- **Heads.** Zeno was succeeded "first by Cleanthes of Assos and then by
  Chrysippus of Soli". Chrysippus headed the school "from around 230 until
  206 BCE and was by all accounts its foremost theorist and systematizer"
  (§1.1).
- **Periods.** Some scholars divide the school into an "Old Stoa" and
  "Middle Stoicism". Durand reports that "the relevance and accuracy of
  this nomenclature is debated (see Inwood 2022)" (§1.1). The Roman
  imperial period follows.

## Defining commitments

- **A three-part system.** Stoic philosophy was "from Zeno onwards,
  conceived of as comprising three parts: physics (phusikê), logic
  (logikê), and ethics (êthikê)". The parts were thought "to form an
  interconnected and coherent system". Durand adds: "exactly how strongly
  the claim of systematization is to be taken is disputed" (§1.2).
- **Propositional logic.** Durand: the Stoic definition of argument "made
  the Stoic system one of what we might now call propositional logic, by
  contrast with Aristotle’s logic of terms". Its five "indemonstrables"
  include modus ponens and modus tollens (§3.4). See
  [classical logic](../methods/classical-logic.md), which traces
  propositional logic partly to Chrysippus, and
  [validity](../vocabulary/validity.md).
- **The end (telos).** Zeno: "‘living in agreement’". Cleanthes added
  "‘with nature’": "‘the end is living in agreement with nature’"
  (Stobaeus, 63B, quoted §4.1).

Members disagreed with each other. Durand: "The nature and extent of these
intra-school developments and disagreements [...] remains the subject of
ongoing scholarly debate" (§1.1).

## Members

Each member is placed in the school by Durand, §1.1.

- Zeno of Citium, the founder.
- Cleanthes of Assos and Chrysippus of Soli, the next heads.
- Diogenes of Babylon, "the last" of Chrysippus' former students to hold
  the headship. Antipater of Tarsus, his successor.
- Panaetius and Posidonius, 2nd–1st c. BCE.
- Roman-period figures: Arius Didymus, Seneca ("advisor to Nero"),
  Epictetus ("a former slave"), Hierocles, and Marcus Aurelius, whom
  Epictetus' works "heavily influenced".

Cicero is not a member. Durand: "never identifying as a Stoic himself". He
"engaged extensively with Stoic theory".

## Problems characteristic of the school

Durand's section headings name them. In physics: causes and determinism
(§2.8). In logic: propositions, modality, paradoxes and epistemology
(§3.3–3.7). In ethics: the telos, virtue, indifferents and the passions
(§4). The implant has no problem pages for these yet. Free will and

## Contrasts and exchanges

- **Epicureans.** "The Stoa competed with the school founded only a little
  before in Athens by Epicurus, and Stoic and Epicurean views are often
  compared and contrasted" (§1.1).
- **Academic sceptics.** Durand reports "the sharp and powerful attacks
  against Stoic views formulated by the Academic Carneades". Antipater
  "responded at length" (§1.1).
- **Platonists and Aristotelians.** Diogenes and Antipater "heralded
  increased engagement with Platonic [...] and possibly Aristotelian
  doctrine" (§1.1).

# Buridan's ass

## The question

Zupko's statement of the case: "the example which has come down to us known as ‘Buridan’s Ass’, in which a donkey starves to death because it has no reason to choose between two equidistant and equally tempting piles of hay" (SEP §7).
If nothing favours either option, is a choice possible, and by what? The
texts below differ over whether perfect equality can obtain, whether a will
can select without a reason, and whether starving is the consequence
(structural note).

**The name and Buridan.** Zupko: "This particular example is nowhere to be found in Buridan’s writings, although there are versions of it going back at least to Aristotle (see De Caelo 295b32)." (§7).
He judges that "The best explanation of its association with Buridan is that it originated as a parody of his account of free choice by later critics" — critics who found absurd a freedom consisting in the will's ability to "defer or ‘send back’ for further consideration any practical judgment which is not absolutely certain, leaving the donkey free, but dead" (§7; the assessment is Zupko's).
Buridan's doctrine, as Zupko reports it: the will "is still able to defer its choice and do nothing if the goodness of one of the alternatives presented to it is unclear or uncertain" (§7).
Zupko (n. 57) cites Spinoza's *Ethics* II P49 scholium as associating the
example with Buridan, and a use eighty years earlier in the *Logicae
Assertiones* (1597) of Schönlin, Carpentarius, Gutbrott and Faust, §43,
whose Latin reads "cuiusmodi fertur fuisse asinus Buridani".
The Britannica Editors report a different animal: "the animal mentioned in Buridan’s commentary on Aristotle’s De caelo (“On the Heavens”) is actually a dog, not an ass", and say Buridan "concludes that the dog must choose at random" ("Jean Buridan"; excerpt: `raw/dante-paradiso-4-1-9-between-two-viands-longfellow.md`).
That commentary was not read for this page; the two reports are recorded
side by side, unreconciled.

**Senses** (structural note). Bayle's "kind of dilemma" (Arguments in
play) has the argument form of [Dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md) sense 1;
the case is not a moral dilemma in the sense of
[Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?](moral-dilemmas.md), since its
options are equal goods, not conflicting requirements.

## Why it matters

- **Free will.** Lamport reports that "philosophers have viewed Buridan’s Ass as a paradox, discussing it in connection with the problem of free will" (2012, §2; excerpt: `raw/lamport-1984-2012-buridans-principle.md`).
  Bayle writes that those "who hold free-will, properly so called" admit a power of choosing "even when the motives are exactly equal" (*Dictionary*, "Buridan", rem. C; excerpt: `raw/bayle-1697-dictionary-buridan-remark-c-ass.md`).
- **Divine will and creation.** al-Ghazali's two dates answer the
  philosophers' objection to a will that singles out one of two like
  things, in the dispute over the world's origination (*Incoherence*, First
  Discussion §§42–46; excerpt:
  `raw/al-ghazali-tahafut-discussion-1-two-equal-dates-marmura-kamali.md`).
  Melamed & Lin report that "God, on the mainstream medieval view, enjoys freedom of indifference with respect to his creation" (SEP "Principle of Sufficient Reason" §5; excerpt: `raw/sep-sufficient-reason-fall-2024-anaximander-and-indiscernibles.md`).
- **The principle of sufficient reason.** Melamed & Lin read the
  indifference argument Aristotle reports as an early use of the PSR (§5);
  Leibniz answers the ass from his denial of an "indifference of
  equipoise" (*Theodicy* §§46, 49).
- **Computing.** Lamport: "In computing, a device that makes a discrete (usually binary) decision based upon a continuous input value is called an arbiter, and Buridan’s Principle is usually known as the Arbiter Problem" (§5).
- **Rational choice.** Ullmann-Margalit: where preferences are "completely symmetrical", "it looks like we have to conclude that in such cases rational choice is precluded" (2006, p. 157; excerpt: `raw/ullmann-margalit-2006-big-decisions-picking-and-1977-record.md`).

## Positions taken

No survey of the question was found. The literature read does not group
the answers into families; the list below follows the texts, unranked,
each with the case for and against as stated by named authors.

1. **The equal case is possible and the agent stays put.** Spinoza: "I am quite ready to admit, that a man placed in the equilibrium described  (namely, as perceiving nothing but hunger and thirst, a certain food and a certain drink, each equally distant from him) would die of hunger and thirst." (*Ethics* II P49 schol., Elwes tr.; excerpt: `raw/spinoza-1676-ethics-2-p49-scholium-buridans-ass-elwes.md`).
   *For:* the objection's other horn, as Spinoza states it: if the man would not starve, "he would then determine his own action, and would consequently possess the faculty of going and doing whatever he liked".
   *Against:* Bayle calls "his concession" "very ill grounded" and offers two escapes (position 4); al-Ghazali calls endless indecision "impossible, its falsity known by [rational] necessity" (§46).
2. **A will that specifies without a reason (liberty of indifference).** al-Ghazali: the man "will inevitably take one of them through an attribute whose function is to render a thing specific, [differentiating it] from its like." (§46, Marmura tr.).
   Bayle reports the same power as held by defenders of free will "properly so called": the soul can say "I chuse this rather than that, though I see nothing more worthy of my choice in this than in that" (rem. C).
   *For:* al-Ghazali's dilemma — equality can be supposed, and endless indecision is impossible (§46).
   *Against:* the philosophers al-Ghazali answers: "it would be impossible for him to take either" (§43); Leibniz: "The mere desire to make use of one's freedom has no effect of specifying, or determining us to the choice of one course or the other." (§305), and such a choice "would be, as it were, mere chance, without determining reason" (§303; excerpt: `raw/leibniz-1710-theodicy-49-and-303-307-buridans-ass-huggard.md`).
3. **Perfect equipoise cannot occur in nature.** Leibniz: the case "is a fiction that cannot occur in the universe, in the order of Nature, although M. Bayle be of another opinion" (§49).
   *For:* "the universe cannot be halved by a plane drawn through the middle of the ass" so that all is equal on both sides; there will "always be many things in the ass and outside the ass, although they be not apparent to us, which will determine him" (§49). He concedes: "if the case were possible, one must say that the ass would starve himself to death" (§49).
   *Against:* al-Ghazali: to say equality "is utterly inconceivable" is "sheer foolishness, the supposition [of this equality] being possible" (§46); Leibniz himself names Bayle as "of another opinion" (§49).
4. **Break the tie by self-assertion or by lot.** Bayle: "there are at least two ways whereby a man may disengage himself from the snare of the equilibrium" — resolving "I will prefer this before that, because I will have it so", and "the lot or chance"; with a lot "the equilibrium would not keep him in a state of inaction, as Spinoza pretends" (rem. C).
   *For:* Bayle's example of deciding precedence between two ladies by drawing lots (rem. C).
   *Against:* Leibniz: "because it pleases me" implies "already a leaning towards 'the object that pleases'" (§306); by the lot, "it is not man who decides", or "man himself is no longer in equipoise, because the lot is not" (§307).
5. **The will may defer.** Buridan's doctrine as Zupko reports it (above):
   the will can "defer its choice and do nothing" when the better option is
   unclear, a deferment possible only if "the intellect would judge it to be good to consider the matter further" (§7, quoting QNE III.5).
   *For / against:* Zupko reports the ass as critics' parody of this
   doctrine, "leaving the donkey free, but dead" (§7); no defence of it
   against the parody was found in the sources read.
6. **Picking, not choosing.** Ullmann-Margalit & Morgenbesser (1977)
   distinguish the two; in Ullmann-Margalit's later wording: "One chooses for reasons; one picks when reasons cannot prevail. This happens when the alternatives are entirely symmetrical (or incommensurate)." (2006, p. 171).
   *For:* her supermarket example, picking "a can of Campbell soup from the shelves" (2006, p. 157).
   *Against:* no criticism of the distinction was found in the sources read; the 1977 paper itself was not read. She introduces it after quoting Leibniz: "In things which are absolutely indifferent there can be no choice" (2006, p. 157).
7. **A physical theorem: some starting positions starve.** Lamport: "Buridan’s Principle. A discrete decision based upon an input having a continuous range of values cannot be made within a bounded length of time." (§1).
   *For:* his continuity argument, "The key assumption in this argument is continuity" (§1); to a driver who takes some other action when undecided he replies that this "merely pushes the decision back one level" (§2); to noise, "Randomness can make it impossible deliberately to starve the ass, but it cannot prevent the ass from accidentally starving." (§2).
   *Against:* the escapes Lamport reports people propose (§2), each
   answered by him as above; no published critique was fetched.

## Arguments in play

**Bayle's dilemma (reconstruction).** Bayle conjectures the ass "might be a sophism, which that philosopher proposed as a kind of dilemma": if the ass stays, "he must die of hunger"; if it turns, "then he is endowed with a free will"; "These two consequences are absurd", so the ass "must ever find himself more strongly moved by one of the objects than by the other" (rem. C).
Formalised by this implant (logic, not a position): E = the two objects
act equally; Stay; Turn; Die; Free. `logic.py check --premises "E -> (Stay | Turn)" "Stay -> Die" "Turn -> Free" "~Die" "~Free" --conclusion "~E"`
prints, after listing the premises:

```
  C:  ~E    [~E]

VALID
```

Dropping "~Free" (granting the free-will horn) prints "INVALID" with the
counterexample "Die=F, E=T, Free=T, Stay=F, Turn=T". The form is a
destructive dilemma ([Dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md),
[Validity](../vocabulary/validity.md)); which horn to reject is what
separates positions 1 (reject ~Die), 2 (reject ~Free) and 3 (accept ~E).

**Lamport's continuity argument** (§1): with the ass's position after
time *t* a continuous function of its start *x*, A_t(0) = 0 and
A_t(1) = 1, he concludes that "there exists a finite range of starting positions for which the ass starves to death".
He writes that "With proper circuit design, the probability of not having reached a decision by time t is an exponentially decreasing function of t" (§5).
Related: [Newcomb's problem](newcombs-problem.md) and
[the St Petersburg paradox](st-petersburg-paradox.md) are the branch's
other decision-theory cases; no source read links them to this one.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Anaximander**, as Aristotle reports: "the earth keeps its place because of its indifference" (*De Caelo* II.13, 295b10–16; Stocks tr.; excerpt: `raw/aristotle-de-caelo-2-13-295b-indifference-hungry-man-stocks.md`).
- **Aristotle** (*De Caelo* II.13, 295b32): cites as an analogy "the men who, though exceedingly hungry and thirsty, and both equally, yet being equidistant from food and drink, is therefore bound to stay where he is"; of the indifference view: "This view is ingenious but not true."
- **al-Ghazali** (*Tahafut*, written 1091–1095 per Marmura's introduction), First Discussion §46: the two equal dates; position 2.
- **Dante** (*Paradiso* IV.1–3, Longfellow tr.; completed by 1321 per Wetherbee, SEP "Dante"): "Between two viands, equally removed / And tempting, a free man would die of hunger".
  No source read connects these lines to Buridan.
- **John Buridan** (born "around 1300", alive in 1358: Zupko §1): the deferring will; position 5; the ass not in his writings (Zupko).
- **Schönlin, Carpentarius, Gutbrott & Faust**, *Logicae Assertiones* (1597) §43, as Zupko translates: "whoever attributes freedom to a brute animal is mistaken, whether in the case where it is not inclined to walk to one place more than another, or of the kind raised by Buridan’s ass" (n. 57).
- **Spinoza**, *Ethics* (completed 1676, per Zupko n. 57) II P49 schol.: position 1.
- **Pierre Bayle**, *Dictionary* (vol. II 1697 per the 1826 "Life"), "Buridan" rem. C: two conjectures on Buridan's use; position 4; and "I cannot tell whether I guess right what it was".
- **G. W. Leibniz**, *Theodicy* (1710) §§46, 49, 303–307: position 3 and the replies to Bayle.
- **Edna Ullmann-Margalit & Sidney Morgenbesser**, "Picking and Choosing",
  *Social Research* 44(4) (1977): 757–785 (bibliographic record only);
  Ullmann-Margalit 2006, [doi:10.1017/s1358246100009358](https://doi.org/10.1017/s1358246100009358): position 6.
- **Leslie Lamport**, "Buridan's Principle", written 1984, *Foundations of
  Physics* 42 (2012): 1056–1066,
  [doi:10.1007/s10701-012-9647-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10701-012-9647-7): position 7.

Further reading (DOI-verified, not read): Nicholas Rescher, "Choice

## Framings and reframings

- **Equilibrium and will.** In the *De Caelo* passage the hungry man is
  one of two analogies (with an evenly stretched hair) in Aristotle's
  report of an argument about why the earth stays at the centre; in
  al-Ghazali the same shape of case bears on will (§§43, 46) (structural
  note, from the two excerpts).
- **A parody, not a thesis.** Zupko's reading: the example is critics'
  caricature of Buridan's deferring will (§7).
- **A sophism.** Bayle's second conjecture, and his third: "the sophism called Buridan's ass may be only the Pons Asinorum of the logicians, mentioned by Rabelais" (rem. C).
- **A physical law.** Lamport: "Philosophers have discussed Buridan's ass for centuries, but it apparently never occurred to any of them that the planet is not littered with dead asses only because the probability of the ass being in just the right spot is infinitesimal." (author's publications page).
- **Not choice at all.** Ullmann-Margalit's "picking" (position 6).

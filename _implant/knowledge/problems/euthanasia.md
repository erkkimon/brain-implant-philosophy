# Euthanasia and assisted dying

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Map: Robert Young,
[SEP Fall 2024 "Voluntary Euthanasia"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/euthanasia-voluntary/) (rev. 2024-03-09;
excerpt: `raw/sep-euthanasia-voluntary-fall-2024-young.md`). Young states the case for legalisation and then replies to each objection in
his own voice; every assessment cited to him is his.

## The question

Young (§1): "When a person performs an act of euthanasia, she brings about the death of another person because she believes the latter’s
present existence is so bad that he would be better off dead, or believes that unless she intervenes and ends his life, his life will very
soon become so bad that he would be better off dead."

Young (§1) distinguishes three kinds by the person's will:

- **Voluntary** —
  "that is, with those instances of euthanasia in which a clearly competent person makes a voluntary and enduring request to be helped to die".
- **Non-voluntary** — "instances of euthanasia where a person lacks the competence at the time when a decision is to be made to request
  euthanasia and has not previously competently declared a preference for it via an advance directive".
- **Involuntary** — "where a competent person’s life is brought to an end despite an explicit expression of opposition to euthanasia",
  which Young says "is, and ought to be, unlawful".

**Physician-assisted suicide**: the term "is usually restricted to forms of assistance which stop short of the physician ‘bringing about
the death’ of the patient, for example, those involving means that have to be activated by the patient" (§1). **Active and passive**: in
passive voluntary euthanasia "life-sustaining or life-prolonging measures are withdrawn or withheld in response to a competent patient’s
request" (§4.4). Young (§2) lists five conditions "often said to be necessary" for candidacy, among them that the person "is suffering
from a terminal illness;", "has an enduring, voluntary and competent wish to die" and "is unable without assistance to end her life,"; he
adds: "It should be acknowledged that these conditions are quite restrictive, indeed more restrictive than many think appropriate."

## Why it matters

- **The law.** Young's 2024 revision (§1) reports: "In November 2000, the Netherlands passed legislation to legalize the practice of
  voluntary euthanasia." "The Belgian parliament passed similar legislation in 2002 and Luxembourg followed suit in 2009." Oregon
  legislated "in 1997 to permit physician-assisted suicide"; "In 2021, Spain legalized voluntary euthanasia"; Canada's national parliament
  legislated in 2016 for "both physician-assisted suicide and voluntary euthanasia"; in Australia, with NSW's 2022 act in force in 2023,
  it became "available in each of the states"; in Switzerland assisted suicide "has been legally tolerated [...] for a number of decades".
  (Young, March 2024; not checked against statute).
- **Medical ethics.** Rachels (1975) addresses the American Medical Association's 1973 statement: "The intentional termination of the life
  of one human being by another—mercy killing—is contrary to that for which the medical profession stands and is contrary to the policy of
  the American Medical Association."
- **Doing and allowing.** Woollard ([SEP "Doing vs. Allowing Harm"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/doing-allowing/),
  rev. 2021-07-07, preamble; excerpt: `raw/sep-doing-allowing-and-double-effect-fall-2024-euthanasia.md`) opens with it: if there is no
  moral difference between doing and allowing harm, "there should be no moral objection to active euthanasia in circumstances where
  passive euthanasia is permissible".

## Positions taken

Grouped as Young groups them (§§3–4): a two-part case for, five objections. Not ranked. **1. Permissible, on autonomy and well-being
together.** Young (§3): "One central ethical contention in support of voluntary euthanasia is that respect for persons demands respect for
their autonomous choices as long as those choices do not result in harm to others." "A second contention in support of voluntary
euthanasia was mentioned at the beginning of this entry, namely the importance of promoting the well-being of persons." Young's own view
(§3): "Each consideration is necessary for moral justification of the practice, but taken in isolation neither suffices (see, e.g., Young
2007, 2017; Sumner 2011, 2017)." *Against:* positions 3–6. Keown (2022, p. 87) draws from the well-being premise the consequence set out
in position 6: "In short, the absence of autonomy does not cancel the duty of beneﬁcence." Young (§3) reports that others "maintain that
individuals can be in error about whether their lives continue to be worth living (cf., Foot 1977)".

**2. Active euthanasia is permissible where passive is (Rachels 1975).** James Rachels,
["Active and Passive Euthanasia"](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197501092920206), *NEJM* 292(2): 78–80 (excerpt:
`raw/rachels-1975-active-and-passive-euthanasia.md`). *For:* withholding treatment "may take the patient longer to die, and so he may
suffer more than he would if more direct action were taken and a lethal injection given" (p. 2). The contrast pair: "In the first, Smith
stands to gain a large inheritance if anything should happen to his six-year-old cousin." Smith drowns the child; Jones, planning the
same, finds the child already face down, and "he stands by, ready to push the child's head back under if it is necessary, but it is not
necessary" (pp. 3–4). "That is the only difference between them." Rachels: "the bare difference between killing and letting die does not,
in itself, make a moral difference" (p. 4). *Against:* Woollard (§1): "Rachels’ argument depends on two claims: (1) the two cases are
exactly alike except that one is a killing and the other a letting die; (2) that there is no moral difference between the cases. Both
these claims have been challenged." Hills (2018) "challenges (1)", since "Smith does not have the option to merely let his cousin die";
"Kamm claims that it would be permissible to kill Smith to bring the child back to life but impermissible to kill Jones for this reason
(Kamm 2007, 17)"; Kagan (1988) argues that the inference assumes that "if a factor has genuine moral relevance, then for any pair of
cases, where the given factor varies while others are held constant, the cases in that pair will differ in moral status".

**3. Active is impermissible, passive may be permissible (the killing/letting-die objection).** Young (§4.4) reports the view that active
euthanasia "is claimed to require an unjustifiable intentional act of killing to satisfy the patient’s request (cf., for example, Finnis,
1995; Keown in Jackson and Keown 2012; Keown 2018)"; on it "if someone’s life is intentionally terminated she has been killed, whereas if
she is just no longer being aggressively treated, her death should be attributed to the underlying disease". Rachels states the causal
version he answers: "The doctor who gives the patient with cancer a lethal injection has himself caused his patient's death; whereas if he
merely ceases treatment, the cancer is the cause of the death." (p. 5). *Against:* Rachels (p. 5): "it is not exactly correct to say that
in passive euthanasia the doctor does nothing, for he does do one thing that is very important: he lets the patient die." Young (§4.4, his
assessment): "Despite its popularity and widespread use, the distinction between passive and active euthanasia is neither particularly
clear nor morally helpful." His cases: "‘pulling the plug’ on a respirator keeping an otherwise dying patient alive, as against not
replacing the oxygen supply when it runs out"; his conclusion:
"In short, there is no good reason to think that whereas so-called passive voluntary euthanasia is morally acceptable active voluntary euthanasia never can be."

**4. Impermissible: life is inviolable (sanctity of life).** Young (§1): "some of those who hold that a person’s life is inviolable deny
that a person can ever be better off dead (e.g., Keown in Jackson and Keown 2012)". Neil Gorsuch, *The Future of Assisted Suicide and
Euthanasia* (Princeton 2006; book not read), as its publisher describes it
([press page](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691140971/the-future-of-assisted-suicide-and-euthanasia); excerpt:
`raw/gorsuch-2006-future-of-assisted-suicide-publisher-description.md`): an argument "against legalization" resting on "the idea that
human life is intrinsically valuable and that intentional killing is always wrong", which "leaves wide latitude for individual patient
autonomy and the refusal of unwanted medical treatment and life-sustaining care". Young (§3) lists "Gorsuch 2006" among opponents.
*Against:* Young (§1) points for "further reasons to reject arguments for the inviolability of the life of a person" to "McMahan 2002;
Young 2007; Sumner 2011, 2017"; and (§4.4) the premise that killing in medicine is always unjustified "has been challenged by, for
instance, Rachels 1986 and McMahan 2002".

**5. Impermissible: rational nature outranks well-being (Velleman 1999).** J. David Velleman,
["A Right of Self-Termination?"](https://doi.org/10.1086/233924), *Ethics* 109 (1999): 606–628 (not read; reported by Young §1): "A
Kant-inspired variant on this latter position has been advanced by Velleman (1999). He considers that a person’s well-being can only
matter if she is of intrinsic value and so that it is impermissible to violate a person’s rational nature (the source of her intrinsic
value) for the sake of her well-being." The exception, per Young, is "when a person’s life is so degraded as to call into question her
rational nature". *Against:* Young (§1, his assessment): "This position appears to be at odds with the well-established right of a
competent patient to refuse life-prolonging medical treatment".

**6. Legalisation leads to non-voluntary euthanasia (slippery slope).** Young (§4.5): the claim is that "we will then have set foot on a
slippery slope that will lead us eventually to support other forms of euthanasia, including, in particular, non-voluntary euthanasia."
Keown ([2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0008197321001057), CC BY; excerpt:
`raw/keown-2022-logical-link-voluntary-non-voluntary-euthanasia.md`) distinguishes two versions (p. 86). The empirical argument "runs that
drafting and enforcing safeguards against mistake and abuse is not feasible"; the logical argument "runs that if the principle of respect
for autonomy and the principle of beneﬁcence justify voluntary euthanasia, then the principle of beneﬁcence justiﬁes non-voluntary
euthanasia" (p. 84). Keown: it "takes no position on the merits of VE" (p. 85); his conclusion: "the arguments for PAS logically justify
VE, and the arguments for VE logically justify NVE" (p. 108). *Against:* Young (§4.5, his assessment): "To begin with, there is nothing
logically inconsistent in supporting voluntary euthanasia while maintaining the moral inappropriateness of non-voluntary euthanasia.
(However, for an attempt to press the charge that there is such an inconsistency see, e.g., Keown 2022.)" Keown replies to Young (p. 100):
"The LA does not assume that a patient’s best interests are comprised entirely by the patient’s best medical interests."

**Logic check (this implant, 2026-09-27; logic, not a position).** Write R = an autonomous request, B = the patient would benefit from
death, J = ending the life is justified. Young's view that each consideration is necessary and neither suffices has the form (R & B) → J;
the logical argument needs B → J. `logic.py check --premises "(R & B) -> J" --conclusion "B -> J"` outputs `INVALID` with the
counterexample row `B=T, J=F, R=F`; `logic.py check --premises "B -> J" "B" --conclusion "J"` outputs `VALID` and
`matches schema: modus ponens`. So the dispute is over which premise the case for VE rests on, which is where Keown (§II: "the ethical
case typically advanced for VE focuses on patient autonomy" but is "seriously incomplete without appeal to the principle of beneﬁcence")
and Young (§3) differ.

**The empirical record, as each side reads it.** Keown (2022, p. 95), discussing Battin, describes
"the widely reported “1,000 cases” in 1990" in the Netherlands, where patients "were intentionally given a lethal injection by their
doctors without having made an explicit request", and the Remmelink Commission's "endorsement of NVE among the 1,000 cases". Young (§4.5,
reporting the national studies Rietjens et al. 2009, Onwuteaka-Philipsen et al. 2012, van der Heide et al. 2017): "The findings from these
national studies have consistently shown that there is no evidence for the existence of such a slippery slope." Studies (abstracts read;
`raw/euthanasia-empirical-studies-netherlands-oregon-2009-2016-abstracts.md`):

- Onwuteaka-Philipsen et al., [*Lancet* 380 (2012): 908–915](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(12)61034-4) — weighted physician
  questionnaires on stratified samples of Dutch deaths in 1990, 1995, 2001, 2005, 2010: "In 2010, of all deaths in the Netherlands, 2·8%
  (95% CI 2·5-3·2; 475 of 6861) were the result of euthanasia"; "Ending of life without an explicit patient request in 2010 occurred less
  often (0·2%; 95% CI 0·1-0·3; 13 of 6861) than in 2005, 2001, 1995, and 1990 (0·8%; 0·6-1·1; 45 of 5197)"; 77% of 2010 cases "were
  reported to a review committee".
- Emanuel et al., [*JAMA* 316 (2016): 79–90](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.8499) — review of US, Canadian and European data "for the
  period 1947 to 2016": "Between 0.3% to 4.6% of all deaths are reported as euthanasia or physician-assisted suicide in jurisdictions
  where they are legal. The frequency of these deaths increased after legalization." "In no jurisdiction was there evidence that
  vulnerable patients have been receiving euthanasia or physician-assisted suicide at rates higher than those in the general population."
- Ganzini, Goy, Dobscha, [*Arch. Intern. Med.* 169 (2009): 489–492](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2008.579) — 56 Oregonians who
  requested physician aid in dying or contacted an advocacy group: the top-rated reasons were "wanting to control the circumstances of
  death and die at home; loss of independence; and concerns about future pain, poor quality of life, and inability to care for one's
  self".

## Arguments in play

- **Double effect in pain relief and palliative sedation.** Young (§4.3): on the doctrine it is permissible "to, for example, alleviate
  pain (a good effect) by administering a drug, knowing that doing so will shorten life, but impermissible to administer the same drug
  with the direct intention of terminating a patient’s life (a bad effect)." Young's criticism: it "can be relevant to the permissibility
  of voluntary euthanasia only when a person’s death is bad for her". McIntyre
  ([SEP "Doctrine of Double Effect"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/double-effect/), rev. 2023-07-17, §5.1, her
  assessment) reports that "Physicians and researchers have insisted repeatedly that it is a myth that opioids administered for pain
  relief can be expected to hasten death (Sykes and Thorns, 2003 provide a review of a large number of studies supporting this claim)." On
  terminal sedation (§5.2): Rehnquist CJ in *Vacco v. Quill* (1997) "invoked double effect [...] as a justification for the practice known
  as terminal sedation or continuous sedation"; Quill, Dresser and Brock (1997) "argue that double effect, if applied consistently, would
  rule out the practice of terminal sedation", since it "inevitably causes death". The doctrine in general:
  [the trolley problem](trolley-problem.md).
- **Palliative care makes it unnecessary** (Emanuel 1999; Keown 2012, per Young §4.1); Young replies it is not "a panacea" and "not
  everyone wishes to avail themselves of palliative or hospice care".
- **Requests cannot be known to be competent** — "we can never have sufficient evidence to be justified in believing that a dying person’s
  request to be helped to die is competent, enduring and genuinely voluntary" (Young §4.2, stating it); the answer he reports: "a cooling
  off period should normally be required".

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Ancient Greeks and Romans** "did not believe that life needed to be preserved at any cost"; **More**, *Utopia* (1516), a community
  that "would facilitate the death of those whose lives had become burdensome as a result of ‘torturing and lingering pain’" (Young §1).
- **Glanville Williams** (1957), for legalisation; **Yale Kamisar** (1958), against, "One of the ﬁrst scholars to identify a logical link"
  (Keown 2022, p. 87).
- **AMA** (1973), **Rachels** (1975) — position 2; **[Foot](../thinkers/foot.md)** (1977) — cited by Young (§§1, 3); **Finnis** (1995), **Keown** (2002–2022) —
  3, 4, 6; **Velleman** (1999) — 5; **Gorsuch** (2006, 2007) — 4, 6.
- **Callahan**, **Kass**, **D. Jones**, **Gorsuch** (2007 reply to Tallis) — among the "several scholars of bioethics and law" who "have
  also endorsed the LA" (Keown 2022, p. 87).
- **Dworkin and Frey**, **Battin**, **Lillehammer**, **S. Smith**, **Young**, **Jackson**, **Daskal** — have "either rejected or at least
  not accepted" it (Keown 2022, abstract).
- **Onwuteaka-Philipsen, van der Heide et al.** (Dutch studies 1990–2017); **Emanuel et al.** (2016); **Ganzini et al.** (2009).

## Framings and reframings

- **Morality or legal control.** Young's preamble treats the five objections as "made by those who deny that voluntary euthanasia is
  morally permissible and who are, in consequence, opposed to its being legalized"; Keown (2022, p. 86) says the moral question is "not,
  however, the central question in the contemporary legal and political debate. That question is the feasibility of legal control".
- **Transparency.** Young (§4.5): "some commentators have pointed out that there may, in reality, be more danger of the line between
  voluntary and non-voluntary euthanasia being blurred if euthanasia is practised in the absence of legal recognition";
  Onwuteaka-Philipsen et al. (2012) conclude "the euthanasia law resulted in a relatively transparent practice".
- **Related problems.** [The trolley problem](trolley-problem.md) (doing/allowing, double effect); [abortion](abortion.md);
  [moral dilemmas](moral-dilemmas.md).

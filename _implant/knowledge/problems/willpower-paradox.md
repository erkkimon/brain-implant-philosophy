## The question

Wikipedia's list entry: "Willpower paradox: Those who keep their minds open are more goal-directed and more motivated than those who declare their objectives to themselves." (rev. 1376699902, "Decision theory").
Wikipedia's article: "The willpower paradox is the idea that people may do things better by focusing less directly on doing them, implying that the direct exertion of volition may not always be the most powerful way to accomplish a goal." (rev. 1369084223, lead).
The study behind it asks: "How does the way in which you talk to yourself shape your future actions?" (Senay et al. 2010, introduction).
Herbert, who gave the findings the name, puts the contrast as: "It is the difference between “Will I do this?” and “I will do this.”" (2010).

Structural note (this implant): the sources read use the name for two different questions.
In Herbert 2010 and Wikipedia it names an empirical contrast between interrogative and declarative
self-talk. In Goschke & Job 2023 it names a conceptual question: "How is it possible that a person most strongly desires to perform a behavior (e.g., eat chocolate) and at the same time desires to recruit self-control to prevent themselves from doing it?" (abstract).
Goschke & Job do not cite Senay et al. (string "Senay" not found in their text).

## Why it matters

- **Self-affirmation in practice.** Albarracín, in the university's release: "The popular idea is that self-affirmations enhance people's ability to meet their goals," and "It seems, however, that when it comes to performing a specific behavior, asking questions is a more promising way of achieving your objectives." (ScienceDaily 2010).
- **Therapy and counselling.** Senay et al. ground their hypothesis in practice: "Several independent lines of research and practice suggest that engaging in interrogative as opposed to declarative talk (e.g., Will I vs. I will ) may lead to increased intrinsic motivation." (introduction), citing motivational interviewing.
- **Addiction recovery.** Herbert opens: "WILLINGNESS is a core concept of addiction recovery programs—and a paradoxical one." and "Twelve-step programs emphasize that addicts cannot will themselves into healthy sobriety—indeed, that ego and self-reliance are often a root cause of their problem." Wikipedia's article reports Michael J. Taleff (*Counselor Magazine*, 2011) as writing "Willpower in our field (psychology) is a paradox" (rev. 1369084223); Taleff's text was not read (the archived copy was not retrievable on 2026-10-09).
- **Theories of self-control (the second use).** Goschke & Job: "Although this paradox has occasionally been discussed in philosophical action theories ( Barnes, 2019 ; Bermúdez, 2018b ; Mele, 2013 ; Sripada, 2014 ), to the best of our knowledge it has been almost completely neglected in psychology and cognitive neuroscience." (introduction).

## Positions taken

No grouping of positions on the self-talk use was found in the sources read; findings and responses are listed by owner, unranked.

**The self-talk findings (Senay, Albarracín & Noguchi 2010) — the researchers' findings.**
- *Experiment 1* ("Fifty-three introductory psychology students enrolled in the study in exchange for course credit."): "The participants solved significantly more anagrams when they were told to think about whether they would do anagrams ( M = 2.60, SD = 1.72) than when they were told to think that they would do anagrams ( M = 1.84, SD = 1.42), t (49) = 2.11, p = .04, g = 0.6." The authors add: "Nonetheless, these results were obtained with explicit instructions."
- *Experiment 2* (50 students; Will I, I will, I or Will written in an ostensible handwriting task): "the Will I prime produced better performance than any other prime, t (46) = 3.1, p = .003, g = 1.03." and "In contrast, there were no significant differences across the I will, I , and Will primes, t (49)s < 1, n.s."
- *Experiment 3* (46 students; outcome: intended hours of exercise): "participants previously primed with patterned sequences had stronger intentions to exercise in the Will I as compared with the I will condition, t (42) = 7.45, p < .001, g = 3.05." and "However, this effect disappeared when participants had previously written random sequences, t (42) = 1.06, n.s."
- *Experiment 4* (56 students; intrinsic and extrinsic reasons for exercising): "These results, along with a significant Sobel test ( z = 2.03, p = .043, R 2 = .06), confirmed the role of intrinsic motivation as a mediator of the effect of the prime on exercise intentions."
- *The authors' summary and limitation:* "Our findings identified interrogative self-talk as an important motivator of goal-directed behavior." and "our studies did not specifically show that the performance of a goal-directed behavior was determined by intentions to perform this behavior, which could have been partially under the control of processes beside intention." (General Discussion).

**Replication attempt (Puchalska-Wasyl 2014, abstract only).** "Two of the experiments reported here were meant to replicate that study, but the expected differences were not confirmed." Her third experiment: "Experiment 3 showed that if a self‐posed question about future behavior was answered positively, task performance was better than in groups exposed either to the self‐statement ‘I will do it’ or to a negative answer following the question. However, these differences occurred only in those who self‐reported the awareness of the impact of self‐talk on their thought processes." (*Applied Cognitive Psychology* 28(3), 2014, [doi:10.1002/acp.3007](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3007); closed access, full text not read).

**Reported limitations (Alderson-Day & Fernyhough 2015, review).** They summarise the 2010 results — "Imagining questions in inner speech prior to starting the task (e.g., statements such as “Will I . . .?”) were associated with better anagram performance and intention to exercise compared with imagining declarative statements (e.g., “I will . . .”), with the latter being mediated by changes in the intrinsic motivation to exercise." — and add: "Such protocols have their limitations: they do not include a control for checking that participants were actually engaging in the kinds of self-talk they were instructed to use, nor whether participants also deployed self-talk during the subsequent performance tasks (i.e., anagrams)." (*Psychological Bulletin* 141(5), [doi:10.1037/bul0000021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000021)).

**Reported as consistent (Lohmann, Jones & Albarracín 2019).** In a door-choice paradigm: "Introducing questions thus sped up responses regardless of what question was asked ( Table 1 ), which is consistent with the findings of Senay and colleagues (2010)." (*Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 85, [doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2019.103840](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2019.103840)). Albarracín is an author of both papers.

**An audit of the journal (Francis 2014, record only).** Francis's test for excess success reports that "In total, problems appeared for 82 % (36 out of 44) of the articles in Psychological Science that had four or more experiments and could be analyzed." (abstract; *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 21(5), [doi:10.3758/s13423-014-0601-x](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0601-x)). Europe PMC lists Senay et al. 2010 among its references; whether that paper is among the 36 was not established here (full text closed).

**The conceptual use (Goschke & Job 2023): resolutions they classify.** "To avoid the paradox, any coherent theory of self-control must abandon either the assumption (a) that recruitment of self-control is an intentional process, or (b) that humans are unitary agents, or (c) that self-control consists in overriding the currently strongest desire." (abstract).
- *Nonintentional (automatic) self-control:* "A first possible resolution of the willpower paradox is to abandon the assumption that the recruitment of self-control is an intentional process."
- *Divided minds and dual systems:* "The second resolution abandons the unitary-agent assumption and postulates distinct systems mediating either impulsive versus reflective valuations or goal-directed versus habitual behavior."
- *Nonsynchronous (preventive) self-control:* "Both forms of preventive self-control avoid the willpower paradox because they are initiated before a desire has become the strongest motivational tendency."
- *A possible fourth:* a "fourth resolution of the willpower paradox that rests on the assumption that self-control can—under certain circumstances—have an intrinsic value that is independent from the utility of the long-term goal it is supposed to support." (introduction, outline).

## Arguments in play

(none recorded as separate argument pages yet). Goschke & Job's three assumptions:
"Intentionality assumption. The recruitment of self-control strategies is an intentional, self-initiated and deliberate process."
"Unitary-agent assumption . Persons are unitary agents in the sense that when intentionally choosing actions or cognitive strategies, their choices are determined by which option has the highest subjective expected utility of all options considered at the moment of choice."
"Synchronicity assumption . Self-control serves to override one’s currently strongest (most preferred) desire, that would otherwise determine behavioral choice, in favor of a currently weaker (less preferred) superordinate or long-term goal."
Their dilemma: "Either the person assigns the highest expected utility to a current desire, in which case there is no motivational basis for the intention to override this desire, or the person assigns the highest expected utility to a long-term goal, in which case self-control is superfluous."
They add: "Note that we do not claim that it is paradoxical to have multiple conflicting goals."

**Propositional check (this implant, 2026-10-09; logic, not a position).** A simplified rendering:
*S* = intentional synchronic self-control occurs; *G* = the long-term goal has the highest
expected utility at that moment; *D* = a current desire is the strongest. Premises:
`S -> G` (an intentional recruitment needs a motive, under the intentionality and unitary-agent assumptions),
`S -> D` (the synchronicity assumption), `D -> ~G` (only one option is strongest).
`logic.py check --premises "S -> G" "S -> D" "D -> ~G" --conclusion "~S"` outputs `VALID`.
Dropping any one premise gives `INVALID`, with counterexample rows `D=T, G=F, S=T` (without `S -> G`),
`D=F, G=T, S=T` (without `S -> D`) and `D=T, G=T, S=T` (without `D -> ~G`) — the propositional
counterpart of the three resolutions. The mapping of assumptions to premises is this implant's rendering, not Goschke & Job's notation.

The self-talk use has no deductive core in the sources read; its claims are the experimental findings above.

## Framings and reframings

- **Willfulness against willingness.** Herbert reads the experiments through addiction recovery: "Yet recovering addicts must be willing. That is, they must be open to the possibility that the group and its principles are powerful enough to trump a compulsive disease." and "Put in terms of addiction recovery and self-improvement in general, those who were asserting their willpower were in effect closing their minds and narrowing their view of their future."
- **Questions and autonomy.** Herbert reports Senay's explanation: "Perhaps, Senay hypothesized, it is because questions by their nature speak to possibility and freedom of choice." The paper's abstract: "This effect was found to be mediated by the intrinsic motivation for action and moderated by the salience of the word order of the primes."
- **Indirection.** Wikipedia's article states the idea generally (lead, quoted in The question) and lists [the paradox of hedonism](paradox-of-hedonism.md) and *wu wei* under "See also" (rev. 1369084223); the sources read do not discuss either link.
- **A label for the findings, not the authors' term.** The phrase "willpower paradox" does not occur in Senay et al.'s text (string search of the PMC manuscript); in the sources read it originates with Herbert's title.
- **Dynamic choice.** Against a static reading of their paradox, Goschke & Job: "However, although such a dynamic perspective is a more realistic conception of behavioral choice, it does not eliminate the willpower paradox."

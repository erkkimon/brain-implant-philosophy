## The question

Wikipedia's list: "Green paradox: Policies intending to reduce future CO2 emissions may lead to increased emissions in the present." (List of paradoxes, rev. 1376699902).
Sinn's statement in the 2008 abstract: "If suppliers do not react, demand reductions by a subset of countries are ineffective. They simply depress the world price of carbon and induce the environmental sinners to consume what the Kyoto countries have economized on. Even worse, if suppliers feel threatened by a gradual greening of economic policies in the Kyoto countries that would damage their future prices; they will extract their stocks more rapidly, thus accelerating global warming." (p. 360).
In 2015: "In particular, policies aimed at reducing future demand for fossil fuels could backfire by inducing resource owners to bring forward their extraction plans, thus accelerating global warming. I have called this behaviour the Green Paradox." (REEP, p. 239).

The question, in the sources' terms: when a demand-reducing climate policy
(a carbon tax, a subsidy to substitutes) is expected to grow stronger over
time, do owners of fossil-fuel stocks respond by extracting more now, and
if so, is the net effect on warming — in the short run and over the whole
horizon — harmful?

**The Hotelling reasoning, as Sinn states it.** The owner can extract now
and invest the net proceeds at interest, or leave the resource in the
ground and gain its price increase; he "is indifferent between extracting
now or a period later if" i = Ṗ / (P − g(S)), Sinn's equation (1), with
*i* the interest rate, *P* the price, Ṗ its change over time and *g(S)* the
unit extraction cost (2008, p. 367). "Equation (1) is a necessary condition for both an optimal extraction plan of the resource owner and a market equilibrium. In the special case where g = 0, this equation reduces to Hotelling’s (1931) condition that the percentage rate of price increase equal the rate of interest." (p. 367; Hotelling, *Journal of Political Economy* 39, 1931, pp. 137–175, [doi:10.1086/254195](https://doi.org/10.1086/254195), DOI-verified, not read here).
In 2015: "In the spirit of Hotelling (1931), the new models typically assume that resource owners optimize the composition of their wealth portfolios, with wealth consisting of both the physical resource in the ground and the financial wealth that can result from extracting and selling some of the resource." and "Under the Hotelling rule, it is essential that resource owners be forward-looking (i.e., that they base their behaviour on expectations of future prices)." (p. 240).
Because the stock is fixed: "However, if firms react to a change in demand today by extracting less, they must extract more tomorrow, and vice versa" (2008, p. 377).

**Arithmetic note (this implant, 2026-10-08; mathematics, not a position).**
Setting *g* = 0 in Sinn's equation (1) gives *i* = Ṗ/*P*: the price's
proportional growth rate equals the interest rate — the special case Sinn
names after Hotelling. With *g* > 0 the same equation gives
Ṗ = *i*·(*P* − *g*), so Ṗ/*P* = *i*·(1 − *g*/*P*) < *i* whenever 0 < *g* < *P*;
this matches Sinn's remark "With extraction costs, i.e., g(S) > 0, P grows at a lower rate and thus tax neutrality is compatible with a rising ad valorem tax rate." (p. 380).

## Why it matters

- **Which climate instruments work.** Sinn: "Whether demand-reducing measures reach this goal is unclear because they exert two countervailing effects on the current extraction volume. On the one hand, they reduce the incentive to extract today because they depress today’s prices. On the other, they increase the incentive to extract today because the anticipated demand and price decline that these policies generate in the future reduces the opportunity cost of the resource in situ. Unless it is demonstrated that the latter effect is dominated by the former, the policies cannot reasonably be proposed as a means to mitigate the greenhouse effect." (2008, p. 377).
- **Beyond taxes.** "Unfortunately, the green paradox not only applies to an increasing tax rate, but may also be relevant for the bulk of the green demand reducing policies discussed in Sect. 2." (p. 381). Gerlagh reports the argument for low-carbon technology: "In this climate change debate, economists have pointed to a green paradox: when policy makers stimulate the development of low carbon energy sources to (partly) replace fossil fuels in the future, oil markets may anticipate a future reduction in demand and increase current supply." (2011, abstract).
- **Unilateral policy.** Long defines the outcome for open economies: "This article examines how, in a world with incomplete coordination among countries, well-intentioned unilateral environmental policies may actually harm the global environment. This outcome is known as the “Green Paradox.”" (REEP 2015, abstract; [doi:10.1093/reep/rev004](https://doi.org/10.1093/reep/rev004)).
- **The supply side of climate policy.** Jensen, Mohlin, Pittel & Sterner, after their review: "We argue that in designing climate policy, policy makers need to consider the supply side of the fossil fuel market." (REEP 2015, abstract; [doi:10.1093/reep/rev010](https://doi.org/10.1093/reep/rev010)).

## Positions taken

No published classification of positions was found in the sources read;
the answers on record are listed by owner, unranked. All are claims about
economic models or data, reported as their authors'.

- **Sinn (2008): a rising tax or a greening demand policy can accelerate warming.** On an ad valorem tax: "If the tax rate rises faster than the cost share times, the discount rate, the rate of return from keeping the resource in situ exceeds the rate of return from extracting the resource and investing the proceeds in the capital market." "This possibility is called the green paradox, because it shows that good intentions do not always breed good deeds." "The green paradox implies that such a policy is likely to backfire and create even more harm for the environment by speeding up global warming." (p. 380). In the conclusion: "If the measures reduce the discounted value of the carbon price in the future more than in the present, the problem of global warming will even be exacerbated because resource owners will have an incentive to anticipate the price cuts by extracting the carbon earlier. There is a green paradox." (p. 388).
  *Neutral cases in the same paper:* "As is well known, a cash flow tax does not affect the extraction path" (p. 377), and "If there are no, or only negligible, extraction costs, the ad valorem tax is as neutral as a cash flow tax, because it then is such a tax." (p. 378).
- **Sinn (2015): the price-wedge trichotomy.** Producers will "(1) Bring their extraction forward if the present value of the expected price wedge (discounted using the rate of return on financial assets) increases over time; (2) not change the extraction path if the present value of the expected price wedge remains constant over time; and (3) postpone extraction if the present value of the expected price wedge decreases over time." (p. 241). Of the symposium reviews, Sinn's reading is: "Overall, they find that the literature confirms the risk of Green Paradox outcomes and strengthens the case for taking a supply-side view of the climate problem." (p. 241) — Sinn's characterisation; compare Jensen et al.'s own summary below.
- **Gerlagh (2011): extraction costs and imperfect substitutes work against it.** "We find that increasing fossil fuel extraction costs counteracts the strong green paradox, while imperfect energy substitutes may make both the weak and strong green paradox vanish." (abstract; [doi:10.1093/cesifo/ifq004](https://doi.org/10.1093/cesifo/ifq004)).
- **van der Ploeg & Withagen (2012): the effect on warming of a cheaper backstop is unclear.** "In the absence of a CO2 tax, the anticipation of a cheaper renewable backstop increases current emissions of CO2. Since the date at which renewables are phased in is brought forward and more generally future emissions of CO2 will decrease, the effect on global warming is unclear." and "Green welfare falls if the backstop is relatively expensive and full exhaustion of fossil fuels is optimal, but may increase if the backstop is sufficiently cheap relative to the cost of extracting the last drop of fossil fuels plus marginal global warming damages as then it is attractive to leave more fossil fuels unexploited and thus limit CO2 emissions." (*Journal of Environmental Economics and Management* 64(3), pp. 342–363, abstract; [doi:10.1016/j.jeem.2012.08.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeem.2012.08.002)). The same abstract also states a case where the subsidy lowers green welfare: "However, if a market economy does not internalize global warming externalities and renewables have not kicked in yet, full exhaustion of fossil fuel will occur in finite time and a backstop subsidy always curbs green welfare."
- **van der Ploeg & Withagen (2015): offsetting by fuel left in the ground.** "We argue that such policies may also encourage more fossil fuel to be locked in the crust of the earth, which can offset the adverse effects of the weak Green Paradox." and "Neither the weak nor the strong Green Paradox occurs for the first-best Pigouvian carbon tax." (REEP 9(2), pp. 285–303, abstract; [doi:10.1093/reep/rev008](https://doi.org/10.1093/reep/rev008)).
- **Jensen, Mohlin, Pittel & Sterner (2015): mostly weakened, not dismissed.** "We consider several factors that affect the existence of the green paradox, including long-term extraction costs, short-term extraction capacities, the mix of policy instruments, and potential spatial carbon leakage to countries that have no climate policy. We find that these and other factors can sometimes strengthen, but mostly weaken, the case for concern about the green paradox." (REEP 9(2), pp. 246–265, abstract).
- **Long (2015): border tax adjustments.** "I show that border tax adjustments can lead to Green Paradox outcomes." (abstract).
- **Di Maria, Lange & van der Werf (2014): an empirical test.** They describe their paper as "the first empirical test of the green paradox hypothesis, according to which well-intended but imperfectly implemented environmental policies may lead to detrimental outcomes due to supply side responses." Their finding, on the U.S. Acid Rain Program: "Using data on prices, heat input and sulfur content of coal delivered to U.S. power plants, we find strong evidence of a price decrease and of an increase in the sulfur premium, some indication that the amount of coal used might have increased, and no evidence of fuel-switching towards higher-sulfur coal. Overall, our evidence suggests that while the mechanism indicated by the theory might be at work, market conditions and concurrent regulation largely prevented a green paradox from arising." (*European Economic Review* 69, pp. 143–162, abstract; [doi:10.1016/j.euroecorev.2013.03.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2013.03.010)).
  *On the state of the evidence, per Sinn (2015):* "The articles primarily present the theoretical literature because there has been very little empirical research on the Green Paradox to date." (p. 241).
- **Sinn's replies (2015).** To the backstop argument, which he attributes to van der Ploeg & Withagen and Jensen et al. — "They also note that subsidies that reduce the unit cost of the backstop would accelerate global warming for a while (weak Green Paradox) but would reduce the total amount of carbon released into the atmosphere." — he answers: "First, it is not clear to me why reducing the total long-term carbon budget should be considered more beneficial to human welfare than postponing extraction." and "Second, I do not see anything in the real world that even comes close to being the perfect backstop assumed in theoretical models." (p. 242). He grants: "Of course, there are model assumptions that would eliminate the risk of a Green Paradox outcome." (p. 243), and on a policy designed so that the wedge's present value declines: "However, I doubt that it will ever be politically possible to fine-tune such a policy and to commit to its implementation over the long term." (p. 243).

**Policy proposals on record.** Sinn 2008: "Among the public finance measures, time-invariant unit taxes on carbon extraction and source taxes on capital income are feasible policy options that satisfy this requirement." (p. 388). Sinn 2015: "I would argue that nothing short of binding global agreements on quantity constraints can successfully reduce the speed of global warming." (p. 244). van der Ploeg & Withagen 2015 on the first-best Pigouvian tax: quoted above.

## Arguments in play

(none recorded as separate argument pages yet). The argument's chain, as
Sinn's 2008 and 2015 texts set it out: a policy expected to tighten (*I*)
lowers expected future producer prices relative to present ones (*F*);
forward-looking owners then extract more now (*W*); hence warming
accelerates in the short run. The step to *worse over the whole horizon* (*S*) is a
separate premise — it is exactly where Gerlagh and van der Ploeg &
Withagen distinguish a weak from a strong version (see Framings).

**Propositional check (this implant, 2026-10-08; logic, not a position).**
`logic.py check --premises "I -> F" "F -> W" --conclusion "I -> W"` outputs
`VALID` and `matches schema: hypothetical syllogism`. With the conclusion
`"I -> S"` and the same two premises it outputs `INVALID` with the
counterexample row `F=T, I=T, S=F, W=T`; adding the premise `"W -> S"`
makes it `VALID`. The tool checks form only: whether each conditional
holds is the economic question the authors above dispute.

## Framings and reframings

- **Weak and strong.** Gerlagh: "We reproduce the green paradox, and to facilitate discussion, differentiate between a weak and a strong version, related to short-term and long-term effects, respectively." (2011, abstract). Sinn's use: "It is a weak paradox because global warming accelerates at least for a while. It is a strong paradox because it moves the economy further away from an intertemporal Pareto optimum, which requires a slower speed of global warming than would occur in competitive markets." (2015, p. 241). van der Ploeg & Withagen's: "A weak Green Paradox arises if the announcement of a future carbon tax or a sufficiently fast rising carbon tax encourages fossil fuel owners to extract reserves more aggressively, thus exacerbating global warming." and "Green welfare (the converse of environmental damages) declines (i.e., the strong Green Paradox) if the beneficial climate effects of locking up more fossil fuel do not outweigh the short-run weak Green Paradox effects." (2015, abstract). The three texts define "strong" differently: by Pareto optimum (Sinn), by long-term effects (Gerlagh), by green welfare (van der Ploeg & Withagen) — a structural note, this implant, from the quotations above.
- **An announced expropriation.** Wikipedia's article: "That is the green paradox: environmental policy slated to become greener over time acts as an announced expropriation that provokes owners to react by accelerating the rate of extraction of their fossil fuel stocks, thus accelerating climate change." (rev. 1368899299, citing Long 1975 and Konrad, Olson & Schöb 1994). Its lead calls Sinn's book "controversial" (the article's word, without a cited source for it).
- **A condition, per Wikipedia.** "Sinn emphasises that a condition for the green paradox is that the resource be scarce in the sense that its price will always be higher than the unit extraction and exploration costs combined." (rev. 1368899299, uncited sentence). Sinn 2015 grants that assumptions can remove the outcome (quoted under Positions).
- **Paradox in which sense?** Sinn gives the name's reason: it "shows that good intentions do not always breed good deeds" (2008, p. 380). Wikipedia files it under "Decision theory" in its list and in the category "Paradoxes in economics". Whether it meets this implant's normative sense of [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md) — acceptable premises, acceptable reasoning, unacceptable conclusion — or the older sense of a view contrary to common opinion that the vocabulary page distinguishes, is not discussed in the sources read.
- **Related, per Wikipedia.** The article's "See also" lists the Jevons paradox (no page here yet).

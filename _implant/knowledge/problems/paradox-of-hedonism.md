## The question

Sidgwick names it: "Here comes into view what we may call the fundamental paradox of Hedonism, that the impulse towards pleasure, if too predominant, defeats its own aim." (I.iv.2).
His illustration: "A man who maintains throughout an epicurean mood, keeping his main conscious aim perpetually fixed on his own pleasure, does not catch the full spirit of the chase; his eagerness never gets just the sharpness of edge which imparts to the pleasure its highest zest." (I.iv.2).
He limits its scope: "This effect is not visible, or at any rate is scarcely visible, in the case of passive sensual pleasures." (I.iv.2); of art, "in order to get them, one must forget them" (I.iv.2).
Moore (SEP §1) defines it as "the plausible claim that some of our hedonically motivated or determined action actually secures less pleasure than we would otherwise have got (e.g., Sidgwick: 48f)".
Crisp (SEP "Well-Being" §4.1): "The hedonist will point out the so-called ‘paradox of hedonism’, that pleasure is most effectively pursued indirectly."
Dietz's first-pass formulation: "The paradox of hedonism: If one intrinsically desires nothing other than one’s own pleasure, then one will be prevented from obtaining pleasure." (2021, p. 388).

**In what sense a paradox.** Dietz: "Note that the phenomenon is meant to be “paradoxical” not in the strong sense of presenting us with an apparently sound argument leading to an absurd conclusion, but rather in the milder sense of being a surprising observation." (p. 388, n. 3). Sidgwick: "though it presents itself as a paradox, there does not seem to be any difficulty in its practical realisation, when once the danger indicated is clearly seen." (II.iii.2). Butler uses the word in 1726: "how much soever a paradox it may appear" (Sermon XI). Martínez & Labiano, by contrast, call it "arguably one of the most powerful anti-hedonist arguments" (2022, abstract; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5817/glb2022-2-5); excerpt: `raw/crossref-paradox-of-hedonism-analysis-2005-2006-glb-2022.md`).

**Where it comes from.** Dietz: "the classic statement of the paradox, by Joseph Butler, dates back to 1726." (p. 388). Butler: "So that if self-love wholly engrosses us, and leaves no room for any other principle, there can be absolutely no such thing at all as happiness or enjoyment of any kind whatever; since happiness consists in the gratification of particular passions, which supposes the having of them." (Sermon XI). Sidgwick's naming is in the 7th edition read here (1907); which earlier edition first carries the phrase was not checked.

## Why it matters

- **Psychological hedonism.** Moore (SEP §1) places the paradox beside weakness of will, as "related" to the modest thesis "that only pleasure or displeasure motivates us" (excerpt context). Sidgwick states it in I.iv ("Pleasure and Desire"), where the question at issue is "whether there are no desires and aversions which have not pleasures and pains for their objects--no conscious impulses to produce or avert results other than the agent’s own feelings." (I.iv.2).
- **Hedonism about well-being and the experience machine.** Crisp (SEP §4.1) uses it in the hedonist's reply to the [experience machine](experience-machine.md): "If I consciously try to maximize my own pleasure, I will be unable to immerse myself in those activities, such as reading or playing games, which do give pleasure." and if we believe activities "are valuable independently of the pleasure we gain from engaging in them, then we shall probably gain more pleasure overall." In *Hedonism Reconsidered*: "One version of the paradox of hedonism is that one will gain more enjoyment by trying to do something other than to enjoy oneself." (Crisp 2006, p. 637; excerpt: `raw/crisp-2006-hedonism-reconsidered-experience-machine.md`).
- **Egoism as a method of ethics.** Sidgwick's second treatment is in Book II, "Egoistic Hedonism", answering an objection that "the systematic pursuit of pleasure is in some danger of defeating itself" (II.iii.2, excerpt context).
- **Consequentialism.** Dietz: "It has been observed that there is a parallel “paradox of consequentialism,” that is, that thinking in consequentialist terms can prevent agents from producing the best consequences." (p. 394, n. 16; he lists Williams 1973, Stocker 1976, Railton 1984 among others).
- **Ancient hedonism.** Martínez & Labiano argue "that some of the main elements in Epicurus' ethical position enable him to avoid the negative conclusion of this paradox", while the Cyrenaics' claims "make their ethical position especially vulnerable to this paradox" (2022, abstract; full text not read).
- **Its place in the literature.** Dietz: "In fact, in recent general discussions of hedonism about well-being, the paradox of hedonism is often not even mentioned." (p. 388), naming Feldman (2004) and Gregory (2016) in n. 4.

## Positions taken

No source read here groups the positions into families; the list follows
the chronology of the sources. None is ranked here.

- **Self-love defeats itself when it engrosses the mind (Butler 1726).** "Disengagement is absolutely necessary to enjoyment; and a person may have so steady and fixed an eye upon his own interest, whatever he places it in, as may hinder him from attending to many gratifications within his reach, which others have their minds free and open to." His conclusion: "the contracted affection may be so prevalent as to disappoint itself, and even contradict its own and private good." (Sermon XI). Garrett's report: "Or we might obsess over our interest to such a degree that we would continually be exercising the principle of self-love but not actually satisfying our particular passions and consequently not be happy." (SEP §5). Dietz reads Butler's ground as "an early version of a desire-based theory of pleasure": "Butler’s rough idea is that pleasure has to do with satisfying our desires, and so in order to obtain pleasure, we had better have independent desires to satisfy." (pp. 390–391).
  *Against (Butler's ground):* Sidgwick, after stating Butler's view — "We could not pursue pleasure at all, unless we had desires for something else than pleasure; for pleasure consists in the satisfaction of just these “disinterested” impulses." — replies "Butler has certainly over-stated his case," citing pleasures "of sight, hearing and smell" that come without prior desire (I.iv.2).
- **Practically self-limiting, not self-defeating (Sidgwick 1874/1907).** "I should not, however, infer from this that the pursuit of pleasure is necessarily self-defeating and futile; but merely that the principle of Egoistic Hedonism, when applied with a due knowledge of the laws of human nature, is practically self-limiting;" — that is, "a rational method of attaining the end at which it aims requires that we should to some extent put it out of sight and not directly aim at it." (II.iii.2). On whether one can adopt other aims at will: "For the reaction of our activities upon our emotional nature is such that we may commonly bring ourselves to take an interest in any end by concentrating our efforts upon its attainment." (II.iii.2).
- **Happiness reached by not aiming at it (Mill 1873).** "I never, indeed, wavered in the conviction that happiness is the test of all rules of conduct, and the end of life. But I now thought that this end was only to be attained by not making it the direct end." "Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so. The only chance is to treat, not happiness, but some end external to it, as the purpose of life." (ch. V). He limits it to "those who have but a moderate degree of sensibility and of capacity I for enjoyment" (ch. V, as printed). Dietz: "In his autobiography, John Stuart Mill reports having experienced the paradox firsthand in the form of a depression." (p. 389).
- **Indirect pursuit as the hedonist's reply (Crisp, SEP "Well-Being" §4.1; Crisp 2006).** The paradox is offered on the hedonist's side: on Crisp's account, the hedonist insists that the experience machine objection "rests on ‘common sense’ intuitions, the place in our lives of which may itself be justified by hedonism" (SEP §4.1), "a strategy similar to that developed by ‘two-level utilitarians’" (§4.1).
- **An objection to hedonism about well-being (Dietz 2021).** Dietz's argument, his own, targets "Axiological hedonism: What is intrinsically good for subjects, and (therefore) what is intrinsically desirable simpliciter, is (just) pleasure." (p. 393). Its steps are below under Arguments in play. Martínez & Labiano likewise describe the paradox as an argument that "pleasure cannot represent the only thing that directly makes us better off" (2022, abstract).
  *Responses Dietz reports* (p. 394, n. 16), by parallel with the consequentialism debate: deny the acceptability requirement ("even if it would be a bad idea for agents to believe in or try to follow a consequentialist theory, this does not mean that the theory is incorrect", with Hare 1981, Railton 1984, Parfit 1984, Scheffler 1994); or "deny Rational Desire, and thus to argue that we could accept hedonism without falling into the paradox of hedonism" (after Railton's "sophisticated consequentialism"). Dietz's own way out for the hedonist is the backgrounding move: pleasure, desire satisfaction or a hybrid "are not themselves intrinsically good for us, but rather that they operate in the background, making other states intrinsically good for us." (pp. 408–409).
- **Explained by contingent facts, or by the nature of pleasure.** Dietz reports the common assumption: "It is commonly assumed that this phenomenon, assuming that it is real, would have to be explained by contingent empirical facts, such as the difficulty of predicting what will make us happy." (p. 390). He defends the Butler-style explanation in Dietz ([2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/00048402.2018.1483409)), not read here.
- **Feldman 2006 and Timmermann 2005.** Timmermann (2005, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/65.2.144)) proposed "Another paradox of hedonism"; Feldman's reply is titled "Timmermann's new paradox of hedonism: neither new nor paradoxical" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/66.1.76)). Only the titles were verified (Crossref); neither text was read, so their content is not reported.

**Distribution, as reported.** No survey figure is recorded. Moore (SEP §1) calls the claim "plausible"; Broad, as Dietz quotes him, finds Butler's observations "“profoundly true and important”" (Broad 1930: 74, via Dietz p. 389).

## Arguments in play

(none recorded as separate argument pages yet).

**Dietz's argument (2021, p. 394), as he states it.** "P1. Rational Desire: If you are rational, then if you believe that X is not (intrinsically) desirable, you will not (intrinsically) desire X." P2: if axiological hedonism is true, pleasure is the only intrinsically desirable thing, so its believer believes nothing else is intrinsically desirable (excerpt context). "P3. The paradox of hedonism: If we are rational, and we intrinsically desire nothing other than pleasure, then we will be significantly prevented from obtaining pleasure." "P4. The acceptability requirement: Other things equal, we should reject an axiological theory if rational agents who believe in its claims about which objects are intrinsically desirable will be significantly prevented from obtaining those objects." "C3. Therefore, other things equal, we should reject axiological hedonism." Dietz on its form: "Since the argument is clearly valid, it shows that we face an inconsistent set: Rational Desire, the acceptability requirement, the paradox of hedonism, and axiological hedonism" (p. 394).

**Propositional check (this implant, 2026-10-01; logic, not a position).**
Atoms, for one rational agent: *r* rational; *b* believes axiological
hedonism; *n* believes nothing other than pleasure is intrinsically
desirable; *d* intrinsically desires nothing other than pleasure; *p* is
significantly prevented from obtaining pleasure; *j* other things equal,
axiological hedonism should be rejected. P1 is instantiated to X = anything other than
pleasure (`r & n -> d`), P2 to `b -> n`, P3 to `r & d -> p`, and P4 to
`(r & b -> p) -> j`. `logic.py` returns `VALID` for these four premises and
conclusion `j`. Dropping P1 (premises `b -> n`, `r & d -> p`,
`(r & b -> p) -> j`) returns `INVALID` with the counterexample row
`b=T, d=F, j=F, n=T, p=F, r=T`: without Rational Desire the conclusion does
not follow, which is the premise the second response in n. 16 denies. The
tool does not represent the quantifier over X, "other things equal", or
"significantly"; the check covers the propositional skeleton only.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Joseph Butler** (*Fifteen Sermons*, 1726; Sermon XI) — engrossing self-love "may be so prevalent as to disappoint itself"; "the classic statement" per Dietz (p. 388).
- **John Stuart Mill** (*Autobiography*, 1873, ch. V) — happiness attained "by not making it the direct end"; links the view to "the anti-self-consciousness theory of Carlyle".
- **Henry Sidgwick** (*The Methods of Ethics*, 1874; 7th ed. 1907, I.iv.2, II.iii.2) — names the "fundamental paradox of Hedonism"; Egoistic Hedonism "practically self-limiting".
- **C. D. Broad** (*Five Types of Ethical Theory*, 1930: 74, via Dietz p. 389) — the "most wretched lives are led by men who have nothing to do but think of their own happiness and scheme for it."
- **Andrew Moore** (SEP "Hedonism", 2004/2013, §1) — the paradox as a claim about hedonically motivated action.
- **Jens Timmermann** (2005) and **Fred Feldman** (2006) — exchange in *Analysis* (titles only).
- **Roger Crisp** (2006, p. 637; SEP "Well-Being" §4.1) — indirect pursuit in the hedonist's reply to the experience machine.
- **Alexander Dietz** (2019; 2021) — the paradox as an objection to axiological hedonism; the paradox of desire satisfaction; the backgrounding move.
- **Javier Martínez & Mikel Labiano** (2022) — Epicurus and the Cyrenaics (abstract only).

## Framings and reframings

- **From psychology to axiology.** Dietz: "First, it may just not be obvious how, exactly, the paradox of hedonism is supposed to provide us with an objection to hedonism about welfare." (p. 390). His reframing turns the observation into an argument about what rational believers of a theory will desire (p. 394).
- **A wider paradox.** Dietz: "I have also argued that the paradox of hedonism, if it is a problem at all, is in fact a symptom of a deeper problem, the paradox of desire satisfaction, which challenges all theories which claim that satisfying our desires is an essential part of well-being." (p. 409).
- **Means and ends.** Sidgwick: "it might seem difficult to execute the practical paradox of attaining pleasure by aiming at something else." (II.iii.2), answered by his remark on taking an interest in any end through effort (quoted above).
- **Self-love versus particular passions.** Garrett's report of Butler: "no particular passion has happiness in general as its object." (SEP §5).

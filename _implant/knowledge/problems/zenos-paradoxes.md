# Zeno's paradoxes of motion

## The question

Can anything move, if a distance or a stretch of time can be divided without
end? Zeno's book is lost; "what we know of his arguments is second-hand,
principally through Aristotle and his commentators", and they come "in
paraphrase" (Huggett 2024, preamble). Aristotle counts four arguments against
motion (*Physics* VI.9, 239b10–14):

- **Dichotomy.** "The first asserts the non-existence of motion on the ground that that which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal" (239b11).
- **Achilles.** "in a race the quickest runner can never overtake the slowest, since the pursuer must first reach the point whence the pursued started, so that the slower must always hold a lead" (239b14). Aristotle: "This argument is the same in principle as that which depends on bisection" (239b14–18).
- **Arrow.** "the flying arrow is at rest, which result follows from the assumption that time is composed of moments" (239b30). Diogenes Laertius gives a one-line form: "A moving body moves neither where it is nor where it is not" (*Lives* IX.72, Hicks tr.; excerpt: `raw/diogenes-laertius-9-72-zeno-moving-body.md`).
- **Stadium** (briefly). Two rows of equal bodies pass each other and a stationary row; Aristotle: "This, he thinks, involves the conclusion that half a given time is equal to double that time" (240a1–4).

Huggett's Dichotomy (§3.1): before 1/2 the runner must cover 1/4, before
that 1/8, …; the series "contains no first distance to run", and infinitely
many finite distances, "which, Zeno would have us conclude, must take an
infinite time". The Arrow "explicitly turns on a particular kind of
assumption of plurality: that time is composed of moments (or ‘nows’) and
nothing else" (§3.3). Huggett reads them as "‘reductio ad absurdum’ arguments (or ‘dialectic’ in
the sense of the period)"; a [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md) is "a
demonstration that a contradiction or absurd consequence follows from
apparently reasonable assumptions" (§1.1).

**Who Zeno was.** Plato's *Parmenides* is the biographical source: "Almost everything that we know about Zeno of Elea is to be found in the opening pages of Plato’s Parmenides" (Huggett, preamble).
In Jowett's translation Zeno "was nearly 40 years of age" when "Socrates, then
a very young man, came to see them", and his writings "were meant to protect
the arguments of Parmenides against those who make fun of him" (*Parmenides*
127b–128d; excerpt: `raw/plato-parmenides-127b-128d-zeno-and-his-book.md`);
Huggett estimates "a birth date for Zeno around 490 BC" (preamble).

## Why it matters

- Aristotle: the arguments "cause so much disquietude to those who try to
  solve the problems that they present" (*Physics* VI.9, 239b10–14).
- Russell (1914, Lecture VI) calls them "serious arguments, raising
  difficulties which it has taken two thousand years to answer" (excerpt:
  `raw/russell-1914-external-world-lecture-6-zeno.md`).
- Huggett (§1.2) reports the received view as holding that the paradoxes
  "reveal problems that cannot be resolved without the full resources of
  mathematics as worked out in the Nineteenth century"; he adds that the
  question also concerns physics: "nothing guarantees a priori that space has the structure of the continuum, or even that parts of space add up according to Cauchy’s definition" (§5).
- Diogenes Laertius: "Aristotle says that Zeno was the inventor of
  dialectic" (IX.25).

## Positions taken

Taken from Huggett's §§3 and 5, in roughly his order. None is ranked here.

- **Appeal to experience.** Simplicius reports that Diogenes the Cynic
  answered by "silently standing and walking" (Huggett §3.1, citing
  Simplicius, *On Aristotle's Physics* 1012.22). *Against:* Huggett (§3.1)
  judges that "A couple of common responses are not adequate", holding that
  this one "would not impress Zeno, who, as a paid up Parmenidean, held that
  many things are not as they appear".
- **Aristotle (1): divide the time too.** Aristotle says Zeno's argument
  "makes a false assumption in asserting that it is impossible for a thing to
  pass over or severally to come in contact with infinite things in a finite
  time", because "anything continuous" is called infinite "either in respect
  of divisibility or in respect of their extremities" (*Physics* VI.2,
  233a21–26). For Achilles: the leader "is overtaken nevertheless if it is
  granted that it traverses the finite distance prescribed" (VI.9,
  239b26–29). *For:* Huggett (§3.1) reports that Sattler (2020) "argues that
  understanding motion via such a mathematical correspondence between
  distance and duration was a crucial advance made by Aristotle".
- **Aristotle (2): potential vs actual infinity.** Aristotle later calls the
  first reply "adequate as a reply to the questioner" but "as an account of
  the fact and explanation of its true nature it is inadequate" (*Physics*
  VIII.8, 263a13–18; SEP cites 263a15); a continuum's halves "are not
  actual but potential halves", so passing through infinitely many units is possible "if
  they are potential" and not "If the units are actual" (263a28–263b6).
  *Against:* Huggett (§3.1) writes that it "assumes that a clear distinction can be drawn between potential and actual infinities, something that was never fully achieved", and that the distinction "has played no role in mathematics since Cantor tamed the transfinite numbers".
- **The received view (Russell, Grünbaum, Salmon): Cauchy sums and Cantor's
  transfinites.** "Following a lead given by Russell (1929, 182–198), a
  number of philosophers—notably Grünbaum (1967), but also see Salmon
  (2001)—took up the task of showing how modern mathematics could solve all
  of Zeno’s paradoxes" (Huggett §1.2). Russell's own words: if half the
  course takes half a minute, the next quarter a quarter, "the whole course
  will take a minute" (1914, Lecture VI); for Achilles, "the view that an
  infinite number of instants make up an infinitely long time is not true".
  Huggett (§3.2) holds that the series of Achilles' positions has an
  ordinal number: "we shall take it that the series is mathematically
  legitimate". *For:* Huggett (§5) states "the tools of standard modern mathematics are up to the job of resolving the paradoxes". *Against:* Bergson (1911, ch. IV, n. 99) says "we do not consider the sophism of Zeno refuted by the fact that the geometrical progression ... has a finite sum"; Huggett (§1.2) reports that "more recently a number of commentators have challenged the specific interpretations of the received view".
- **At-at theory of motion (for the Arrow).** Motion at an instant "depends
  on whether it travels any distance in a finite interval that includes the
  instant in question", the arrow being at successive points at successive
  times (Huggett §3.3). Russell (1914, Lecture VI): "there is no next position
  and no next moment". Huggett names Arntzenius (2000,
  [doi:10.5840/monist20008328](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist20008328)) and
  Salmon (2001, 23–4) for "further discussion of this ‘at-at’ conception"
  (§3.3). *Against:* Huggett (§3.3) grants it "carries the counter-intuitive implication that motion is not something that happens at any instant, but rather only over finite periods of time".
- **Aristotle on the Arrow: time is not made of moments.** "time is not
  composed of indivisible moments any more than any other magnitude is
  composed of indivisibles" (*Physics* VI.9, 239b5–9). Huggett (§3.3) reports
  Pemberton (2022,
  [doi:10.3366/anph.2022.0061](https://doi.org/10.3366/anph.2022.0061)):
  "Aristotle rejects the claim that time is composed of instants at all".
- **Temporal becoming (Bergson, James, Whitehead).** "Such thinkers as
  Bergson (1911), James (1911, Ch 10–11) and Whitehead (1929) argued that
  Zeno’s paradoxes show that space and time are not structured as a
  mathematical continuum" and "that the way to preserve the reality of
  motion was to deny that space and time are composed of points and
  instants" (Huggett §5). Bergson: the arrow's "movement AB is as simple, as
  indecomposable, in so far as it is movement, as the tension of the bow
  that shoots it"; "each of his steps must be treated as indivisible"
  (1911, ch. IV; excerpt:
  `raw/bergson-1911-creative-evolution-arrow-and-achilles.md`). *Against:*
  Huggett (§5) judges that, given the mathematical resolution, "no such
  conclusion seems warranted".
- **Supertasks.** "Black (1950–51) dubbed ‘infinity machines’" (§5; Black,
  *Analysis* 11: 91–101,
  [doi:10.1093/analys/11.5.91](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/11.5.91)).
  Black and his followers held that the paradoxes "showed that after all
  mathematics was not applicable to space, time and motion", asking whether
  one can "complete what is known as a ‘supertask’" (Huggett §5). Huggett
  (§3.1) cites a run "punctuated by finite rests" as "arguably showing the
  possibility of completing an infinite series of finite tasks in a finite
  time (Huggett 2010, 21–2)".
- **Non-standard analysis.** "McLaughlin and Miller (1992) and McLaughlin
  (1994) show how Zeno’s paradoxes can be resolved in non-standard
  analysis"; Huggett adds "there is no need for non-standard analysis to
  solve the paradoxes: either system is equally successful"; Reeder (2015,
  [doi:10.1007/s11229-014-0620-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-014-0620-1))
  "argues that non-standard analysis is unsatisfactory regarding the arrow"
  (Huggett §5).
- **The Stadium.** Aristotle locates "The fallacy of the reasoning" in the assumption that a body passes a moving and a resting body in equal time (*Physics* VI.9, 240a1–4). Huggett (§3.4) reports "The general verdict is that Zeno was hopelessly confused about relative velocities in this paradox"; Sattler (2015) "argues against this and other common readings"; Davey (2007) offers a reading on point-sized bodies; a further reconstruction takes it "as an argument against an atomic theory of space and time".
- **A physical question too.** Huggett (§5) reports that for the received
  view "a purely mathematical solution is not sufficient", and that
  "quantum theories of gravity likely imply" space and time are not a
  continuum.

- **Zeno of Elea** (b. ca 490 BCE, Huggett's estimate) — the arguments;
  **Plato**, *Parmenides* 127b–128d — the biographical source.
- **Aristotle** — *Physics* VI.2, VI.9, VIII.8: both replies;
  **Diogenes the Cynic** — walking (via Simplicius 1012.22).

  his book" (Huggett, preamble); **Diogenes Laertius** — IX.72, IX.25.

## Framings and reframings

- **Whose view is under attack?** "For the first half of the Twentieth
  century, the majority reading—following Tannery (1885)—of Zeno held that
  his arguments were directed against a technical doctrine of the
  Pythagoreans"; "a series of commentators (Vlastos, 1967, summarizes the
  argument and contains references) forcefully argued that Zeno’s target was
  instead a common sense understanding of plurality and motion"; "Tannery’s
  interpretation still has its defenders (see e.g., Matson 2001)" (Huggett
  §5). Russell (1914, Lecture VI) holds, "in spite of MM. Milhaud and Paul
  Tannery", that Zeno "is anxious to prove that motion is really
  impossible".
- **Mathematics vs physics.** Huggett (§5) reports that Black's strand
  accepts the mathematics but denies it applies to motion, while the received view seeks to show "that
  that mathematics correctly describes objects, time and space" (Huggett §5).

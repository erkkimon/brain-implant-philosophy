# The surprise examination paradox

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
evidence levels per [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).
Source for the map: Roy Sorensen,
[SEP Fall 2024 "Epistemic Paradoxes"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/epistemic-paradoxes/)
(substantive revision 2022-03-03; excerpt:
`raw/sep-epistemic-paradoxes-fall-2024-surprise-test.md`), with Chow

`raw/chow-1998-surprise-examination-unexpected-hanging.md`). Sorensen is

assessments below are marked as his. Also called the unexpected hanging
or prediction paradox; one of the [problems](./index.md) filed as a
[paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md).

## The question

"A teacher announces that there will be a surprise test next week. It
will be a surprise in that the students will not be able to know in
advance on which day the exam will be given" (Sorensen, SEP 2022, §1).
The student's objection, for a class meeting Monday, Wednesday, Friday:
a Friday test would be predictable on Thursday; with Friday gone, a
Wednesday test would be foreseeable on Tuesday; with both gone, "I would
know that the test must be on Monday [...] Therefore, it is impossible
for there to be a surprise test" (§1). Chow gives the week-long version,
in which the students "are of course totally surprised when the exam
occurs (on Wednesday, say)" (1998, §1, arXiv pp. 1–2).

Sorensen names the clash: "On the one hand, we have the student’s
elimination argument. [...] On the other hand, common sense says that
surprise tests are possible even when we have had advance warning that
one will occur at some point" (§1). The term the question contracts on
is *surprise*: "a test is a surprise if and only if the student cannot
know beforehand which day the test will occur. Therefore the riddle of
the surprise test qualifies as an epistemic paradox" (§1).

**Where it comes from.** Chow: "It has many variants, the earliest probably being Lennart Ekbom’s surprise drill, and the best known to mathematicians (thanks to Quine and Gardner) being an unexpected hanging" (1998, §1). Sorensen reports that the backward elimination argument "can be discerned in German folktales dating back to 1756 (Sorensen 2003a, 267)" (§1).

## Why it matters

- Sorensen: "The surprise test has been claimed to invalidate the law of bivalence, the KK principle [...] and the closure principle [...] (Immerman 2017)" (§1).
- Chow reports the suggestion that the paradox "may be related to the
  iterated prisoner’s dilemma" (§4, arXiv p. 10) and that "Sorensen [30]
  has argued that the two are really the same", while Chow himself judges the prisoner's dilemma
  "essentially distinct" because it "does not lead to an explicit
  contradiction" (1998, §4, arXiv p. 11).
- Chow records links "to Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, game theory,
  and several other logical paradoxes (e.g., the liar paradox and the
  sorites paradox)" (1998, arXiv p. 1); compare [the liar](liar-paradox.md)
  and [the sorites](sorites-paradox.md).
- Chow's "meta-paradox": the paradox "seems easy to resolve", yet "nearly
  a hundred papers on the paradox have been published, and still no
  consensus on its correct resolution has been reached" (1998, §1, p. 2).

## Positions taken

Grouped as the SEP entry orders them (§§1.1–6.2), with the families Chow
and the KK literature add. None is ranked here.

- **The announcement is self-defeating (O'Connor, Cohen).** "In the twentieth century, the first published reaction to the surprise text paradox was to endorse the student’s elimination argument" (Sorensen, §1.1). O'Connor ([1948](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lvii.227.358)) compared it to "‘I am not speaking now’": consistent, yet it "“could not conceivably be true in any circumstances” (O’Connor 1948, 358)"; Cohen ([1950](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lix.233.85)) called it a pragmatic paradox, "a statement that is falsified by its own utterance" (§1.1).
  *Against:* Sorensen (§1.1) calls this "too monolithic": the announcement "does compromise one aspect of the surprise", but "this compromise is not itself enough to make the announcement self-falsifying".
- **The teacher can out-randomise or out-compute the student.** "Some game theorists suggest that the teacher could defeat this strategy by choosing the test date at random" (§1.2).
  *Against:* Sorensen (§1.2): "This plan is risky. If, through the chance process, the last day happens to be selected, then abiding by the outcome means giving an unsurprising test."
  A variant adapts Scriven's (1964) Predictor/Avoider: "Let the teacher be Avoider and the student be Predictor. Avoider must win" (§1.2).
  *Against:* Lewis and Richardson ([1966](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00398598)) hold that "Scriven equivocates" on whether both "have enough time to finish their calculations" (§1.2); Sorensen judges such idealising "would fail to disarm the student’s elimination argument" (§1.2).
- **No truth yet about the future (Weiss).** Weiss ([1952](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lxi.242.265)) "concludes that the student’s argument falsely assumes he knows that the announcement is true", which the student can know only "after it becomes true" (§1.3).
  *Against:* Sorensen reports that Quine "abominates Weiss’ reasoning", which in Sorensen's words "breeches the law of bivalence"; Quine "believes that the riddle of the surprise test should not be answered by surrendering classical logic" (§1.3; see [classical logic](../methods/classical-logic.md)).
- **The student cannot know the announcement (Quine).** Quine ([1953](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lxii.245.65)) treats the elimination as "only a reductio ad absurdum of the supposition that the student knows that the announcement is true"; so "a test on any day would be unforeseen" (Sorensen, §2). Chow reports this as "Perhaps the most popular candidate" for rejection, (1998, §3, p. 8), an approach he finds in Binkley 1968, Quine 1953 and Sorensen 1984 among others (p. 9).
  *Against:* Sorensen: "Common sense suggests that the students are informed by the announcement" (§2); a general scepticism about the future is "disproportionate and indiscriminate" (§2). Murzi, Eichhorn & Mayr: "Quine's solution comes at a heavy price" — why would students not know it "on the basis of the testimony offered by their trustworthy and reliable teacher?" ([2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/tht3.473), §"The students' reasoning"; excerpt: `raw/murzi-eichhorn-mayr-2021-surprise-exam-kk-is-innocent.md`).
- **Self-reference: the announcement as a knower sentence (Kaplan & Montague; Shaw, Fitch; Nerlich).** Kaplan and Montague ([1960](https://doi.org/10.1305/ndjfl/1093956549)) read the announcement as self-referential — "(K-3) Either the test is on Monday but you do not know it before Monday, [...] or this announcement is known to be false" — and let the dates shrink to zero: "(K-0) This sentence is known to be false" (§5.1). Nerlich ([1961](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lxx.280.503)) makes the announcement "a Gödel sentence about the student" (§5.1). Chow's "logical school": the announcement "must be formulated as a self-referential statement!"; Fitch's formalisation "seems to vindicate Shaw" by showing it "self-contradictory" (1998, §2, pp. 5–6). Kritchman and Raz (2010) locate the flaw in "a hidden assumption that one can prove the consistency of the mathematical theory in which the derivation is done" (arXiv abstract; excerpt: `raw/surprise-examination-paradox-bibliographic-records.md`).
  *Against:* "Several commentators on the surprise test paradox object that interpreting surprise as unprovability changes the topic" — it becomes "a variation of the liar paradox" (§5.1); Chow reports the objection that the logical proof "does not give any explanation for why the teacher’s announcement appears to be vindicated after the fact" (§2, p. 6), which Chow answers by holding the students "misinterpret the announcement" (p. 7).
- **Doxastic logic and reflection (Binkley).** Binkley ([1968](https://doi.org/10.2307/2024556)): "The student can foresee that he will not believe the announcement if no test is given by Thursday", so "the student on Sunday will not believe the announcement when it is first uttered" (§5.3).
  *Against:* Sorensen calls the Thursday account "compelling" but says spreading "the incredulity from the future to the past is open to three challenges" (§5.3).
- **Knowledge of the announcement is not retained; it becomes a blindspot (Wright & Sudbury; Sorensen; Kripke; Harman).** Sorensen's own solution (1988, 328–343, per §5.4): on Thursday the remaining disjunct "(iii) is a blindspot for the students but not for, say, the teacher’s colleagues. Hence, the teacher can give a surprise test on Friday [...] Knowledge can be lost without forgetting anything" (§5.4). Murzi et al. credit the diagnosis to "Wright and Sudbury (1977) and Sorensen (1988)": the reasoning fails through "the inconsistency between a certain knowledge retention principle and the existence of blindspots for knowledge" (2021, introduction; [Wright & Sudbury 1977](https://doi.org/10.1080/00048407712341031) not read). Kripke "takes KR, the knowledge retention principle, to be the culprit" (Murzi et al., 2021); Harman's defeaters make "The students lose knowledge of the test announcement even though they do not forget" (Sorensen, §6.2). Murzi et al. (2021) argue for this family.
  *Against:* Sorensen himself lists its costs — "losing knowledge without forgetting, disagreement amongst equally well-informed ideal reasoners, rationally changing your mind without the acquisition of counter-evidence" (§6). Williamson calls the blindspot analysis "incomplete" because "It yields no objection to the reasoning in the Glimpse" (2000, p. 138, as quoted by Murzi et al. 2021).
- **Give up the KK principle: knowledge cannot be iterated far enough (Williamson; Harrison; McLelland & Chihara).** Hemp (IEP §3) reports thinkers who hold "the paradox can be solved by rejecting the principle" — "Harrison 1969, McLelland and Chihara 1975 and Williamson 1992: 226-32 and 2000:135-146" (excerpt: `raw/hemp-iep-kk-principle-surprise-examination.md`). Williamson ([1992](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/101.402.217); [2000, ch. 6](https://doi.org/10.1093/019925656x.003.0007)) pairs the surprise with a margin-for-error case, "the Glimpse", to be treated alike by "disallowing certain applications of KK" (Murzi et al. 2021). Chow reports others who discard "the “temporal retention principle”" or "“Hintikka’s KK principle”" (1998, §3, p. 9).
  *Against:* Murzi et al. (2021, §"KK is innocent") argue a paradoxical conclusion is reached before KK is used: "Invalidating KK is therefore of no use". Sorensen's designated-student variant, Chow reports, makes the needed knowledge "immediately plausible without reference to time or the KK principle" (1998, §3, p. 9).
- **Dissolve the concept of knowledge (Quine, later).** Sorensen reports that "Those willing to abandon the concept of knowledge can dissolve the surprise test paradox", and replies it "is like using a suicide bomb to kill a fly" (§2).
- **No single resolution needed (Chow).** Chow holds that when several formalisations capture the paradox, "it is misguided to speak of the resolution" (italics in the original) (1998, §1, p. 3; see Framings).

No survey figure is recorded.

## Framings and reframings

- **Epistemic, not semantic.** Sorensen: a liar-like mock-up "is not an epistemic paradox. For the paradoxes turn on the semantic concept of truth rather than an epistemic concept" (§5.1). Chow likewise "decided that the Kaplan–Montague paradox of the knower was distinct from the surprise examination paradox" (bibliography preface, arXiv p. 14).
- **A Moorean sentence.** Chow reports the view that the surprise exam is "a more intricate version" of a "“Moore paradox” or a “blindspot,”" seen by shrinking the week to one day (1998, §3, p. 9).
- **Two schools, both reasonable.** Chow describes an "epistemological
  school" and a "logical school" and holds that "both approaches are
  reasonable and neither is guilty of evasion" (1998, §1, p. 3).
- **Knowledge is personal and dated.** On Sorensen's account, "This
  solution makes who you are relevant to what you can know" (§5.4).

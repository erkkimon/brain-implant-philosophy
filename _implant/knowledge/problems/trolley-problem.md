# The trolley problem

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md):
every verdict about a case below belongs to a named philosopher or to a
survey, never to this page. Sources are the SEP entries by Woollard
("Doing vs. Allowing Harm") and McIntyre ("Doctrine of Double Effect"),
and the primary papers they cite (excerpt:
`raw/sep-double-effect-and-doing-allowing-fall-2024-trolley.md`).

## The question

The problem is a *pair* (or family) of cases, and the question is about the
difference between them, not about one case alone.

- **Foot's pair (1967).** "a judge must choose between framing and killing
  an innocent man and allowing five innocents to be killed in a riot. In
  the second, a trolley driver must choose between turning a trolley so
  that it runs over an innocent man attached to a track and allowing the
  trolley to run over and kill five innocent people" (Woollard,
  [SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/doing-allowing/),
  §3, summarising Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the
  Double Effect", *Oxford Review* 5 (1967): 5–15; repr. *Virtues and Vices*,
  1978, pp. 19–32, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199252866.003.0002)).
  Foot "claimed that it was wrong to kill in the first case, but not wrong
  in the second" (Woollard §3).
- **Thomson's bystander (1976, 1985).** Thomson named the problem and
  "modified the case so that it was a bystander, not the driver, who had to
  make the choice" (Woollard §3; Thomson, "Killing, Letting Die, and the
  Trolley Problem", *The Monist* 59 (1976): 204–217,
  [doi](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist197659224); "The Trolley Problem",
  *Yale Law Journal* 94 (1985): 1395–1415).
- **Pushing.** The contrasting case McIntyre cites: "pushing someone onto
  the track in front of the speeding trolley in order to stop it in order
  to protect five people who cannot escape on the track ahead"
  ([SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/double-effect/),
  §4.5).

Stated as a question: if it is permissible to divert the trolley from five
to one, what explains why, in Woollard's words, "it is not normally
permissible to kill one to save five" — in the judge case, or by pushing? Woollard frames the two
ways of answering: "(a) appeal to some additional distinction to explain
why it is permissible for the bystander to turn the trolley towards five to
save one, even though it is not normally permissible to kill one to save
five or (b) show that we should abandon the intuition that it is
permissible for the bystander to turn the trolley" (§3).

## Why it matters

- **Testing moral principles.** The pair is used to test candidate
  principles — doing vs. allowing and double effect —
  against case verdicts (Woollard §3; McIntyre §4.5).
- **Moral cognition.** Mikhail (2011) takes "the paired intuitions in the
  Trolley Problem as proof of the fundamental role of the distinction
  between intended means and foreseen side effects in an implicit principle
  guiding moral judgment" (McIntyre §4.5).
- **Autonomous vehicles.** McIntyre reports proposals to treat the switch
  verdict as a precedent for vehicle algorithms (Lin 2016, 76–7), and asks
  "whether the highly abstract and context-less trolley problem elicits
  reliable moral judgments that can be used as a precedent in programming
  autonomous vehicles" (§4.5; also Nyholm & Smids 2016).
- **Whether the cases are dilemmas.** If both options were wrong, the switch
  case would be a [moral dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md) in McConnell's
  sense; this page's sources do not use that classification (a structural
  note, manifest G4), and the question connects to
  [Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?](moral-dilemmas.md).

## Positions taken

Grouped as McIntyre groups the double-effect literature (§4.5), with the
doing/allowing proposals from Woollard (§3). None is ranked.

- **Doing vs. allowing explains the pair (Foot).** "the judge must choose
  between killing one and merely allowing five to die, while the trolley
  driver must choose between killing one and killing five" (Woollard §3).
  Woollard reports that Thomson's bystander version "undermines Foot’s
  claim that the difference between doing and allowing explains our
  intuitions about these cases", since the bystander chooses between
  killing and letting die.
- **Double effect explains it.** On the doctrine, "sometimes it is
  permissible to cause a harm as an unintended and merely foreseen side
  effect [...] even though it would not be permissible to cause such a harm
  as a means" (McIntyre, preamble). Applied: in switching, the one's death
  is a side effect; in pushing, it is the means (§4.5). Mikhail (2011) is
  McIntyre's example of those who take the paired intuitions as evidence
  for such a principle.
- **Switching is not permissible after all (Thomson 2008; absolutist
  double effect).** Thomson "changed her mind and argued that the consensus
  that it is permissible for the bystander to turn the trolley was
  mistaken", via a third option of turning the trolley onto oneself
  (Woollard §3; "Turning the Trolley", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 36
  (2008): 359–374, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1088-4963.2008.00144.x)).
  McIntyre places here "those who uphold an absolutist version of the
  principle of double effect and deny that it provides a permission to
  swerve the trolley (Anscombe, 1982)" (§4.5).
- **Double effect does not explain it (McIntyre 2001).** One "could reject
  the claim that the principle of double effect could explain the
  permissibility of switching the trolley [...] while remaining neutral
  about what verdict to render about such artificial cases" (McIntyre
  §4.5, describing her own 2001 position).
- **Redirection is permissible, sacrifice of self not required
  (Fitzpatrick 2009).** "it must be permissible to sacrifice others when we
  would not sacrifice ourselves: morality cannot demand such extreme
  self-sacrifice" (Woollard §3).
- **Direction of a threat carries no weight (Parfit 2017)**, with Kamm's
  (2020) reply that it "might supervene on other morally relevant features"
  (Woollard §3).

**Survey (2009).** Asked "Trolley problem (five straight ahead, one on side
track, turn requires switching, what ought one do?): switch or don't
switch?", 931 target-faculty respondents answered: accept or lean toward
switch 68.2 %, don't switch 7.6 %, other 24.2 % (Bourget & Chalmers,
[PhilPapers Surveys results](https://philpapers.org/surveys/results.pl),
2009; excerpt: `raw/philpapers-2009-survey-trolley-and-ethics.md`). The
question describes only the switch case. A survey reports what
respondents said, not which answer is right. The 2020 figures could not be
retrieved on 2026-09-26 (the results site returned a bot challenge); they
are an open todo.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Thomas Aquinas** (13th c.) — credited with the principle of double
  effect, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 64 a. 7 (McIntyre §1). Not a trolley
  author; the ancestor of one family of answers.
- **Philippa Foot** — the original pair and the doing/allowing explanation,
  1967.
- **Judith Jarvis Thomson** — the name, the bystander version (1976, 1985,
  1986) and the 2008 reversal.
- **G. E. M. Anscombe** — absolutist double effect denying a permission to
  swerve (1982, as placed by McIntyre §4.5).
- **Alison McIntyre** — double effect does not explain the switch case
  (2001).
- **John Mikhail** — the pair as evidence for an implicit moral grammar
  (2011).
- **Frances Kamm, Derek Parfit, Fitzpatrick** — see Positions (Woollard §3).

## Framings and reframings

- **The trolley problem is separate from doing/allowing.** Woollard: "the
  two should be kept separate. First, we must settle whether the
  doing/allowing distinction is morally significant" — then the trolley
  problem is "a further challenge" (§3).
- **Doubts about the method.** McIntyre questions whether "artificial" and
  "context-less" cases elicit reliable judgments (§4.5); Thomson 2008
  suggests "people’s willingness to view it as permissible is a result of
  inadequate reflection" (McIntyre §4.5, reporting Thomson).

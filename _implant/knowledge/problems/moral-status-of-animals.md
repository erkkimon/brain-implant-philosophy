# The moral status of animals

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Map: Lori Gruen & Susana Monsó,
[SEP Fall 2024 "The Moral Status of Animals"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-animal/)
(rev. 2024-08-12; excerpt: `raw/sep-moral-animal-fall-2024-moral-status.md`), and Scott D. Wilson,
[IEP "Animals and Ethics"](https://iep.utm.edu/anim-eth/) (excerpt:
`raw/iep-animals-and-ethics-wilson-indirect-theories-marginal-cases.md`). Both entries argue in
places; their assessments are theirs. Gruen is also one of the authors the SEP entry reports (§3).

## The question

Gruen & Monsó (intro): "Is there something distinctive about humanity that justifies the idea that humans have moral status while non-humans do not?"
Their gloss (§1): "A morally considerable being is a being who can be wronged." Jaworska &
Tannenbaum ([SEP "The Grounds of Moral Status"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/grounds-moral-status/),
intro; excerpt: `raw/sep-grounds-moral-status-fall-2024-animals-contractualism.md`): "An entity has moral status if and only if it matters (to some degree) from the moral point of view for its own sake."
Two sub-questions run through the literature: whether animals count directly or only through
humans, and, if directly, whether their interests count equally with like human interests.

## Why it matters

- **Practice.** Wilson (intro) contrasts reactions to "puppy mills" with those to factory farms;
  the entries tie the theories to food, experimentation and captivity (IEP §3a–b; SEP §2.2).
- **The boundary of morality.** Where the line falls also decides the status of humans who lack
  the capacities proposed as criteria — the argument from marginal cases below.
- **Which beings.** Gruen & Monsó (§1.4): "sentience remains a non-observable capacity, and consequently despite the scientific advances there is a lot of uncertainty regarding the sentience of animals that are very phylogenetically distant from us, such as insects."
  See [The Edge of Sentience](../works/the-edge-of-sentience.md).

## Positions taken

Wilson (intro) groups the field in "three general categories: Indirect theories, direct but unequal theories, and moral equality theories."
Gruen & Monsó add views that reject capacity-based criteria (§3). In the order of the IEP:

1. **Indirect duties only.** "Indirect theories deny animals moral status or equal consideration with humans due to a lack of consciousness, reason, or autonomy." (IEP intro, naming Kant, Descartes, Aquinas and Carruthers.)
   Kant, as the SEP entry quotes the *Lectures on Ethics* (§1.3.1; Ak 27:459): "If a man shoots his dog because the animal is no longer capable of service, he does not fail in his duty to the dog, for the dog cannot judge, but his act is inhuman and damages in himself that humanity which it is his duty to show towards mankind."
   Korsgaard's reading ([Tanner Lecture 2004](https://tannerlectures.utah.edu/_documents/a-to-z/k/korsgaard_2005.pdf),
   p. 90; excerpt: `raw/korsgaard-2004-fellow-creatures-tanner-lecture.md`): "But these moral duties, it turns out, are not owed to the other animals, but rather to ourselves."
   Carruthers (1992: 153–54, as quoted in IEP §1e) on torturing a cat for fun: "it remains wrong independently of its effect on any animal lover".
2. **Contractualist exclusion.** IEP §1d: in *The Animals Issue* (Cambridge UP, 1992;
   [doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511597961)) "Carruthers notes that if we do so extend Rawls’s conception, animals will have no direct moral standing."
   Contractors "will not adopt rules that give special protection to animals" (IEP §1d).
3. **Direct but unequal.** IEP §2: "the interests of animals count directly in the assessment of actions that affect them, but do not count for as much as the interests of human beings."
   One ground reported there (§2b): rationality, autonomy and self-consciousness "confer a full and equal moral status to those that possess them".
4. **Equal consideration (utilitarian).** Bentham's note ([IPML ch. XVII §1, para. IV
   n.](https://www.econlib.org/library/Bentham/bnthPML18.html), 1823 ed.; excerpt:
   `raw/bentham-1789-principles-ch17-can-they-suffer.md`): "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"
   Singer (["All Animals Are Equal"](https://digitalcommons.brockport.edu/phil_ex/vol5/iss1/6),
   1974, p. 107; excerpt: `raw/singer-1974-all-animals-are-equal.md`): "If a being suffers, there can be no moral justification for refusing to take that suffering into consideration."
   Sentience "is the only defensible boundary of concern for the interests of others" (p. 108).
   Equality "does not imply that we must treat both groups in exactly the same way" (p. 104).
5. **Animal rights.** Regan, *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983; UC Press reissue
   [doi](https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520404441)), as the IEP (§3b) reports: "the only property that is common to both normal adult human beings and the marginal cases is the property of being a subject-of-a-life."
   Such beings, in Regan's words (1983: 243, quoted in IEP §3b), "have beliefs and desires; perception, memory, and a sense of the future, including their own future".
6. **Kantian direct duties.** Korsgaard (2004, p. 82): "I am going to argue that despite appearances, and despite what he himself thought, Kant’s arguments reveal the ground of our obligations to the other animals."
   Her conclusion (p. 106): "Animal nature is an end-in-itself, because our own legislation makes it so."
   Her 2018 book (*Fellow Creatures*, OUP, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198753858.001.0001);
   publisher abstract only): "This book argues that we are obligated to treat all sentient animals as “ends in themselves.”"
   Wood (1998), as the SEP (§1.3.1) reports: beings with "“the infrastructure of rational nature”, should be directly morally considerable".
7. **Relational, care and virtue views.** SEP §3: "some feminists have argued instead that we ought to understand moral status in relational terms given that moral recognition is invariably a social practice."
   Anderson (2004: 289, quoted in SEP §3): "It depends, deeply, on the kind of relations they can have with us."
   Virtue view reported from Clark 1977 and Diamond 2001 (SEP §3): eating animals is wrong because "we do not display the traits of character that kind, sensitive, compassionate, mature, and thoughtful members of a moral community should display."
   Donaldson & Kymlicka (SEP §2.2): "other rights arise as a result of the different relations that humans have had with specific animals."

**Opinion data.** 2020 PhilPapers Survey (Bourget & Chalmers), question "Eating animals and animal
products", population "All respondents", N = 1764 answering (excerpt:
`raw/philpapers-2020-survey-eating-animals.md`): accept or lean towards omnivorism 48.02%,
vegetarianism 26.47%, veganism 18.37%, other 12.53% (inclusive figures; the survey's own
definitions: omnivorism "yes and yes", vegetarianism "no and yes", veganism "no and no"). This
records what philosophers answered in 2020, not what is right.

## Arguments in play

- **From suffering.** Singer (1974, p. 107): "The capacity for suffering and enjoying things is a pre-requisite for having interests at all, a condition that must be satisfied before we can speak of interests in any meaningful way."
  Bentham separates killing from tormenting: "If the being eaten were all, there is very good reason why we should be suffered to eat such of them as we like to eat" but "is there any reason why we should be suffered to torment them? Not any that I can see."
  Korsgaard (1996: 154, quoted in SEP §1.4): "it is a pain to be in pain. And that is not a trivial fact"
- **Speciesism.** SEP §1.1: "In the 1970s, Richard Ryder coined this term while campaigning in Oxford to denote a ubiquitous type of human centered prejudice, which he thought was similar to racism."
  Singer (1974, pp. 106–107): "If possessing a higher degree of intelligence does not entitle one human to use another for his own ends, how can it entitle humans to exploit non-humans?"
  Gruen & Monsó's own assessment (§1.1): "Species membership is morally irrelevant, a bit of luck that is no more morally interesting than being born in Malaysia or Canada."
  The reply that species itself matters is named by Wilson (IEP §1f(i)): "There is one property that is immune to this line of argument, namely, the property of being human."
- **Argument from marginal cases.** IEP §1f(i), in the entry's words: "If we are justified in denying direct moral status to animals then we are justified in denying direct moral status to the marginal cases. We are not justified in denying direct moral status to the marginal cases. Therefore we are not justified denying direct moral status to animals."
  Structural note (this implant, 2026-09-27): with DenyA = we are justified in denying direct
  status to animals and DenyM = the same for the marginal cases, `logic.py check --premises "DenyA -> DenyM"
  "~DenyM" --conclusion "~DenyA"` printed "VALID" and "matches schema: modus tollens"; the dispute
  is over the premises, not the form. Singer's 1974 version (p. 113) names "humans with severe and irreparable brain damage, and also of infant humans".
  Replies reported: the species property above; denying premise 2 ("Cf. Frey, 1980; Francis and Norman, 1978", IEP §1f(i));
  and "it is not counter-intuitive to conclude that the remaining individuals do not have a direct moral status after all" (IEP §1f(i), a view the entry reports).
  Contractualists answer through carers (SEP "Grounds" §2.4): one "may reasonably opt out of any agreement that does not afford sufficient moral status to one’s children and others one cares about, including those who are severely cognitively impaired (Morris 2011, pp. 265–267 and Carruthers 2011, pp. 387–394)."
  Korsgaard (2004, p. 82) answers inside Kant: infants and the impaired keep "their standing as rational beings under the Kantian conception."
- **Against indirect duties.** Nozick (1974: 36, quoted in IEP §1f(ii)): "why should killing animals brutalize him and make him more likely to harm or kill persons"?
  Gruen & Monsó (§1.3.1), their own assessment: "This indirect view is unsatisfying—it fails to capture the independent wrong that is being done to the non-person."
  Wilson (IEP §1f(ii)) lists three replies open to indirect theorists: reject the charge, give "an alternative explanation", or "reject the claim that those sorts of acts are necessarily wrong."
- **Rights against utility.** Regan, as the IEP reports (§3b): "By focusing on interests themselves, Utilitarianism will license the most horrendous actions."
  SEP §2.2 states the objection: utilitarianism "cannot accommodate one of our most basic prima facie principles, namely that killing a morally considerable being is wrong."
  The entry then reports "at least two replies" (§2.2). On Singer and killing, IEP §3a: "Whether we are required to refrain from painlessly killing animals will depend on whether animals have an interest in continuing to exist in the future."
  SEP §2.2: "the utilitarian argument for the moral significance of animal suffering in meat production is not an argument for vegetarianism."
  Rights may yield, per SEP §2.2: "the violation of a right is still wrong, even if it can be justified in particular circumstances."
- **Uncertain sentience.** SEP §1.4: "several authors have defended the need to incorporate precautionary principles to guide our interactions with these animals, considering that we should err on the side of caution when dealing with beings whose sentience is uncertain (e.g. Fischer 2016; Birch 2017; Knutsson & Munthe 2017)."
- **Against argument as the method.** SEP §3: "Some philosophers suggest that rational argumentation fails to capture those features of moral experience that allow us to really see why treating animals badly is wrong."
  Gruen & Monsó (§3), their own remark: "most now realize that the task of arguing that humans have a unique and exclusive moral status is rather difficult."

## Framings and reframings

- **Marginal cases or species overlap.** Gruen & Monsó (§1.3.1): "This problem, unfortunately known in the literature as the problem of “marginal cases”, poses serious difficulties for “personhood” as the criterion of moral considerability."
  Horta ([2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/japp.12051), abstract; excerpt:
  `raw/horta-2014-argument-from-species-overlap-abstract.md`) aims "to show that the name most often used for this argument (‘the argument from marginal cases’) reflects and reinforces these misunderstandings."
  Korsgaard's 2018 abstract: "It criticizes the “marginal cases” argument and advances a view of moral standing as attaching to the atemporal subjects of lives."
- **Rights vs. welfare labels.** SEP §2.2: Singer's position is "popularly, though erroneously, referred to as an animal rights position".
- **Direct vs. indirect.** Jaworska & Tannenbaum ("Grounds" §2.4) read Kant as giving reasons "to treat animals better than their (lack of) moral status implies"; they add: "Critics and proponents disagree whether these considerations can establish reasons (e.g., reasons not to interfere) that are for these beings’ sake".
- **Relations over capacities.** Anderson (2004: 289, via SEP §3): "Moral considerability is not an intrinsic property of any creature, nor is it supervenient on only its intrinsic properties, such as its capacities."
  Crary (2016: 85, via SEP §3): "human beings and other animals have empirically discoverable moral characteristics"; Gruen's "“entangled empathy” is a process that involves both affect and cognition (Gruen 2015)."
- **Related problems.** [Famine, affluence and morality](famine-affluence-and-morality.md) (Singer's
  other applied argument); [AI consciousness](ai-consciousness.md) (moral status beyond animals).

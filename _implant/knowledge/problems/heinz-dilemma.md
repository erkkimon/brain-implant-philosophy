# The Heinz dilemma

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
sources graded as in [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).

Norlock, Superson ([SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/justice-virtue/);

## The question

**The story**, as Kohlberg read it to an audience in 1971 ([ERIC ED 057 919](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41153022_0378), PDF p. 18;
excerpt: `raw/kohlberg-1971-contribution-of-developmental-psychology-heinz-story.md`):

> "In Europe, a woman was near death from a very bad disease, a special kind of cancer. There was one drug that the doctors thought might save her. It was a form of radium that a druggist in the same town had recently discovered. The drug was expensive to make, but the druggist was charging ten times what the drug cost him to make. He paid $200 for the radium and charged $2,000 for a small dose of the drug. The sick woman's husband, Heinz, went to everyone he knew to borrow the money, but he could only get together about $1,000 which is half of what it cost. He told the druggist that his wife is dying, and asked him to sell it cheaper or let him pay later. But the druggist said, "No, I discovered the drug and I'm going to make money from it." Heinz got desperate and broke into the man's store to steal the drug for his wife."

His questions followed: "Should the husband have done that? Was it right or wrong?" (PDF p. 19).
In the interview form the question is put before the act: subjects "are
read the Heinz dilemma and then asked 'Should Heinz steal the drug?'", and
the answer must "be followed up by the question 'Why?' or 'Why not?'"
(Keefer 1993, [ERIC ED357236](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED357236.pdf),
PDF p. 11, describing Colby and Kohlberg 1987; excerpt:
`raw/heinz-dilemma-empirical-and-bibliographic-records.md`). LeBar's
citation for the thought experiment is "Kohlberg 1981, p. 12" (SEP "Justice as a Virtue", §4).

**Two questions, kept apart** (structural note, this implant). (1) The first-order question: may, or
must, Heinz steal? (2) The psychologist's question: what does the *way*
a person answers reveal about moral development? Kohlberg's stages answer
the second. His dilemmas were built as a choice "between following fixed social rules and authority and serving situational human needs and welfare"
(Kohlberg 1972, [ERIC ED 069 413](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152917_0452),
PDF p. 6; excerpt: `raw/kohlberg-1972-apa-values-symposium-content-and-structure.md`).

**Structural note (this implant).** "Dilemma" in the story's name is the
loose sense of [Dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md). A *moral dilemma* in
McConnell's sense needs two requirements that cannot both be met, neither
overridden ([Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?](moral-dilemmas.md)).
By that definition alone: an answer on which stealing is not required
(the Stage 5 answer below) leaves at most one requirement, and an answer
on which one requirement overrides the other has an overridden
requirement; in either case the definition is not met. Whether the case is a moral
dilemma therefore depends on the answer given.

## Why it matters

- **Measuring moral development.** Kohlberg "developed a test, which has been widely used, to determine the stage of any individual at any given time. The test requires responses to ethical dilemmas and is to be scored by consulting an elaborate manual."
  (Matthews & Mullin, [SEP "Childhood"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/childhood/), §3.)
  Rest calls Heinz the dilemma "used extensively in Kohlbergian research"
  ([ERIC ED 144 980](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED144980.pdf), p. 3;
  excerpt: `raw/rest-1977-defining-issues-test-heinz-and-the-drug.md`).
- **Relativism.** The ERIC abstract of Kohlberg's 1971 address says it sets out to "show what is wrong with ethical relativity conceptions"; Kohlberg reads the story there "To clarify just what the issue is", and the address claims "six culturally universal stages" (PDF pp. 1, 18, 22).
- **Justice and care.** LeBar passes from Gilligan's contrast on
  Kohlberg's Heinz case to Noddings' "ethic of care", of which he writes "Following Gilligan, she sees much ethical theory as missing a feminine voice" (SEP "Justice as a Virtue", §4).

## Positions taken

**On what Heinz should do** (answers on record; not ranked):

- **Should steal; duty.** A philosopher Kohlberg classified Stage 6, asked
  whether Heinz should steal even without affection for his wife: "Yes. The value of her life is independent of any personal ties. The value of human life is a source of a categorical moral "ought" to any rational being acting in the role of a moral agent."
  (Kohlberg & Shulik 1981, [ERIC ED 228 121](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41153421_0887),
  PDF p. 8; excerpt: `raw/kohlberg-shulik-1981-aging-person-as-philosopher-heinz-stages-5-6.md`.)
- **May steal; not a duty.** A philosopher Kohlberg classified Stage 5: "While what Heinz did was not wrong, it was not his duty to do it. In this case it was not wrong for Heinz to steal the drug, but it goes beyond the call of duty; it is a deed of supererogation." (PDF p. 7.)
- **Should steal; value of life.** A graduate student: "I think he should steal it because if there is any such thing as a universal human value, it is the value of life and that would justify it."
  — who then said "all values are relative to your culture" (1971, PDF p. 20).
- **Relative.** A high-school senior, "Bob": "In my mind I can either condemn him or condone him. In this case I think it was fine." (1971, PDF p. 19.)
- **Should not steal; find another way.** Amy, eleven, in Gilligan's study,
  as Gilligan quotes her in 2018: "He shouldn’t steal, the wife shouldn’t die, there must be another way to solve this problem,"
  and "The two of them should just talk it out and find some better way to solve the problem."
  ([Gilligan 2018](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1245466.pdf), pp. 28–29;
  excerpt: `raw/gilligan-2018-revisiting-in-a-different-voice-amy-and-jake.md`.)
- **Wrong to steal.** Kohlberg reports a "National Opinion Research Survey" of "a representative sample of adult Americans": "Seventy-five percent said it was wrong to steal, though most said they might do it."
  (1971, PDF p. 19; he gives no year, sample size or reference.) No other
  answer distribution was found in a source read for this page.

**On what the answers show** (not ranked):

- **Kohlberg: stage is in the structure of the reasoning.** "We distinguish between the content of value judgment and the structure of value judgment. The structure of value judgement is the way in which the individual judges and chooses and reasons, the reasons behind his choice."
  (1972, PDF p. 9.) Of a Stage 5 criterion answer on Heinz: "It is this which leads his reasoning to be scored Stage 5, and not his use of a philosophic, social contract theory to justify his judgments." (1981, PDF pp. 11–12.)
  The stages, as Matthews & Mullin list them: "the premoral (the 1st involving punishment and obedience, and the 2nd naive hedonism), the morality of conventional role conformity (the 3rd involving seeking good relations, the 4th maintaining authority) and the morality of accepted principles (the 5th involving the morality of contract, individual rights and accepted law, the 6th individual principles of conscience)."
  (SEP §3.) Kohlberg "maintained that no one skips a stage or regresses to an earlier stage" (§3).
  At the top, reversibility: Stage 6 reasoners "imagine themselves as the wife and the druggist as well as the husband" (1981, PDF p. 9); LeBar reports that Kohlberg took Rawls' original position to exemplify it (SEP §2).
- **Gilligan: a second voice.** Norlock reports that "Gilligan argues that Kohlberg wrongly prioritizes a “morality of rights” and independence from others as better than, rather than merely different from, a “morality of responsibility” and intimate relationships with others (19)."
  ([SEP "Feminist Ethics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/feminism-ethics/), §2.2.)
  LeBar: "Gilligan carefully frames this contrast as one between voices, not a matter of ranking of dispositions or virtues" (SEP §4).
  Gilligan in 2018: "I did not do a statistical study." (p. 27.)
- **Rest: rating and ranking issues.** The Defining Issues Test gives
  the Heinz story with "12 statements", which the subject rates "on a 1 to 5 scale" and ranks; it "is assumed that people at different developmental stages define the crucial issues of a moral dilemma differently" (Rest 1977, pp. 3–4).
- **Gibbs: Stage 5 is a theory.** As Kohlberg reports him: "Gibbs claims that Stage 5 is merely a philosophic theory." (1981, PDF p. 11.) Kohlberg replies that Stage 5 is "a universal "natural" stage" (same page).

## Arguments in play

Each objection with its reply where a read source records one.

- **Philosophical partiality.** LeBar: "his approach treats utilitarianism as less cognitively advanced (more primitive) than rationalist views like Kant’s, and utilitarians (like R.M. Hare) naturally called into question the objectivity and intellectual fairness of Kohlberg’s account." (SEP §2.)
  Kohlberg's own words: "to claim that Stage 6 is better than Stage 5 is to make a very controversial philosophic claim." (1981, PDF p. 8.)
- **Cultural bias.** Simpson 1974 ([doi:10.1159/000271335](https://doi.org/10.1159/000271335)), abstract in the Crossref record: stage definitions and assumptions "are ethnocentric and culturally-biased".
  Snarey 1985 ([doi:10.1037/0033-2909.97.2.202](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.97.2.202);
  not read) is reported by Gibbs et al. 2007 ([doi:10.1016/j.dr.2007.04.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2007.04.001), abstract)
  as a survey of "45 cross-cultural studies in 27 countries" that "identified a basic stage trend but also the cultural specificity of Kohlberg's highest stages".
  For universality: Kohlberg 1971 cites findings in "the United States, Great Britain, Taiwan, Yucatan, and Turkey" (PDF p. 22);
  Gibbs et al. 2007 report "multimethod convergence" for "basic moral judgment stage development" across cultural groups (abstract).
- **Sex bias.** LeBar: "the evidence for Kohlberg’s stage sequence was drawn from studies of boys, and when one applies the sequence to the study of young girls, it turns out that girls on average end up at a less advanced stage of moral development than boys do." (SEP §2.)
  Against a sex difference in stage: Walker 1984 ([doi:10.2307/1130121](https://doi.org/10.2307/1130121)), ERIC abstract: "the overall pattern is one of nonsignificant sex differences in moral reasoning".
  Baumrind 1986 ([doi:10.2307/1130606](https://doi.org/10.2307/1130606)) replies that "the source and specific nature of these differences have yet to be established" (ERIC abstract).
- **Against Gilligan's gender claim.** Superson lists critics who say her claims perpetuate "sexist stereotypes (Tronto 1993)", that "there are not significant differences in the reasoning of males and females at different life stages (Walker 1993)", that differences track "education or general cognitive development rather than gender (Greeno and Maccoby 1993)", that "her sample size and characterizations are inadequate" (Luria 1993), and that her generalizations "can be used to support racist views (Moody-Adams 1991, 199)"
  ([SEP "Feminist Moral Psychology"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/feminism-moralpsych/), §1).
  LeBar: "Jean Hampton, among others, responded that Gilligan’s critique was itself a distortion" (SEP §2).
- **Empirical tests of the orientations.** Walker, de Vries & Trevethan 1987 ([doi:10.2307/1130221](https://doi.org/10.2307/1130221); not read), as Keefer reports: "only 40 of 240 subjects (16.7%) were consistent in their use of moral reasoning strategy" (PDF p. 3).
  Jaffee & Hyde 2000 ([doi:10.1037/0033-2909.126.5.703](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.126.5.703)), meta-analysis abstract: "small differences in the care orientation favoring females (d = -.28) and small differences in the justice orientation favoring males (d = .19)".
  Keefer 1993, 32 adults on three Heinz questions: subjects oriented to deliberation were "mostly women", those oriented to justification "mostly men" (abstract).
- **Young children.** Machery and Stich report Turiel's findings that children's "normative cognition is not “undifferentiated”", against the portrayal by "Kohlberg and Piaget" ([SEP "The Moral/Conventional Distinction"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-conventional/), §3).

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Lawrence Kohlberg** (1927–1987) — the story and the six stages; 1971
  and 1972 papers above; Kohlberg 1963 in *Vita Humana*, pp. 11–33
  ([doi:10.1159/000269667](https://doi.org/10.1159/000269667); not read).
  By 1981, "what was once termed Stage 6 is now tentatively identified as an advanced sub-stage of stage 5" (Kohlberg & Shulik, PDF p. 10).
- **R. M. Hare** — the utilitarian objection, as LeBar reports it (SEP §2).
- **Elizabeth Léonie Simpson** (1974) — cultural bias (above).
- **Carol Gilligan** — *In a Different Voice* (Harvard, 1982; not read,
  reported through SEP and Gilligan 2018); taught with Kohlberg for several years
  around the question "did my work imply a “b” route through his stages (which is what he thought) or did it imply a reformulation of moral development?" (2018, p. 27).
- **James Rest** (1977) — the Defining Issues Test.
- **John Gibbs** (1979; 2007 with Basinger, Grime and Snarey) — Stage 5
  as philosophic theory; revisionist universality model.
- **Elliot Turiel** — "a student of Lawrence Kohlberg"; young children's differentiated normative cognition (Machery & Stich, §3).
- **Lawrence Walker** (1984; 1987), **Diana Baumrind** (1986), **John
  Snarey** (1985), **Sara Jaffee & Janet Shibley Hyde** (2000) — reviews above.
- **Jean Hampton** (1993) — reads Amy as reasoning "from the care perspective" and Jake "from the justice perspective"; argues "that neither is the ideal moral view, but that Amy suffers more because she is highly exploitable" (Superson, SEP §1).
- **Nel Noddings** — *Caring* (1984), the ethic of care, "Following Gilligan" (LeBar, SEP §4; date from Norlock, SEP §2.2).

## Framings and reframings

- **Justice versus care.** Matthews & Mullin summarise Gilligan: women and girls "are perhaps more likely to approach moral dilemmas with a “care” orientation" (SEP §3).
  Norlock reports that women in Gilligan's studies "were as likely to express the perspective of justice as they were to express a perspective that valued intimacy, responsibility, relationships, and caring for others" (SEP §2.2).
- **Justification versus deliberation.** Keefer 1993 recasts the two
  orientations as retrospective justification and prospective
  deliberation, and argues the "Why?" follow-up "encourages subjects towards a strategy of justifying the solution" (PDF p. 11).
- **Love beyond justice.** Kohlberg & Shulik: Heinz's "loving willingness" to sacrifice himself "does not settle the justice choice between the right to life of a dying person and the right to property of the druggist" (PDF p. 67).
- **The book beyond Amy and Jake.** Gilligan (2018, p. 28): the two children "appear for about five pages"; the book's two central chapters are "the abortion study".

# Just war

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Map: Seth Lazar, [SEP Fall 2024 "War"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/war/)
(first published 2016-05-03; excerpt: `raw/sep-war-fall-2024-lazar-just-war-theory.md`), who defends his own view in the entry (§§2.3, 4.2); his
assessments are his. Second map: Alexander Moseley, [IEP "Just War Theory"](https://web.archive.org/web/20260106103255/https://iep.utm.edu/justwar/)
(undated; excerpt: `raw/iep-just-war-theory-moseley-ad-bellum-in-bello-post-bellum.md`).

## The question

Lazar (preamble) frames it by two refusals: "Some reject the very idea of the “morality of war”." "Of those, some deny that morality applies at all
once the guns strike up; for others, no plausible moral theory could license the exceptional horrors of war. The first group are sometimes called
realists. The second group are pacifists. The task of just war theory is to seek a middle path between them: to justify at least some wars, but also
to limit them (Ramsey 1961)." "War, here, means large-scale armed conflicts between organised groups" (note 1). Fiala ([SEP "Pacifism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/pacifism/) §1; excerpt:
`raw/sep-pacifism-fall-2024-fiala-absolute-contingent-just-war-pacifism.md`) states the same continuum: "The fundamental question here is “can war be
justified?” (see Fiala and Kling, 2023)."

The question is split in three (Lazar §2.4): "Traditionally, just war theorists divide their enquiry into reflection on the resort to war—jus ad
bellum—and conduct in war—jus in bello. More recently, they have added an account of permissible action post-war, or jus post bellum."

- **Jus ad bellum** — "Jus ad bellum typically comprises the following six principles" (§2.5): "Just Cause: the war is an attempt to avert the right
  kind of injury." "Legitimate Authority: the war is fought by an entity that has the authority to fight such wars." "Right Intention: that entity
  intends to achieve the just cause, rather than using it as an excuse to achieve some wrongful end." "Reasonable Prospects of Success: the war is
  sufficiently likely to achieve its aims." "Proportionality: the morally weighted goods achieved by the war outweigh the morally weighted bads that
  it will cause." "Last Resort (Necessity): there is no other less harmful way to achieve the just cause." Moseley (§2) gives the same six as
  "commonly held".
- **Jus in bello** (§4.1): "Discrimination: Targeting noncombatants is impermissible." "Proportionality: Collaterally harming noncombatants (that is,
  harming them foreseeably, but unintendedly) is permissible only if the harms are proportionate to the goals the attack is intended to achieve."
  "Necessity: Collaterally harming noncombatants is permissible only if, in the pursuit of one’s military objectives, the least harmful means feasible
  are chosen."
- **Jus post bellum** — Lazar (§5): "This topic, jus post bellum, is addressed separately" (no such Fall 2024 SEP entry was found). Moseley (§4): "Orend presents a useful summary of the principles of jus post bellum : the principle of discrimination should be employed to avoid imposing punishment on innocents or non-combatants; the rights or traditions of the defeated deserve respect; the claims of victory should be proportional to the war’s character;  compensatory claims should be tempered by the principles of discrimination and proportionality; and, controversially, the need to rehabilitate or re-educate an aggressor should also be considered." Lazar (§3.2) folds the aftermath into ad bellum proportionality: "Achieving your just cause is not enough. The
  aftermath of the war must also be sufficiently tolerable if the war is to be proportionate, all things considered."

## Why it matters

- **Law.** Lazar (§1): traditionalist "views on the morality of war are substantially led by international law, especially the law of armed conflict";
  "The traditionalist view received its most prominent exposition" in Walzer's 1977 book "the same year as it was decisively codified in international law, in the first additional protocol to the Geneva
  Conventions".
- **The pacifist challenge.** Lazar (preamble), his own assessment: "The real challenge to just war theory comes from pacifism. And we should
  remember, from the outset, that this challenge is real. The justified war might well be a chimera."
- **Dirty hands and emergencies.** Walzer's "supreme emergency" — "rare circumstances in which intentionally killing noncombatants is necessary to
  avert an unconscionable threat" (Lazar §4.1; Walzer 2006: 247–50 per note 18) — is the case Coady discusses under [the problem of dirty hands](dirty-hands.md) (excerpt: `raw/sep-dirty-hands-fall-2024-coady-map.md`); see also [the ticking time bomb scenario](ticking-bomb.md).
- **Self-defence and killing.** Reductivists hold "that justified wars are mere aggregates of justified acts of individual self- and other-defence
  (see Rodin 2002; McMahan 2004a)" (§2.3); structural note (G4): this links the problem to other cases of permissible killing, such as [the trolley problem](trolley-problem.md).

## Positions taken

Grouped as Lazar groups them (preamble, §§1, 4.2), with Fiala's pacifism typology; not ranked. Lazar (note 3): "These are archetypes; most theorists
are traditionalist in some respects, revisionist in others."

**1. Realism.** In Korab-Karpowicz's words ([SEP "Political Realism in International Relations"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/realism-intl-relations/), preamble; excerpt:
`raw/sep-realism-intl-relations-fall-2024-korab-karpowicz-war-and-ethics.md`), realists show "skepticism regarding the relevance of ethical norms to
relations among states"; international politics, "they sometimes claim, is a sphere without justice". *For:* the Athenians in Thucydides:
considerations of right and wrong have "never turned people aside from the opportunities of aggrandizement offered by superior strength" (1.76), and
"the independent states survive [only] when they are powerful" (5.97), as quoted by Korab-Karpowicz (§1.1.1). In his reading (preamble), classical
realists "are critical of moralism—abstract moral discourse that does not take into account political realities"; he concludes (§4): "Realism is thus
more than a static, amoral theory".
*Against:* Lazar (preamble), his assessment: "Although realism undoubtedly has its adherents, few philosophers find it compelling." He points to "a robust response to realism" in Walzer 2006, ch. 1 (note 2).

**2. Pacifism.** Fiala (§2.1): "So, absolute pacifism holds that war and violence are always wrong." "contingent pacifism is usually understood as a
principled rejection of a particular war." Some "derive a version of pacifism described as “contingent pacifism” (May 2015, for example) or “just war pacifism” (Sterba 1998)" (§1); just war pacifism "maintains that modern wars are not fought according to the
standards of the just war theory" (§2.2); Rawls: "the possibility of a just war is conceded but not under present circumstances" (1971, 382, per
§2.2). *For:* Fox (2014, 127, per §2.1): "Should immoral actions be used to stop other (perhaps gravely more) immoral actions?" — "He answers “No,”" in Fiala's report;
Holmes (2017a: xvii, per §1): the tradition ignores the "massive, systematic and deliberate killing of human beings" that occurs in war; "the burden
of proof rests on the proponent of war" (§3). Absolute pacifists reject double effect: "the killing of the innocent in war is always wrong, even if it
is an unintended effect" (§4). *Against:* Fox himself "admits" the conclusion that "even military action aimed at protecting people against acute and
systematic human-rights violations cannot be justified" (§2.1). Anscombe (1981a, 58, per §4.1): "pacifism teaches people to make no distinction
between the shedding of innocent blood and the shedding of any human blood". Luban (1980, 170, per §4): "Such rights are worth fighting for."

**3. Traditionalism (legalism).** Lazar (§1): "States (and only states)" may fight for national defence, defence of allies, or against "“crimes that
shock the moral conscience of mankind” (Walzer 2006: 107)"; in bello, "Combatant Equality: Soldiers who satisfy Discrimination, Proportionality, and
Necessity fight permissibly, regardless of what they are fighting for." *For:* Walzer: "“simply by fighting”, all combatants “have lost their title to
life and liberty” (Walzer 2006: 136)", while noncombatants are "“men and women with rights, and… they cannot be used for some military purpose, even
if it is a legitimate purpose” (Walzer 2006: 137)" (§4.1). Benbaji's contractualist argument (2008, 2011) "starts by observing that states (and their
populations) need disciplined armies for the purposes of national defence" (§4.2); Estlund (2007) grounds a duty to obey in "the epistemic authority
of the state" (§4.2); an institutional argument stresses "the importance of limiting the horrors of war, given that we know that people deceive
themselves about the justice of their cause (Shue 2008, 2010; Dill and Shue 2012; Shue 2013; Waldron 2016)" (§4.2). *Against:* "The simplest objection
against Combatant Equality brings it into conflict with Proportionality (McMahan 1994; Rodin 2002; Hurka 2005)"; "the military success of unjust
combatants does not avert evil, it is itself evil" (§4.1). "Merely posing a threat to others—even a lethal threat—is not sufficient to warrant the
loss of one’s fundamental rights, because sometimes one threatens others’ lives for very good reasons (McMahan 1994)" (§4.1). Against Benbaji, Lazar:
"it rests on contentious empirical speculation about whether soldiers in fact consent in this way"; against the institutional argument: "it doesn’t
address the same question as the act-focused arguments that preceded it" (§4.2). Rodin (2002) "popularized the “bloodless invasion objection”" to
grounding national defence in individual rights (§3.1).

**4. Revisionism.** Revisionists "question the moral standing of states and the permissibility of national defence, argue for expanded permissions for
humanitarian intervention, problematise civilian immunity, and contend that combatants fighting for wrongful aims cannot do anything right, besides
lay down their weapons" (§1). Named: "Luban 1980a", then "Holmes 1989; McMahan 1994; Norman 1995", then "Rodin 2002; McMahan 2004b; McPherson 2004; Arneson 2006; Fabre 2009; McMahan 2009; Fabre 2012" (§1), McMahan 2009 being [*Killing in War*](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199548668.001.0001) (not read). *For:* "liability is
grounded, in war as elsewhere, in one’s responsibility for contributing to a wrongful threat" (§4.1); "all and only unjust combatants are in fact
liable to be killed" (§4.2). Some drop individual responsibility for collective responsibility (Kamm 2004; Bazargan 2013, per §4.2). *Against:* "As
many have noted, this argument undermines Discrimination (McMahan 1994; Arneson 2006; Fabre 2012; Frowe 2014)" (§4.1). McMahan (2011a) replies that
"almost all noncombatants on the unjust side (unjust noncombatants) are less responsible than all unjust combatants" (§4.1); "Lazar (forthcoming-a)
suggests these arguments are unpersuasive" (§4.2).

**5. Moderate traditionalism (Lazar's own).** "Moderate traditionalists think we can avoid the realist and pacifist horns of the responsibility
dilemma only by conceding a moderate form of Combatant Equality", resting on "the principle of Moral Distinction, according to which killing civilians
is worse than killing soldiers" (§4.2). Lazar's conclusion: "A moderate Combatant Equality, then, is the likely consequence of avoiding the pacifist
horn of the responsibility dilemma"; "Nonetheless, much of the killing done by unjust combatants in war is still objectively wrong." *Against:* (none
recorded in the excerpts held; the criticisms above of positions 3 and 4 are the ones the entry records).

Other contested sub-questions recorded: legitimate authority for non-state actors (Fabre 2008; Finlay 2010; Schwenkenbecher 2013, per §3.3);
pre-emptive and preventive war (Shue and Rodin 2007, per §3.5); humanitarian intervention "were it to serve individual human rights better than
non-intervention" (Caney 2005, per §3.1). No survey of philosophers' views on war is held.

## Arguments in play

- **The responsibility dilemma** (Lazar 2010, [doi:10.1111/j.1088-4963.2010.01182.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1088-4963.2010.01182.x); not read; as
  SEP §4.1 states it): "If we set a high threshold of responsibility for liability, to ensure that noncombatants are not liable to be killed, then we
  will also exempt many combatants from liability." That "moves us towards a kind of pacifism" (May 2015). "But if we set the threshold of
  responsibility low, ensuring that all unjust combatants are liable, then many noncombatants will be liable too, thus rendering them permissible
  targets and seriously undermining Discrimination. We are torn between pacifism on the one hand, and realism on the other." Structural note (G4):
  with H = high threshold, L = low threshold, P = many combatants non-liable, N = many noncombatants liable, the form is a constructive dilemma;
  `logic.py check --premises "H | L" "H -> P" "L -> N" --conclusion "P | N"` outputs `VALID` and `matches schema: constructive dilemma`. The dispute
  is over the premises (positions 4 and 5).
- **Proportionality against combatant equality** (McMahan 1994; Rodin 2002; Hurka 2005) — above, 3.
- **The bloodless invasion objection** (Rodin 2002) — above, 3.
- **Double effect** in war and its pacifist rejection (Fiala §4) — above, 2. Argument pages: open work in [arguments](../arguments/index.md).

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Mozi and the Mohists** (Mozi "fl. ca. 430 BCE"), as Fraser reports ([SEP "Mohism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/mohism/)
  §1; excerpt: `raw/sep-mohism-fraser-and-mencius-van-norden-fall-2024-war.md`): "They condemned unprovoked military aggression and attempted to
  dissuade warlike rulers from attacking other states. Far from being pacifists, however, they saw a strong defense force as crucial to state
  survival." "Military aggression is wrong for the same reasons that theft, robbery, and murder are" ("Condemning Aggression", §2).
- **Thucydides** ("c. 460–c. 400 B.C.E.") — the Athenians at Melos, read by realists (Korab-Karpowicz §1.1).
- **Mencius** (4th c. BCE), per Van Norden ([SEP "Mencius"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/mencius/)): conquest by war is like
  "climbing a tree in search of a fish" [1A7]; on Qi's invasion of Yan he "complained that he had not encouraged the specific actions that Qi took,
  which apparently included widespread killing of noncombatants and taking spoils of war" (2B8, 1B10, 1B11, 2B9).
- **Cicero** — "His ideas concerning righteousness in war were carried further in the writings of the Christian thinkers St. Augustine and St. Thomas
  Aquinas" (Korab-Karpowicz §1.2).
- **Augustine**, *Contra Faustum* XXII.74 (Stothert tr., 1887; excerpt:
  `raw/aquinas-st-2-2-q40-a1-and-augustine-contra-faustum-22-74-city-of-god-19-7-war.md`): "The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful
  cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power, and such like"; *City of God* XIX.7 (Dods tr.) answers "the wise man
  will wage just wars" with "As if he would not all the rather lament the necessity of just wars, if he remembers that he is a man".
- **Arabic commentators, 9th–12th c.** — Moseley (§1) says they, like Augustine, "provided comments on the morality of war"; he names none (none named
  in the sources held).
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 40 a. 1 (1920 Dominican tr.): "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary. First, the
  authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged." "Secondly, a just cause is required"; "Thirdly, it is necessary that the
  belligerents should have a rightful intention". He answers the objection from Matthew 26:52, "All that take the sword shall perish with the sword."
  Moseley (§1) calls his the "most systematic exposition in the Western tradition".
- **Francisco de Vitoria** (1486–1546), *De Indis* and *De iure belli* (1539; not read), per Izbicki and Kaufmann ([SEP "School of Salamanca"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/school-salamanca/); excerpt:
  `raw/sep-school-salamanca-fall-2024-vitoria-de-iure-belli.md`): excluded "difference in religion and enlargement of empire" as just reasons; "a war
  was unjust if it caused more harm than good"; "it may not be enough if a prince believes that his war is just". The entry records "seven “unjust
  titles”" and "eight “just titles”" for war against the Amerindians and its authors judge that "Vitoria weakened his defense of the Indians by saying
  that Spain might govern them for their own benefit." **Molina** held that "both sides may believe that their cause is just".
- **Hugo Grotius** (1583–1645), *De jure belli ac pacis* (1625) II.1 (Campbell tr.; excerpt:
  `raw/grotius-1625-rights-of-war-and-peace-2-1-just-causes-campbell.md`): "where the power of law ceases, there war begins"; "The justifiable causes
  generally assigned for war are three, defence, indemnity, and punishment". Moseley (§1) lists Suárez, Pufendorf, Wolff and Vattel with him.
- **Locke**, per Moseley (§4): a people unjustly defeated has "nothing left but patience" (*Second Treatise* §177).
- **Michael Walzer**, *Just and Unjust Wars* (1977; 4th edn. 2006; not read): per Lazar (§1) "extraordinarily influential among philosophers,
  political scientists, international lawyers, and military practitioners"; the traditionalist statement (above, 3).
- **[Anscombe](../thinkers/anscombe.md)** (1981), **Rawls** (1971), **Holmes** (1989, 2017), **Sterba** (1998), **Fox** (2014), **May** (2015) — above, 2 and Arguments.
- **McMahan** (1994, 2009, 2011), **Rodin** (2002), **Fabre** (2012), **Frowe** (2014) — above, 4; **Benbaji** (2008, 2011), **Shue** (2008–2013),
  **Estlund** (2007) — above, 3; **Lazar** (2010, 2015) — above, 5.

## Framings and reframings

- **Historical vs. analytical.** Lazar (§2.1) "discusses only contemporary analytical philosophers working on war" and refers history to Reichberg,
  Kalmanovitz, Schwartz and Cox.
- **Reductivism vs. exceptionalism** (§2.3): "Reductivists think that killing in war must be justified by the same properties that justify killing
  outside of war"; many traditionalists reply "that there is something special about war" (Zohar 1993; Kutz 2005; Benbaji 2008; Dill and Shue 2012).
- **In bello independent of ad bellum?** Moseley (§3): the split lets one say "a nation fighting an unjust cause may still fight justly"; he adds:
  "for what does it matter, it can be asked, if a nation wages a war of aggression but does so cleanly?"
- **Victors' justice.** Moseley (§4): "It has often been remarked that justice, like history, is written by the victors."
- **Non-violence in other traditions.** Fiala (§5.1): "Hindus, Jains, and Buddhists share a concern for ahimsa or nonviolence as a basic moral
  virtue"; Alvi (2021, per §5.1) finds "a notion of nonviolent jihad" in "the idea of jihad as spiritual struggle". Islamic just war (jihad)
  jurisprudence and the Mahābhārata's rules of righteous war are not covered by any source read; left for a later source.

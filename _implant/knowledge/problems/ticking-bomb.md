# The ticking time bomb scenario

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Map: Seumas Miller, [SEP Fall 2024
"Torture"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/torture/) (rev. 2024-06-28; excerpt:
`raw/sep-torture-fall-2024-ticking-bomb.md`), who argues his own view there; his assessments are his.

## The question

Miller's version (§3.2): "A terrorist group has planted a small nuclear device with a timing mechanism in London and it is about to go off. If it does it will kill thousands and make a large part of the city uninhabitable for decades."
The captured man leads the group; "the police know that there is a reasonable chance that he will
talk, if tortured", "all their other sources of information have dried up", evacuation "cannot be
undertaken in the limited time available", and torture "is unlawful". Is torturing him permitted,
required or forbidden — and, a separate question (§4), should the law ever allow it? Miller calls
it "The classic, indeed cliché, example used to justify torture" (§3).

Earlier statements of the case:

- **Bentham, 1804**, "Means of extraction for extraordinary occasions" (UCL Bentham Papers
  74b/428–30; unpublished except three sentences), as transcribed by
  [Davies 2012](https://doi.org/10.16995/ntn.643) (excerpt:
  `raw/davies-2012-bentham-and-torture-means-of-extraction.md`): "For the purpose of rescuing from
  torture these hundred innocents, should any scruple be made of applying equal or superior
  torture, to extract the requisite information from the mouth of one criminal, who having it in
  his power to make known the place where at this time the enormity was practicing or about to be
  practiced, should refuse to do so?" Davies ([3]) lists four features it shares with the modern
  case: a harm calculus, time pressure ("await the slow formalities of justice"), independent
  verification, a rescuing motive.
- **Shue, 1978** (p. 141; [*Phil. & Pub. Aff.* 7(2): 124–143](http://links.jstor.org/sici?sici=0048-3915%28197824%297%3A2%3C124%3AT%3E2.0.CO%3B2-O);
  excerpt: `raw/shue-1978-torture-artificial-cases.md`): "There is a standard philosopher's
  example which someone always invokes: suppose a fanatic, perfectly willing to die rather than
  collaborate in the thwarting of his own scheme, has set a hidden nuclear device to explode in the
  heart of Paris."
- **Levin, 1982** (*Newsweek*; excerpt: `raw/levin-1982-the-case-for-torture.md`): a terrorist "has
  hidden an atomic bomb on Manhattan Island"; "If the only way to save those lives is to subject
  the terrorist to the most excruciating possible pain, what grounds can there be for not doing
  so? I suggest there are none."

Legal definition: UN Convention against Torture (1984), Art. 1(1) — "severe pain or suffering,
whether physical or mental," intentionally inflicted "for such purposes as obtaining from him or a
third person information or a confession", involving "a public official" ([OHCHR, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20241230203248/https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-against-torture-and-other-cruel-inhuman-or-degrading);
excerpt: `raw/un-1984-convention-against-torture-articles-1-2.md`). The case is a
[dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md) in the loose sense; whether it is a moral dilemma in the
technical sense is itself one of the positions below (6).

## Why it matters

- **Policy.** Luban (2005, p. 1440; [*Va. L. Rev.* 91: 1425–1461](https://virginialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/1425.pdf);
  excerpt: `raw/luban-2005-liberalism-torture-ticking-bomb.md`) quotes the Schlesinger Report on
  Abu Ghraib: "“[f]or the U.S., most cases for permitting harsh treatment of detainees on moral
  grounds begin with variants of the ‘ticking time-bomb’ scenario.”"
- **Absolute norms.** Luban (p. 1440): the case "is proffered against liberals who believe in an
  absolute prohibition against torture".
- **Dirty hands and moral dilemmas.** Miller (§3) presents his examples as ones "many of which might
  be regarded as instances of dirty hands (Walzer 1973 and Meisels 2015)"; Coady (SEP "Dirty Hands"
  §7; excerpt: `raw/sep-dirty-hands-fall-2024-coady-map.md`) says the ticking bomb "is basically the
  scenario Walzer uses" in 1973. See [The problem of dirty hands](dirty-hands.md).

## Positions taken

Grouped as Miller groups them (preamble, §§3–4); not ranked. For each: the case for, in its
holders' words, and the case against, in its critics'.

**1. Permissible or required (consequentialist and related).** Those who "point to so-called ticking bomb scenarios to support their case" "often adhere to some form of consequentialism"; Miller names Allhoff (2003, 2012) and Bagaric and Clarke (2007), "albeit the classic utilitarian justification remains that of Bentham (1804)".
*For:* Bentham asks whether any "pretence be made so much as to the praise of blind and vulgar
humanity, by the man who to save one criminal, should determine to abandon a hundred innocent
persons to the same fate?" Levin: torture here is "not merely permissible but morally mandatory".
Posner, as quoted by [Pallitto 2011](https://newpol.org/issue_post/torture-and-historical-memory/):
"only the most doctrinaire civil libertarians [...] deny that if the stakes are high enough,
torture is permissible" (Posner's chapter in [Levinson ed. 2004, pp. 291–298](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195172898.003.0016)
not read; [Gardner](https://web.archive.org/web/20260606161211/https://www.dangardner.ca/article/is-torture-always-wrong)
quotes other wording; excerpt: `raw/posner-2004-torture-terrorism-interrogation-as-quoted.md`).
*Against:* Miller (§3): "A standard objection to this kind of appeal to consequentialism is that it licenses far too much: torture of a few innocent victims may well be justified, on this account, if it provides intense pleasure for a much larger number of sadists."
Levin states the extension himself: "Once you concede that torture is justified in extreme cases,
you have admitted that the decision to use torture is a matter of balancing innocent lives
against the means needed to save them."

**2. Never permissible (absolutism).** Miller (§3) lists Arrigo (2004), Davis (2005), Luban (2005), Juratowitch
(2008), Mayerfield (2008), Brecher (2008), Matthews (2008), Shue (2016) as having "argued that
torture can never be morally justified". The legal counterpart, CAT Art. 2(2): "No
exceptional circumstances whatsoever, whether a state of war or a threat of war, internal
political instability or any other public emergency, may be invoked as a justification of
torture." *For:* Luban (p. 1427): ticking-bomb stories "use intuitions based on the exceptional
case to justify institutionalized practices and procedures of torture". Coady (§7, his
assessment): "it is surely plausible that our conviction that murder or torture is always wrong
has more authority than any conviction that we should always maximise the greatest happiness of
the greatest number or always maximise preference satisfaction."
*Against:* Miller (§3.2) calls absolutism "a hard view to sustain", as "being tortured is not
necessarily worse than being killed", and concludes: "It is plausible, therefore, that there are
some imaginable circumstances in which it is morally permissible to torture someone." He points
to Steinhoff 2013 and Allhoff 2012 for criticisms of Matthews and Brecher.

**3. Practically absolute.** Michael Davis (2005: 170, as quoted by Miller §3.2): "For all
practical purposes – and so, for moral agents like us – torture is absolutely morally wrong".
Holders, per Miller, "can happily accept that the offenders in putative examples should be
tortured" while calling the scenarios "entirely fanciful". *For:* Shue (1978, p. 142): "Notice how
unlike the circumstances of an actual choice about torture the philosopher's example is." *Against:* Miller (§3.2): holders "still
need to offer a principled account of the moral limits to torture", and "it is by no means
self-evident that this kind of scenario is entirely fanciful".

**4. Permissible in a one-off case, never legal.** "originally advanced by Machan (1990) before
being argued in more detail by Miller (2005) and (2009), and later by McMahan (2008)" (preamble).
Shue (1978, p. 143): "An act of torture ought to remain illegal so that anyone who sincerely
believes such an act to be the least available evil is placed in the position of needing to
justify his or her act morally in order to defend himself or herself legally"; a judge "can
surely be expected to suspend the sentence". Miller (§4): "the sentence should be commuted to,
say, one day in prison" (his note 21 says Shue 1978: 143 makes the suggestion too). *For:* Miller
(§4): "it is just a mistake to assume that what morality requires or permits in a given situation
must be identical with what the law requires or permits". *Against:* Dershowitz (below): without a
legal route "police would torture below the radar screen of accountability".

**5. Legal by judicial warrant.** Dershowitz, ["Want to torture? Get a warrant"](https://www.sfgate.com/opinion/openforum/article/want-to-torture-get-a-warrant-2880547.php)
(*SF Chronicle*, 22 Jan 2002; excerpt: `raw/dershowitz-2002-want-to-torture-get-a-warrant.md`):
"no torture would be permitted without a "torture warrant" being issued by a judge", on "the
absolute need to obtain immediate information in order to save lives coupled with probable
cause". *For:* "The real question is not whether torture would be used -- it would -- but whether
it would be used outside of the law or within the law"; the goal is "to reduce and limit the
amount of torture". *Against:* Posner, as Gardner quotes him: "Having been regularized, the
practice will become regular." Luban (p. 1452): "Judges do not fight their culture—they reflect
it." Miller (§4): "So Dershowitz is entirely misguided in his advocacy of torture warrants."
Dershowitz states the objection himself: a precedent "would legitimize torture and make it
easier to extend its permissible use beyond the ticking bomb case". Miller's preamble says
Dershowitz "claims that torture is morally justified in some extreme emergencies"; in Gardner's
interview Dershowitz says "he's not trying to resolve whether torture is morally right in some
circumstances".

**6. Right, yet a wrong remains (dirty hands; moral remainder).** Coady reports that Walzer's 1973
paper used a leader's "need to torture a suspected terrorist" (§2); McConnell reports that Walzer
"believes that the politician faces a genuine moral dilemma" (SEP "Moral Dilemmas" §8; excerpt:
`raw/sep-moral-dilemmas-fall-2024-dirty-hands-and-moral-residue.md`). Against it, Coady (§7):
"For the utilitarian, (as indeed for Ross) there is no real moral remainder". The full case for
and against: [dirty hands](dirty-hands.md), [moral dilemmas](moral-dilemmas.md).


**The empirical premise (efficacy), disputed.** Miller reports that opponents "typically claim
that torture does not work, since those who are tortured tell their torturers whatever they want
to hear" (preamble), and points to "Thiessen (2010) for arguments that so-called enhanced interrogation techniques do in fact work" (§3.2; Thiessen not read).
On the CIA programme, the US Senate Intelligence Committee found (2014, finding #1): "The CIA's
use of its enhanced interrogation techniques was not an effective means of acquiring intelligence
or gaining cooperation from detainees." The CIA's 2013 response concluded that its interrogations
"produced unique intelligence that helped the US disrupt plots, capture terrorists, better
understand the enemy, prevent another mass casualty attack, and save lives" (¶38), while "not
arguing in favor of the decision to use the enhanced techniques" (¶37) (excerpt, with archived
URLs: `raw/ssci-2014-and-cia-2013-interrogation-effectiveness.md`). No survey figure is recorded.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Jeremy Bentham** (MSS 1777–80, 1804) — Davies: "frequently been regarded as the father of the
  ‘ticking bomb’ argument"; the 1770s texts "are alien to widespread contemporary assumptions".
- **Michael Walzer** (1973, *Phil. & Pub. Aff.* 2: 160–180) — the case as dirty hands (Coady).
- **Henry Shue** (1978) — "I can see no way to deny the permissibility of torture in a case just like
  this" (p. 141); Coady (§7): in 2006 ("Torture in Dreamland", not read) Shue "criticises his own admission".
- **Michael Levin** (1982); **Alan Dershowitz** (2002; SEP cites *Why Terrorism Works*, 2003, ch. 4, not read);
  **Richard Posner** (2004, as quoted); **Oren Gross** (2004; per Luban's n. 46, "the catastrophic case can
  actually occur"); **David Luban** (2005; "the ticking-bomb scenario is an intellectual fraud", p. 1452);
  **Michael Davis** (2005); **Seumas Miller** (2005, 2009, SEP 2024); **Jeremy Davies** (2012) — as above.

## Framings and reframings

- **Artificial case.** Shue (1978, pp. 141–142): "there might well be one in philosophy that
  artificial cases make bad ethics. If the example is made sufficiently extraordinary, the
  conclusion that the torture is permissible is secure. But one cannot easily draw conclusions for
  ordinary cases from extraordinary ones".
- **Stipulation.** Luban (p. 1442): the scenario "cheats its way around these difficulties by
  stipulating that the bomb is there, ticking away, and that officials know it and know they have
  the man who planted it. Those conditions will seldom be met." His replacement (p. 1444): "the
  debate between the certainty of anguish and the mere possibility of learning something vital and
  saving lives."
- **Reply: real cases.** Miller (§3) calls his policing example "realistic" ("provided by a former
  police officer from his own experience") and cites "the real-life Daschner case" of 2002; Luban
  (p. 1441): "I don’t mean that the time-bomb scenario is completely unreal."
- **Thought experiment, not history.** Davies ([2]): "The historical record yields, at best, only
  very tenuous analogues to the ticking bomb scenario."

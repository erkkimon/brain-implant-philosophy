# The justification of punishment

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Maps: Antony Duff and Zachary
Hoskins, [SEP Fall 2024 "Legal Punishment"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/legal-punishment/)
(rev. 2021-12-10; excerpt: `raw/sep-legal-punishment-fall-2024-justification-map.md`), and Alec Walen,
[SEP Fall 2024 "Retributive Justice"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/justice-retributive/)
(rev. 2020-07-31; excerpt: `raw/sep-justice-retributive-fall-2024-desert-and-objections.md`). Both
entries argue views of their authors; their assessments are marked as theirs.

## The question

Duff and Hoskins (§1): "How can a practice that not only burdens those subjected to it but aims to burden them, and which conveys society’s condemnation, be justified?"
Their two features are that punishment is intended to be burdensome and that it is condemnatory;
Feinberg (1970 reprint, p. 98; [*Monist* 49(3): 397–423, 1965](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist196549326);
excerpt: `raw/feinberg-1965-expressive-function-of-punishment.md`) states the second: punishment "is a
conventional device for the expression of attitudes of resentment and indignation, and of judgments
of disapproval and reprobation".

The question is several questions. Duff and Hoskins (§1): "As Hart famously pointed out (Hart 1968: 1–27), we must distinguish at least three justificatory issues."
In their paraphrase: why have a system of punishment at all (Hart's "general justifying aim"), who
may be punished, and how much. Hart's text was not read; its first printing is verified as
[*Proc. Aristotelian Soc.* 60: 1–26, 1960](https://doi.org/10.1093/aristotelian/60.1.1), reprinted
as ch. 1 of [*Punishment and Responsibility*](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199534777.003.0001)
(records: `raw/justification-of-punishment-bibliographic-records.md`). Rawls (1955, §I;
[*Phil. Rev.* 64: 3–32](https://doi.org/10.2307/2182230); excerpt:
`raw/rawls-1955-two-concepts-of-rules-punishment-telishment.md`) draws a related line "between
justifying a practice and justifying a particular action falling under it".

## Why it matters

- **Scale and cost.** Walen (§4.5) lists the costs of penal institutions — financial ("states spent over $51 billion on corrections in 2015", attributed in the entry to the US Bureau of Justice Statistics), errors, abuse of power, collateral harm to families — and argues, as his own view, that "It is implausible that these costs can be justified simply by the importance of punishing wrongdoers as they deserve to be punished."
- **Test case for ethical theories.** Rawls (§IV): "It is surely no accident that two of the traditional test cases of utilitarianism, punishment and promises, are clear cases of practices."
  The punishing-the-innocent objection below is posed against consequentialist theories generally. Neighbouring cases: [trolley problem](trolley-problem.md),
  [ticking bomb](ticking-bomb.md), [dirty hands](dirty-hands.md).
- **Free will.** Duff and Hoskins (§5) report the free-will-sceptic challenge: "Free will scepticism holds that people’s behaviour is the product of determinism, luck, or chance, and thus that we are not morally responsible for our behaviour in the respects that would justify the ideas that those who commit crimes are blameworthy and deserve punishment (see Pereboom 2013; Caruso 2018)." See also [moral luck](moral-luck.md).

## Positions taken

Grouped as Duff and Hoskins group them (§§3–7); not ranked, no position pages yet. For each: the
case for, in its holders' words, and the case against, in its critics'. No survey of philosophers
on this question was found; none is reported.

**1. Consequentialism (deterrence, incapacitation, reform).** Bentham (1789, ch. XIII, para. II;
1823 edn, vol. II, via [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/introductiontopr02bent); excerpt:
`raw/bentham-1789-principles-ch13-cases-unmeet-for-punishment.md`): "But all punishment is mischief : all punishment in itself is evil. Upon the principle of utility, if it ought at all to be admitted, it ought only to be admitted in as far as it promises to exclude some greater evil".
Its ends (note to para. II) are reformation, "disablement", and influence on others' wills, "in which
case it is said to operate in the way of example"; punishment is ruled out (para. III) where
"groundless", "inefficacious", "unprofitable, or too expensive", or "needless". Rawls's statement of
the view (§I): "punishment is justifiable only by reference to the probable consequences of
maintaining it as one of the devices of the social order."
*For:* Duff and Hoskins (§3): "It is commonly suggested that punishment can help to reduce crime by deterring, incapacitating, or reforming potential offenders"; replies to the exclusion objection below are credited to Hoskins (2011a: 379–81) and Chiao (2018: 36).
*Against:* the punishing-the-innocent objection (next paragraph); the Kantian objection, "In Kantian terms, such punishment treats those punished as mere means to achieving some social good, rather than respecting them as ends in themselves (Kant 1797: 473; Murphy 1973)." (Duff and Hoskins §3); and "A more recent objection (Duff 2011: 75–79)" that such systems are "inappropriately exclusionary". Hegel, as quoted by Duff and Hoskins (§6): "deterrence treats ‘a man like a dog instead of with the freedom and respect due to him as a man’ (Hegel 1821: 246."

**The punishing-the-innocent objection and its owners.** E. F. Carritt (*Ethical and Political
Thinking*, 1947, p. 65, as Rawls quotes him; not read): "if some kind of very cruel crime becomes
common, and none of the criminals can be caught, it might be highly expedient, as an example, to
hang an innocent man, if a charge against him could be so framed that he were universally thought
guilty". Duff and Hoskins (§3) give the general form: "The most familiar line of objection to consequentialist penal theories contends that consequentialists would be committed to regarding manifestly unjust punishments (the punishment of those known to be innocent, for instance, or excessively harsh punishment of the guilty) to be in principle justified if they would efficiently serve the aim of crime reduction: but such punishments would be wrong, because they would be unjust"
— citing McCloskey 1957: 468–69 ([*Phil. Rev.* 66(4)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2182745), not read), Hart
1968, Ten 1987, Primoratz 1999, Boonin 2008. Replies they record:
(a) bite the bullet — "One is to argue that such ‘unjust’punishments would be justified if they would really produce the best consequences" (Smart 1973: 69–72; Bagaric and Amarasekara 2000);
(b) rules and practices — "thus we, and especially our penal officials, will do best if we think and act as if such punishments are intrinsically wrong and unjustifiable" (Rawls 1955; Hare 1981). Rawls's own version (§I): an institution of "telishment", whose officials "have authority to arrange a trial for the condemnation of an innocent man whenever they are of the opinion that doing so would be in the best interests of society"; the public "will be uncertain as to whether a convicted man has been punished or telished", and "A utilitarian justification for this institution is most unlikely."
(c) richer ends — "Another response is to argue that a richer or subtler account of the ends that the criminal law should serve will generate suitable protection against unjust punishments" (Braithwaite and Pettit 1990).
Rejoinder to (b), per Duff and Hoskins: "to which the critic will respond that this still makes the wrongness of punishing a known innocent contingent on its effects, and fails to recognise the intrinsic wrong that such punishment does" (Duff 1986; Primoratz 1999).
Retributivism faces its own version, per Walen (§3.2): "Of course, the innocent will inevitably sometimes be punished; no human system can operate flawlessly." He argues punishers who meet the right standard of proof "act permissibly, even if they unwittingly punish the innocent".

**2. Retributivism.** Duff and Hoskins (§4): "retributivist accounts contend that punishment is justified as an intrinsically appropriate, because deserved, response to wrongdoing".
Moore (1997: 87, as Walen quotes him, §3.2): "Retributivism … is the view that punishment is justified
by the desert of the offender". Kant (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 1797, AA 6:331; Hastie tr. 1887,
p. 195, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/thephilosophyofl00kantuoft); excerpt:
`raw/kant-1797-metaphysics-of-morals-6-331-333-punishment-island-hastie.md`): "Juridical Punishment can never be administered merely as a means for promoting another Good either with regard to the Criminal himself or to Civil Society, but must in all cases be imposed only because the individual on whom it is inflicted has committed a Crime."
The measure is equality (6:332): "the undeserved evil which any one commits on another, is to be
regarded as perpetrated on himself." The island (6:333): "Even if a Civil Society resolved to dissolve itself with the consent of all its members— as might be supposed in the case of a People inhabiting an island resolving to separate and scatter themselves throughout the whole world— the last Murderer lying in the prison ought to be executed before the resolution was carried out."
Kant's reason: "in order that every one may realize the desert of his deeds, and that bloodguiltiness
may not remain upon the people". Duff and Hoskins (§3) add that Kant "himself suggested that as long as
we reserve punishment only for those found guilty of crimes, then it is permissible to punish with an
eye toward potential benefits (Kant 1797: 473)". Duff and Hoskins (§4) distinguish "Positive retributivism", in which desert "provides a reason in favour of punishment", from "Negative retributivism", which provides "rather a constraint on punishment".
Variants they list: fair play — punishment "removes that unfair advantage" taken over the law-abiding
(H. Morris 1968; Murphy 1973; Dagger); and emotion-based accounts (Murphy and Hampton 1988; Moore 1997).
*For:* Walen (§1): "Many share the intuition that those who commit wrongful acts, especially serious crimes, should be punished even if punishing them would produce no other good." His case: a convicted rapist, now incapacitated, could be sent to a tropical island while filmed in prison garb to preserve deterrence; "Many share the intuition that there is still some reason to want him to be punished more harshly (see Moore 1997: 98–101)." Against consequentialism, Walen (§1): "They have difficulty explaining a core and intuitively compelling feature of retributivism, namely the widely shared sense that it is always or nearly always impermissible both to inflict punishment on those who have done no wrong and to inflict disproportionately large punishments on those who have done some wrong."
*Against:* Walen (§1) reports: "The direct intuition can be challenged with the claim that it is merely the reflection of a morally dubious psychological propensity to feel an excess of what Nietzsche, in the Genealogy of Morals, called ressentiment" — to which, he reports (§5.1), Moore replies that one should resist the "“elitist and condescending”" temptation to withhold that judgment from others. Walen's own assessments: "The core challenge for justifying retributivism, then, is justifying the claim that hard treatment is equally deserved." (§5); on fair play (§5.2), "Though influential, the problems with this argument are serious."; on Kant's measure (§3.6), "Lex talionis provides a controversial principle of proportionality". Duff and Hoskins (§4) ask of fair play "how are we to determine how great was the unfair advantage gained by a crime".

**3. Expressive and communicative accounts.** Duff and Hoskins (§5): "Perhaps the most influential version of retributivism in recent decades seeks the meaning and justification of punishment as a deserved response to crime in its expressive or communicative character."
Feinberg (p. 98): "Punishment, in short, has a symbolic significance largely missing from other
kinds of penalties"; and (p. 118) "it is social disapproval and its appropriate expression that should
fit the crime, and not hard treatment (pain) as such." Duff's version, per Duff and Hoskins (§5; Duff
2001, 2011b): "His punishment then constitutes a kind of secular penance that he is required to
undergo for his crime". *For:* Walen (§3.5), his assessment: "Insofar as retributive justifications for the hard treatment element of punishment seem inadequate—see section 5—this may be the best default position for retributivists."
*Against:* critics named by Duff and Hoskins (§5: Bickenbach 1988; Ten 1990; von Hirsch 1999; and others) argue "in particular that it cannot show penal hard treatment to be a necessary aspect of a communicative enterprise which is still to respect offenders as responsible and rational agents who must be left free to remain unpersuaded".

**4. Mixed (hybrid) accounts.** Duff and Hoskins (§6): "On Hart’s account, the compelling rationale for punishment (what he termed its ‘general justifying aim’) lies in its beneficial effects, but our pursuit of that aim must be constrained by nonconsequentialist principles"
Walen (§1) describes the form: "retributivism provides a necessary condition for punishment, but
consequentialist considerations provide the reasons to punish". Rawls (§I) assigns the two views to two
offices: "utilitarian arguments are appropriate with regard to questions about practices, while
retributive arguments fit the application of particular rules to particular cases"; in the particular
case "the law looks back, the judge looks back, the jury looks back". Duff and Hoskins (§6), their classification: "Rawls’s account (1955) has also been characterised as a hybrid view of this sort, but in fact it is a version of rule utilitarianism"
*For:* Rawls (§I): "One reconciles the two views by the time-honored device of making them apply to different situations."
*Against:* "Critics have charged that hybrid accounts are ad hoc or internally inconsistent (see Kaufman 2008: 45–49)." and "In addition, retributivists argue that hybrid views that integrate consequentialist rationales with retributivist side-constraints thereby relegate retributivism to a merely subsidiary role" (Duff and Hoskins §6). Walen (§3.3), his assessment: "negative retributivism seems to justify using people merely as a means (within retributive limits) for promoting the greater good (Duff 2001: 13)."

**5. Abolitionism and alternatives.** Duff and Hoskins (§7): "The more powerful abolitionist challenge is that punishment cannot be justified even in principle." and "Abolitionists find that the various attempted justifications of this intentionally burdensome condemnatory treatment fail, and thus that the practice is morally wrong — not merely in practice but in principle."
Classic texts they list: Christie 1977, 1981; Hulsman 1986, 1991; de Haan 1990; Bianchi 1994; against
punishment in any form, Golash 2005; Boonin 2008; Zimmerman 2011. Christie's point, per their §2: "the law ‘steals’ the conflicts which crime involves from those to whom they properly belong (Christie 1977)" ([*Brit. J. Criminol.* 17: 1–15](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.bjc.a046783), not read).
Alternatives: "Most prominently, many abolitionists look to ‘restorative justice’ as an alternative to punishment."; and "some free will sceptics (see Pereboom 2013; Caruso 2021) instead endorse incapacitating dangerous offenders on a model similar to that of public health quarantines".
*For:* Walen (§1) records that the case for retributivism over consequentialism "presupposes that punishment is justifiable (for criticism of this premise, see Golash 2005; Boonin 2008)".
*Against:* Duff and Hoskins (§7): "Advocates of restorative justice often contrast it with ‘retributive’ justice; they argue that we should look for restoration rather than retribution or punishment, and seek to repair harms caused rather than to inflict punitive suffering for wrongs done. But one might regard this as a false dichotomy (see Allais 2011; Duff 2011a)."; on quarantine models, the objection that "they cannot in principle rule out disproportionate punishment, or that they are inconsistent with respect for persons and with human dignity" (Smilansky 2011).

**The empirical premise (deterrence), for capital punishment only.** Duff and Hoskins (§3): "It is a contingent question whether punishment can satisfy these conditions, and some objections to punishment rest on the empirical claim that it cannot"
The one study read: the US National Research Council committee (2012, report brief; [NAP 13363](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/13363/dbasse_080967.pdf);
excerpt: `raw/nrc-2012-deterrence-and-the-death-penalty-report-brief.md`), reviewing US studies since
*Gregg v. Georgia* (1976): "The committee concludes that research to date is not informative about
whether capital punishment decreases, increases, or has no effect on homicide rates." It adds that it
"does not construe its conclusion that the existing studies are uninformative as favoring one side or
the other". Nothing on the deterrent effect of non-capital penalties is recorded here.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Immanuel Kant** (1797, AA 6:331–333) — punishment only for the crime; equality; the island.
- **Jeremy Bentham** (1789, ch. XIII) — punishment as an evil justified by a greater evil excluded.
- **G. W. F. Hegel** (1821, as quoted by Duff and Hoskins §6) — against deterrence.
- **E. F. Carritt** (1947, as quoted by Rawls) — the framed-innocent objection.
- **John Rawls** (1955) — practice vs. action; "telishment".
- **H. J. McCloskey** (1957, cited by Duff and Hoskins) — the innocent-punishment objection.
- **H. L. A. Hart** (1959/60; 1968) — the three questions; mixed account (per Duff and Hoskins §6).
- **Joel Feinberg** (1965) — the expressive function.
- **Herbert Morris** (1968, as quoted by Walen §5.2) — fair play; **J. J. C. Smart** (1973) — accepting the implication.
- **Nils Christie** (1977, 1981) — abolitionism; conflicts as property.
- **Michael Moore** (1997) — retributivism defined by desert; guilt as its emotional ground (per Duff and Hoskins §4).
- **R. A. Duff** (1986, 2001, 2011) — communicative retributivism; the exclusion objection.
- **David Boonin** (2008), **Deirdre Golash** (2005) — against punishment in any form.
- **Derk Pereboom** (2013), **Gregg Caruso** (2018, 2021) — free-will scepticism; quarantine model.

## Framings and reframings

- **Many questions, not one.** Hart's distinction of aim, distribution and amount (Duff and Hoskins
  §1); Rawls's practice/action distinction (§I).
- **Definition first.** Feinberg (pp. 95–98): definitions of punishment as hard treatment by an
  authority leave out the condemnatory element; he writes that "the incoherence of a familiar form of
  the retributive theory results from failure to appreciate the force of (2)" (p. 98).
- **Replace the concept.** Duff and Hoskins (§2): "Some abolitionists, however, argue that we should seek to eliminate the concept of crime from our social vocabulary"
- **Restoration as the aim of punishment.** Duff and Hoskins (§7), their own argument: "‘restoration’ is better understood, in this context, as the proper aim of punishment, not as an alternative to it"
- **Open.** Duff and Hoskins (§6): "Whether any of these is more plausible than pure consequentialist or pure retributivist alternatives is, not surprisingly, a matter of ongoing philosophical debate."

# Jim and the Indians (Williams's integrity objection)

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md); sources graded as in [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Branch: [Problems](./index.md).
Williams's essay, "A Critique of Utilitarianism", in J. J. C. Smart and Bernard Williams, *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (Cambridge UP, 1973, pp. 77–150; [doi:10.1017/CBO9780511840852](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511840852), registry record only), was not read. Williams is quoted here as the SEP entries quote him: Chappell & Smyth, "Bernard Williams" ([Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/williams-bernard/); `raw/sep-williams-bernard-fall-2024-integrity-objection.md`) and Cox, La Caze & Levine, "Integrity" ([Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/integrity/); `raw/sep-integrity-fall-2024-williams-critique-and-replies.md`). The wording of the Jim case was read in a WJEC teaching sheet (2017) that reprints it ([PDF](https://resource.download.wjec.co.uk/vtc/2015-16/15-16_18/PDFs/Unit%209/5-evaluation-integrity-and-nuclear-weapons.pdf); `raw/wjec-2017-jim-and-the-indians-teaching-sheet.md`), not checked against the book. ## The question

The case, as the WJEC sheet prints it "(From “A Critique of Utilitarianism” by Bernard Williams)": "Jim finds himself in the central square of a small South American town. Tied up against the wall are a row of twenty Indians, most terrified, a few defiant, in front of them several armed men in uniform." The captain's offer: "If Jim accepts, then as a special mark of the occasion, the other Indians will be let off. Of course, if Jim refuses, then there is no special occasion, and Pedro here will do what he was about to do when Jim arrived, and kill them all." It closes: "The men against the wall, and the other villagers understand the situation, and are obviously begging him to accept. What should he do?"

Chappell & Smyth (§4) summarise both of Williams's examples (UFA 97–99): "“Jim”, who is told by utilitarianism to murder one Amazon villager to prevent twenty being murdered, and “George”, who is told by utilitarianism to take a job making weapons of mass destruction". Cox, La Caze & Levine word George: "George has a strong commitment to pacifism, a conviction amounting to an identity-conferring commitment." If he refuses a laboratory job on "biological and chemical warfare", "it will almost certainly go to another chemist, one without George’s pacifist commitment, who will pursue the development of biological and chemical weapons more vigorously than George. Should George take the job or not?"

Williams's own verdict on Jim, as Chappell & Smyth quote it: "He even suggests, albeit rather grudgingly, that utilitarianism tells Jim (at least) to do the right thing. (UFA: 117: “…if (as I suppose) the utilitarian is right in this case…”)". The WJEC sheet instead prints, under "Williams argues that:", the sentence "In this kind of situation I just couldn’t shoot the innocent Indian despite it being the right thing to do from a utilitarian perspective."; it gives no page for it, and the two sources are reported side by side.

What Williams says the case is about (UFA 99, quoted by Chappell & Smyth §4): the point is not "“just a question of the rightness or obviousness of these answers”; “It is also a question of what sort of considerations come into finding the answer” (UFA: 99)."

The word "dilemma" is used here in the moral sense of the [vocabulary page](../vocabulary/dilemma.md); Cox, La Caze & Levine write that "A dilemma arises for George".

## Why it matters

- **Utilitarianism's picture of agency.** Cox, La Caze & Levine: "An important and influential line of argument, first developed by Bernard Williams, seeks to show that certain moral theories do not sufficiently respect the integrity of moral agents." They add that "Williams’s argument against utilitarianism is directed against a particular version of utilitarianism—act-utilitarianism."
- **Doing and allowing.** Chappell & Smyth (§3) tie the examples to "the strong doctrine of negative responsibility"; Alexander & Moore's SEP "Deontological Ethics" ([Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/ethics-deontological/), §2.2; `raw/sep-ethics-deontological-fall-2024-williams-villagers.md`) cite Williams 1973 for the claim that one is not "categorically forbidden to select which of a group of villagers shall be unjustly executed by another who is pursuing his own purposes".
- **Demands of morality.** Alexander & Moore (§1) report critics who "deem it a profoundly alienating and perhaps self-effacing moral theory (Williams 1973)"; see [The demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md) and [Famine, affluence, and morality](../problems/famine-affluence-and-morality.md).
- **Moral dilemmas and residue.** McConnell's SEP "Moral Dilemmas" (§6) names "Williams 1965 and Marcus 1980" as "The best known proponents of arguments for dilemmas that appeal to moral residue"; see [Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md).

## Positions taken

Grouped by the question each answers; order is not rank.

**What Jim should do.**

- **Kill the one (the act-utilitarian answer).** Chappell & Smyth (§4) report Williams himself supposing "the utilitarian is right in this case" (UFA 117, quoted above). Case for, in the terms Cox, La Caze & Levine use for George: "The most likely act-utilitarian conclusion here is that George should accept the job." Case against: the WJEC sheet's sentence attributed to Williams, "I just couldn’t shoot the innocent Indian" (quoted in full above). Alexander & Moore (§2.2) report, citing Williams 1973, the deontological line on which one is not "categorically forbidden to select which of a group of villagers shall be unjustly executed by another who is pursuing his own purposes".
- **Refuse.** No read source reports a named author holding that Jim must refuse. Case for: the WJEC sheet's gloss, "Integrity means there are some lines I cannot cross despite the reasonable case for so doing." Case against: Williams's "if (as I suppose) the utilitarian is right in this case" (UFA 117, via Chappell & Smyth); and Chappell & Smyth (§4) deny that Williams holds that Jim will "regard these actions as the sort of things that come under the ban of some absolute prohibition that holds (in Anscombe’s famous phrase) whatever the consequences".

**What the case shows about utilitarianism.**

- **The integrity objection (Williams).** Chappell & Smyth (§4): "In a slogan, the integrity objection is this: agency is always some particular person’s agency; or to put it another way, there is no such thing as impartial agency, in the sense of impartiality that utilitarianism requires." Williams (UFA 116–117, as they quote him): "It is to alienate him in a real sense from his actions and the source of his action in his own convictions." Case against: the three lines of reply below, and Chappell & Smyth's report (§4) that "On the issue of impartiality, it will no doubt be objected that Williams overstates his case."
- **Not a counter-example.** Chappell & Smyth (§4) argue that reading the stories as "(1) utilitarianism tells Jim to do X and George to do Y, (2) but X and Y are wrong [...] so (3) utilitarianism is false" misreads Williams, "because in fact Williams denies (2)"; they quote Williams (WME 211): "“If the stories of George and Jim have a resonance, it is not the sound of a principle being dented by an intuition”". This is their reading; the WJEC sheet's gloss ("despite it being the right thing to do") is the reading a teaching source prints.

**The replies**, in Cox, La Caze & Levine's grouping ("three main lines of response"):

- **Weaken impartiality (Scheffler).** "One way to do this is by watering down the impartiality of utilitarian theory, explicitly factoring in the permissibility of giving extra weight to one’s own personal projects, commitments, and so on." Owner: "Scheffler 1993" (*The Rejection of Consequentialism*, rev. ed.; [doi:10.1093/0198235119.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0198235119.001.0001), registry only). Case against, per the same entry: "Harris 1989a and 1989b for criticism of the adequacy of this response".
- **Criterion, not decision procedure (Railton and others).** Cox et al.: "one might subscribe to an act-utilitarian account of morally correct action whilst not demanding that someone like George approach life by deliberating in strictly utilitarian ways" (citing "Driver 2012, Moore 2007, Railton 1986"; critics "Cox 2005, Howard-Snyder 1997, and Harcourt 1998"). Sinnott-Armstrong's SEP "Consequentialism" (§7; `raw/sep-consequentialism-fall-2024-criterion-not-decision-procedure.md`): "the principle of utility is intended as only a criterion of right and not as a decision procedure, so utilitarianism does not imply that people ought to calculate utilities before acting (Railton 1984)." Case against: Chappell & Smyth (§4) report that "Williams sees an incoherence at the very heart of the project of indirect utilitarianism", and quote UFA 134: "“utilitarianism’s fate is to usher itself from the scene”".
- **Integrity may be sacrificed (Ashford).** "A second possible line of response to the argument is to deny the presupposition of Williams’s argument that it is absurd for a moral theory to undermine integrity." Owner: "Ashford 2000" (J. Phil. 97(8): 421–439, [doi:10.5840/jphil200097834](https://doi.org/10.5840/jphil200097834), registry only). Case against: Williams's claim, quoted by Chappell & Smyth (§4), that "It is absurd to demand of such a man, when the sums come in from the utility network which the projects of others have in part determined, that he should just step aside from his own project and decision and acknowledge the decision which utilitarian calculation requires."
- **A utilitarian keeps integrity (Carr, Trianosky, Blustein).** Cox et al. call it "The third, and most influential, line of response": "Were George a utilitarian, he would not have been acting against his convictions by taking a job in the chemical weapons factory." Owners: "Carr 1976, Trianosky 1986 and Blustein 1991" (Carr: Ethics 86(3): 241–246, [doi:10.1086/291997](https://doi.org/10.1086/291997), registry only). Case against, per Cox et al.: "The matter is not finally settled, however, for notice that Williams’s critique is premised on a version of the identity theory of integrity."

## Arguments in play

**Negative responsibility.** Chappell & Smyth (§3): "This explains why consequentialism has the strong doctrine of negative responsibility that leads it to what Williams regards as its fundamental absurdity." Their gloss: "Because, for the utilitarian, it can’t matter in itself whether (say) a given death is a result of what I do in that I pull the trigger, or a result of what I do in that I refuse to lie to the gunman who is looking for the person who dies, doing and allowing must be morally on a par for the utilitarian, as also must intending and foreseeing." The distinction Williams keeps, on their reading: "the distinction between my agency and other people’s."

**Why the projects cannot be weighed.** Williams (UFA 116, quoted by Chappell & Smyth §4): "“The point here is not, as utilitarians may hasten to say, that if the project or attitude is that central to his life, then to abandon it will be very disagreeable to him and great loss of utility will be involved. I have already argued in section 4 that it is not like that; on the contrary, once he is prepared to look at it like that, the argument in any serious case is over anyway” (UFA: 116)." And (UFA 100): "“the reason why utilitarianism cannot understand integrity is that it cannot coherently describe the relations between a man’s projects and his actions” (UFA: 100)." Chappell & Smyth read this against "indirect utilitarians such as Peter Railton and Amartya Sen".

**The case for the utilitarian answer.** Sinnott-Armstrong (§7) reports the criterion reply (Railton 1984, quoted above); Cox et al. report Ashford's reply that "One might even consider it a virtue of utilitarianism that it demonstrates how genuinely difficult it is to preserve one’s integrity when confronting a world of massive and easily preventable suffering." Smart's own essay in the same volume was not read; the read sources cite it only for whole-world act evaluation: "If the former is better, then the action is morally right (J.J.C. Smart 1973, 32; Feldman 1997, 17–35)" (Sinnott-Armstrong §3).

**A structural note (not a source claim).** The count in the case as the WJEC sheet gives it: twenty will be killed if Jim refuses; if he accepts, one is killed by Jim and "the other Indians will be let off", so 20 − 1 = 19 are spared. The act-utilitarian comparison is therefore 1 death against 20, on the assumption, stated in the sheet, that any attempt at resistance "will mean that all the Indians will be killed, and himself".

**Assessments by the encyclopedia authors** (attributed): Chappell & Smyth (§1) call Williams's contribution to UFA "in the present writer’s view, a tour de force of philosophical demolition"; they write that utilitarians unfazed by the "usher itself from the scene" result "continue to publish books and articles defending utilitarianism suggests that they do not really wish for the theory to play no direct role in our moral deliberations" (§4). Cox, La Caze & Levine rank the third reply (a utilitarian keeps integrity) "most influential" and judge that "The matter is not finally settled". Sinnott-Armstrong (§7): "It remains controversial, however, whether any form of consequentialism can adequately incorporate common moral intuitions about friendship."

## Framings and reframings

- **Moral residue.** McConnell (§6; `raw/sep-moral-dilemmas-fall-2024-residue-and-regret.md`) states the premise of the residue argument: "But either remorse or guilt is appropriate only if the agent properly believes that he has done something wrong (or failed to do something that he was all-things-considered required to do)." The objection he reports (McConnell 1978, Conee 1982): "An agent can appropriately experience regret even when she does not believe that she has done something wrong", so "to assume that remorse is appropriate is to assume, not argue, that the agent’s situation is genuinely dilemmatic." McConnell's examples are Sartre's student and Sophie's Choice, not Jim; applying the argument to Jim is this page's structural note. Williams's agent-regret (1981) is the notion Chappell & Smyth (§3) connect to conflicts; Williams (PS 183–4, as they quote him) holds that "one of the conflicting oughts must be totally rejected" misrepresents moral thought.
- **Dirty hands.** The political version of an agent required to do wrong for a greater good is on [The problem of dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md); the case of torturing to save many is on [The ticking bomb](../problems/ticking-bomb.md).
- **Killing one to save more.** The redirection case is on [The trolley problem](../problems/trolley-problem.md); Alexander & Moore (§2.2) list Williams's villagers beside Thomson's redirected threat as separate ways one is said not to cause a death.
- **Alienation.** Leopold reports the view that morality, or "certain conceptions of morality, are charged with embodying and encouraging alienation in the form of both a divided self, and a separation of self and world."

## Vocabulary

- [Dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md) — the moral sense in which Jim's and George's cases are read.
- **Negative responsibility** — Chappell & Smyth's term (§3) for the consequentialist doctrine quoted above.
- **Integrity** — "An agent’s integrity, in Williams’ sense, is his ability to originate actions, to further his own initiatives, purposes or concerns, and thus to be something more than a conduit for the furtherance of others’ initiatives, purposes or concerns—including, for example and in particular, those which go with the impartial view." (Chappell & Smyth §4)
- **Criterion of right / decision procedure** — the distinction in Sinnott-Armstrong §7, attributed to Railton 1984.

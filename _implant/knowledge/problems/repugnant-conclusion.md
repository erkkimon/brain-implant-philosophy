# The repugnant conclusion

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). Map: Gustaf Arrhenius, Jesper
Ryberg and Torbjörn Tännsjö, [SEP Fall 2024 "The Repugnant Conclusion"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/repugnant-conclusion/)
(rev. 2017-01-16; excerpt: `raw/sep-repugnant-conclusion-fall-2024-mere-addition-and-responses.md`).
Its evaluative sentences are the three authors' own; the entry's bibliography lists Tännsjö (1992,
1998, 2002) and Ryberg (1996a, 2004) among those who hold the conclusion should be accepted (§2.8),
and Arrhenius as author of the impossibility theorems (§2.7). Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984)
was not read; it is quoted as the entry and [Zuber et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095382082100011X) quote it.

## The question

The entry's opening: "In Derek Parfit’s original formulation the Repugnant Conclusion is stated as follows: “For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better even though its members have lives that are barely worth living” (Parfit 1984)."
Zuber et al. (2021) give the locator "Parfit 1984: 388" (ch. 17, per [Huemer 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzn079), n. 1).
The authors (preamble): "As the name indicates, Parfit finds the Repugnant Conclusion unacceptable and many philosophers agree. However, it has been surprisingly difficult to find a theory that avoids the Repugnant Conclusion without implying other equally counterintuitive conclusions."

**Route 1: total utilitarianism (§1).** Per the entry, Parfit was led to it by cases where our choices affect who will exist — the [non-identity problem](non-identity-problem.md):
"A straightforward way of capturing the No-Difference View is total utilitarianism according to which the best outcome is the one in which there would be the greatest quantity of whatever makes life worth living (Parfit 1984 p. 387). However, this view implies that any loss in the quality of lives in a population can be compensated for by a sufficient gain in the quantity of a population; that is, it leads to the Repugnant Conclusion."

**Route 2: the Mere Addition Paradox (§1).** "In population A, everybody enjoys a very high quality of life. In population A+ there is one group of people as large as the group in A and with the same high quality of life. But A+ also contains a number of people with a somewhat lower quality of life. In Parfit’s terminology A+ is generated from A by “mere addition”."
A+ is held "at least not worse" than A; B (same size as A+, equal, higher average) better than A+;
"By parity of reasoning (scenario B+ and C, C+ etc.), we end up with a population Z in which all lives have a very low positive welfare. Thus, the final conclusion is that Z is better than A, which is the Repugnant Conclusion."
The authors: "By what apparently constitute sound steps of reasoning we have arrived at an absurd conclusion. This is paradoxical."
Parfit sought a "theory X"; "However, Parfit’s own conclusion was that he had not succeeded in developing such a theory." (§1).

**Huemer's numerical version** (2008, §1.3; excerpt: `raw/huemer-2008-in-defence-of-repugnance.md`):
"World A: One million very happy people (welfare level 100). World A+: The same one million people, slightly happier (welfare level 101), plus 99 million new people with lives barely worth living (welfare level 1). World Z: The same 100 million people as in A+, but all with lives slightly better than the worse-off group in A+ (welfare level 3)."

**Arithmetic on Huemer's worlds (this implant, 2026-09-27; mathematics, not a position).**

| World | People | Total welfare | Average welfare | Total minus critical level 5 per person |
|-------|--------|---------------|-----------------|------------------------------------------|
| A  | 1,000,000   | 1,000,000 × 100 = 100,000,000 | 100 | 1,000,000 × 95 = 95,000,000 |
| A+ | 100,000,000 | 101,000,000 + 99,000,000 = 200,000,000 | 2 | 96,000,000 − 396,000,000 = −300,000,000 |
| Z  | 100,000,000 | 100,000,000 × 3 = 300,000,000 | 3 | 100,000,000 × (−2) = −200,000,000 |

By totals: Z > A+ > A. By averages: A > Z > A+. By the critical-level sum (level 5 chosen here only for
illustration): A > Z > A+. The same pair (A, Z) is ordered oppositely by the first and the other two rules.

Scope, per the entry: "The Repugnant Conclusion is a problem for all moral theories which hold that welfare at least matters when all other things are equal." (§1).

## Why it matters

- **Population ethics.** The entry (preamble) calls it "one of the cardinal challenges of modern ethics"
  in its authors' framing; Zuber et al.: "This conclusion has been the subject of several formal proofs of incompatibility in the literature (Ng 1989; Arrhenius 2000, forthcoming) and has been an enduring focus of population ethics."
- **Not only for utilitarians.** The entry (§1): those who hold "we have some obligation to make the
  world a better place" face it too (the sentence quoted at the end of The question).
- **Applied ethics.** The person-affecting move "has been especially popular in the context of medical
  ethics", naming "embryo or egg selection, pre-implantation genetic testing, assisted reproduction
  programs, abortion" (§2.3; see [abortion](abortion.md)).
- **Policy.** Zuber et al. (§3): "Many public decisions affect the world's future population."

## Positions taken

Grouped in the entry's eight categories (§2): "(1) introducing new ways of aggregating welfare into a measure of value; (2) questioning the way we can compare and measure welfare; (3) counting welfare differently depending on temporal or modal features; (4) revising the notion of a life worth living; (5) rejecting transitivity; (6) appeal to other values; (7) accepting the impossibility of a satisfactory population ethics; (8) accepting the Repugnant Conclusion."
Not ranked. Case for and against each, in the words of those the entry and the read papers name.

**1a. Average principle** (§2.1.1). *For:* "Thus in Figure 1, A is preferable to Z, i.e., the Repugnant Conclusion is avoided. In Figure 2, A is preferable to A+, i.e., the Mere Addition Paradox is blocked at the very first step."
*Against:* "Despite these advantages, average utilitarianism has not obtained much acceptance in the philosophical literature. This is due to the fact that the principle has implications generally regarded as highly counterintuitive. For instance, the principle implies that for any population consisting of very good lives there is a better population consisting of just one person leading a life at a slightly higher level of well-being (Parfit 1984 chapter 19)."
Also: "Moreover, average utilitarianism has implications very similar to the Repugnant Conclusion (see Sikora 1975; Anglin 1977)."

**1b. Variable value** — Hurka ([1983](https://doi.org/10.1086/292462)), Ng ([1989](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266267100002406)), Sider 1991 (not read; via §2.1.2 and [Arrhenius 2000](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266267100000249), n. 1, who reports that "Hurka (1983) introduced this idea"). *For:* "The idea behind this view is that the value of adding worthwhile lives to a population varies with the number of already existing lives in such a way that it has more value when the number of these lives is small than when it is large (Hurka 1983, Ng 1989; Sider 1991)."
"If the value of extra lives decreases asymptotically, then there may exist an upper limit to the total value of a population (exactly as the sum of the infinite series 1+1/2+1/4+ … has the upper limit 2). As long as population A in Fig. 1 has a total value above this limit this population will be preferable no matter how populous the low quality population Z is"
(the series sum is mathematics). *Against:* "For instance, on Ng’s explication of this view (Ng 1989) according to which the value of a population is determined by the product of the average welfare and a concave transformation of the number of lives, it follows that it can be better to add people with negative welfare rather than positive welfare to a population. More exactly, Ng’s theory implies the “Sadistic Conclusion” (Arrhenius 2000a):"
"For any number of lives with any negative welfare (e.g. tormented lives), there are situations in which it would be better to add these lives rather than some number of lives with positive welfare." The entry also derives a version of Parfit's "Absurd Conclusion".

**1c. Critical level** — Kavka 1982, Parfit 1984, Blackorby et al. 1997–2005, Broome 2004 (§2.1.3; not read). *For:* "The idea is that a person’s life contributes positively to the value of a population only if the quality of the person’s life is above a certain positive critical level." Z then has negative value (see the table).
*Against:* "An obvious challenge to critical level thinking is to make clear what constitutes the appropriate critical level. On the one hand, the level has to be high enough to avoid versions of the Repugnant Conclusion; on the other, the level has to be low enough not to rule out additions of clearly good lives (for an attempted answer to this challenge see Blackorby et al. 1997; Broome 2004). Critics have also been sceptical to the idea that the addition of lives worth living (albeit below the critical level) contribute negatively to the value of a population. Finally, it has been shown that this view leads to the Sadistic Conclusion (see above)."

**2. Superiority (lexical) views** (§2.2). "However, some theorists—including Parfit—have suggested that this axiological assumption is mistaken. (Parfit 1986, 2016; Griffin 1986; Rachels 2001; Crisp 1988, 1992; Glover 1977; Edwards 1979; Lemos 1993; Skorupski 1999; Klint Jensen 1996; Portmore 1999; Riley 1993, 1999, 2008, 2009)."
*For:* "The idea is that one type of good α can be superior to another type of good β, in the sense that any amount of α is better than any amount of β or that some amount of α is better than any amount of β."
"For instance, as Parfit has suggested, the first step from A to B involves the loss of Mozart’s music; in the move from B to C Haydn’s music is lost; in the move to D Venice is destroyed; and so on down the alphabet. All that is left in the final move to Z is “muzak and potatoes”."
*Against:* "critics have suggested that this view may still be consistent with some versions of the Repugnant Conclusion (e.g., Ryberg 1996b)."; "One can then show (Arrhenius 2005; Klint Jensen 2008) that there must be two types of days, call them α-days and β-days, such that some number of α-days is better than any number of β-days although the difference in quality between these days is only marginal."

**3. Person-affecting views** — Narveson 1967–78, Roberts 1998–2011, Heyd, Bykvist, Singer (§2.3; not read). *For:* the restriction "that an outcome can only be better (or worse) than another if it is better (or worse) for someone (Narveson 1967: Glover 1977; Temkin 1993a)."
Zuber et al. (§2, one of their "paths"): "If a life barely worth living is the best accessible life for each person in the large population then a person-affecting approach may see no reason to prefer the smaller population (Parfit 1984: 395; Roberts 2015)."
*Against* (the entry's authors): "However, if there are different people in the involved populations, such as two future populations, then the two populations are ranked as equally good or as incomparable in value, which is not a very satisfactory solution to the Repugnant Conclusion."
"Less strict interpretations of the person-affecting restriction and comparativism can to a certain extent avoid these problems, but at the price of implying the Repugnant Conclusion again (Arrhenius 2009a; Holtug 2004)."

**4. No lives of positive welfare** (§2.4). "Christoph Fehige (1998) and David Benatar (2006) have suggested that there are no lives enjoying positive welfare." Then the conclusion "is vacuously true" because its antecedent "is false of every possible population". *Against:* "However, the logic of Benatar’s theory has been challenged (Bradley 2010) and a theory of welfare that denies the possibility of lives worth living is quite counter-intuitive (Ryberg 1996a)."

**5. Rejecting transitivity** — Temkin 1987, 2012; Persson 2004; Rachels 2004 (§2.5). *For:* "However, if transitivity of “better than” is denied then the reasoning leading the Repugnant Conclusion is blocked (Temkin 1987, 2012; Persson 2004; Rachels 2004)."
*Against:* Broome's point that "transitivity seems to be part of the meaning and logic of the relation “better than” (Broome 2004)"; and "Moreover, it has been shown that the mere addition argument and the like can be reconstructed on the normative level, in terms of what one ought to choose, without an appeal to transitivity (Arrhenius 2004)." — the result is, per the entry, "a moral dilemma" (see [moral dilemmas](moral-dilemmas.md)).

**6. Other values** (§2.6). Feldman's desert-adjusted "justicism": "Fred Feldman has proposed a desert-adjusted version of utilitarianism, ‘justicism’ which he claims avoids the Repugnant Conclusion (Feldman 1997)." *Against:* "It has been pointed out, however, that Feldman’s reasoning involves a questionable interpretation of the ceteris paribus clause in the repugnant conclusion (Arrhenius 2003)."
Egalitarian and maximin replies: "Maximin ranks A+ as worse than A and thus stops the Mere Addition Paradox in its first step." *Against:* "More problematic for the egalitarian approach, however, is that the Repugnant Conclusion can be derived from conditions to which an egalitarian has no decisive objections (Tännsjö 2008; Arrhenius 2009b)."

**7. Impossibility** (§2.7). "It has been held that it can be proven that there is no population ethics that satisfies a set of apparently plausible adequacy conditions on such a theory. In fact, several such proofs have been suggested (Arrhenius 2000b, 2011)."
Arrhenius 2000 (p. 261; excerpt: `raw/arrhenius-2000-impossibility-theorem-welfarist-axiologies.md`): "There is no welfarist axiology that satisfies the Dominance, the Addition, and the Minimal Non-Extreme Priority Principle and avoids the Repugnant, the Sadistic and the Anti-Egalitarian Conclu- sion."
His assessment (p. 265): "Since it is reasonable to claim that an acceptable normative theory has to take welfare into account, the impossibility theorem cast doubts on the whole project of finding a normative theory that coheres with our considered moral beliefs."
The entry: "Arrhenius has suggested that an impossibility theorem leaves us with three options: (1) to abandon some of the conditions on which the theorem is based; (2) to become moral sceptics; or (3) to try to explain away the significance of the proofs—alternatives which do not invite an easy choice (cf. Temkin 2012)."
*Against pessimism:* Parfit, per the entry, kept "his hopes that such a theory could be found (Parfit 1984 p. 451)."; Zuber et al.'s claim 2 (below).

**8. Accept it** — Tännsjö ([2002](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0953820800003642), not read), Holtug 2004, Huemer 2008; Sikora, Mackie, Hare, Ryberg (§2.8). Two routes per the entry: "According to the first, while it is correct that the conclusion is counterintuitive, there are nevertheless certain theoretical considerations outweighing this intuition to such an extent that we should accept it."
"According to the second, the source of the unacceptability of the conclusion is the fact that we are deluded by our intuition (Sikora 1978, 2004; Mackie 1985; Tännsjö 1992, 1998, 2002; Hare 1993; Ryberg 1996a, 2004)."
*For:* Huemer (abstract): "I defend the ‘Repugnant’ Conclusion that for any possible population of happy people, a population containing a sufficient number of people with lives barely worth living would be better. Four lines of argument converge on this conclusion, and the conclusion has a simple, natural theoretical explanation. The opposition to the Repugnant Conclusion rests on a bare appeal to intuition. This intuition is open to charges of being influenced by multiple distorting factors. Several theories of population ethics have been devised to avoid the Repugnant Conclusion, but each generates even more counterintuitive consequences. The intuition opposing the Repugnant Conclusion is thus among the best candidates for an intuition that should be revised."
His explanation (§7): "Finally, we have a straightforward, natural explanation for why RC would be true, namely, that the value of lives is additive. Other things being equal, adding more of something intrinsically good makes the result just that much better. RC is a simple consequence of this."
*Against:* Arrhenius 2000 (p. 264): "The arguments that have been put forward for accepting, for example, the Repugnant Conclusion, are less than satisfactory."
The entry (§2.8) on the large-numbers reply: "(for a critical discussion of these kind of arguments, see Temkin 2012 and Pummer 2013)"; and the Very Repugnant Conclusion "(Arrhenius 2011)": "We might, for example, accept the former but not the latter because we give greater moral weight to suffering than to happiness."
Parfit's reported remark (Arrhenius 2000, p. 259 note): "In his referee report he writes that ‘if the extra people in A+ have lives that are only just worth living, most people find it easy to believe that A+ would be worse than A’."

**The 2021 joint statement (a reported event).** Twenty-nine authors (Zuber, Venkatesh, Tännsjö, … Broome, …
Asheim; full list in `raw/zuber-et-al-2021-what-should-we-agree-on-repugnant-conclusion.md`) published
"What Should We Agree on about the Repugnant Conclusion?", *Utilitas* 33(4): 379–383 (CC BY 4.0). Claim 1: "1. The fact that an approach to population ethics (an axiology or a social ordering) entails the Repugnant Conclusion is not sufficient to conclude that the approach is inadequate. Equivalently, avoiding the Repugnant Conclusion is not a necessary condition for a minimally adequate candidate axiology, social ordering, or approach to population ethics."
Claim 2: "2. The fact that the Repugnant Conclusion is implied by many plausible principles of axiology and social welfare is not a reason to doubt the existence or coherence of ethics and value theory (although we do not rule out that there may be other reasons for moral skepticism)."
Claim 3: "3. Further properties of axiologies or social orderings – beyond their avoidance of the Repugnant Conclusion – are important, should be given importance and may prove decisive."
Their limits: "We do not here endorse or oppose the Repugnant Conclusion, nor total utilitarianism, nor indeed any specific approach." And: "About what follows, we disagree with one another."
No published reply to the statement was read for this page; none is recorded here. No survey figure is recorded.

## Framings and reframings

- **The intuition is unreliable.** Huemer (§2.4): "In sum, there are several reasons to distrust the Unrepugnant Intuition: this intuition may be produced by a bias towards the world we would prefer to live in, a difficulty in grasping large numbers"; Zuber et al. (§2): "The intuition that the Repugnant Conclusion is repugnant may be unreliable."
- **Practically idle.** Zuber et al. (§2): "An approach that entails the Repugnant Conclusion need not entail any repugnant recommendations in practice." Huemer (§8): "Given any plausible view about the actual effects of population growth, the Total Utility Principle supports no such conclusion."
- **Ill-defined.** Zuber et al. (§2): "If there is no logically prior concept of a life worth living, then the Repugnant Conclusion may be ill-defined."
- **Too much focus.** Zuber et al.: "We believe, however, that the Repugnant Conclusion now receives too much focus." The entry's authors instead call it "one of the cardinal challenges of modern ethics" (preamble).
- **A harder case.** The Very Repugnant Conclusion (§2.8, Arrhenius 2011): adding "any very negative welfare" lives.
- **Related problems.** [Non-identity problem](non-identity-problem.md); [abortion](abortion.md);
  [moral dilemmas](moral-dilemmas.md); [demandingness objection](demandingness-objection.md).

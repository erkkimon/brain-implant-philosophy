# The problem of dirty hands

## The question

Coady's statement (SEP, preamble): "Should political leaders violate the deepest constraints of morality in order to achieve great goods or avoid disasters for their communities? This question poses what has become known amongst philosophers as the problem of dirty hands."

What the versions share, per Coady: "All, however, involve the idea that correct political action must sometimes conflict with profound moral norms."

The name: Coady (§1) reports that Michael Walzer "coined the term “dirty hands” adapting it from Jean Paul Sartre’s play of the same name (Walzer 1973)"; McConnell (§8): "The idea is that no one can rule without becoming morally tainted." The play's date differs between the two SEP entries: McConnell gives Sartre (1946); Reynolds' SEP "Sartre" gives *Les mains sales*, "First produced 1948", translated 1949. Sartre's text was not read; the play is cited here only through these entries.

Walzer's two 1973 examples, as Coady reports them (§2): a leader's "need to torture a suspected terrorist in the hope of preventing the likely killing of hundreds of innocent people", and "a good democratic politician bribing a corrupt ward boss to deliver him votes with the promise of improperly delivered school construction contracts". Walzer's own verdict on the politician (1973, 166, quoted by Coady §6): "We know he is doing right when he makes the deal because he knows he is doing wrong".

## Why it matters

- **Coherence of moral judgement.** Coady (§3): "The advocate of dirty hands says in effect that it is sometimes right to do what is wrong, and this seems tantamount to saying that some act is both wrong and not wrong." Walzer later called his own position "provocative and paradoxical" (2004a, 33, per Coady §3).
- **Moral dilemmas.** McConnell (§8): "For opponents of moral dilemmas, the problem of dirty hands represents both a challenge and an opportunity." See [Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md).
- **War and torture.** Walzer's "supreme emergency" was argued to "justify the Allied terror bombing of German cities in the early stages of World War II (Walzer 1977a, 267–68)" (Coady §1); the torture example is, per Coady (§7), "basically" the ticking bomb scenario (see [The ticking bomb](../problems/ticking-bomb.md)).
- **Democratic responsibility.** Whether citizens share the dirt (Coady §6; below).

Coady (§2) lists five issues: whether the scenario "makes any sense at all"; whether the overriding "takes place within morality or somehow beyond it"; whether it is "necessitated only or primarily by politics"; "how are the circumstances that call for dirty hands best described?"; and its relation "to that of moral dilemmas and the requirements of some form of moral absolutism".

## Positions taken

Grouped by the question each answers; order is not rank.

**The apparent contradiction.** Coady (§3) describes three ways of removing it and one of accepting it:

- **Genuine dirty hands (moral remainder).** Coady (§1) calls them "the rather more reluctant advocates of “dirty hands” who think that some very good ends, such as the aversion of catastrophe, require the doing of evil, but [...] they insist on calling it evil, though necessary evil." Walzer 1973 is the reference statement. McConnell (§8) reads Walzer as holding that "the politician faces a genuine moral dilemma, though, strangely, he also thinks that the politician should choose the good of the community" ("strangely" is McConnell's word). Stocker (2000, per Coady §5) "thinks there is no logical incoherence in the dirty hands story": "double-counting, i.e., giving moral weight to impossible oughts, and allowing for non-action-guiding act evaluations" occurs "“across the board” of moral thinking". Williams' 1978 list of the politician's "disagreeable or distasteful" acts is quoted under Thinkers.
- **No dilemma: choose the lesser evil.** Kai Nielsen (spelled "Nielson" in Coady) calls dirty hands "a conceptual confusion with unfortunate moral residues" (Nielson 2000, 140, per Coady §3); he "disavows utilitarianism and calls himself a “weak consequentialist”"; the agent will "“feel guilty” but feeling guilty is not to be confused with being guilty". Coady adds that "such a dismissal would come naturally to a utilitarian or most consequentialists", and (§7): "For the utilitarian, (as indeed for Ross) there is no real moral remainder, though there may be some room for psychological discomfort."
- **No dirty hands: some prohibitions hold.** Coady (§3): "An insistence on the inviolability of some moral prescriptions makes the paradox disappear as neatly as the Nielson manoeuvre. [...] Such a position is often called absolutist. Absolutist positions have been explicitly held by many philosophers, notably Augustine, Aquinas, and Kant". Kant's own statement is under Thinkers. Sandsmark (2021, per Coady §7) argues the reverse relation: "the moral taint that is essential to the dirty hands act of necessity is best explained by its involving a guilty violation of an absolute prohibition."
- **Morality is overridden by something else.** Coady (§3): "morality is not the only legitimate determinant of right action and that some other determinant may occasionally properly trump it"; on this reading dirty hands theorists "accept morality’s comprehensiveness [...] but reluctantly reject its dominance for one class of decisions." Coady contrasts realists such as E.H. Carr ("no ethical standards are applicable to relations between states…", Carr 1962, 153), who reject moral considerations "with no deep sense of regret or remorse".

**Within morality or beyond it.** Walzer's later account sets "“the utilitarianism of extremity”" against "“a rights normality”" (2004a, 40, per Coady §4); a second route is role morality: "the political ruler, qua political ruler, ought to do what is best for the state; that is his principal role-related obligation" (McConnell §8 on Walzer 1973).

**Justification or excuse.** Walzer, citing Austin: "an excuse is typically an admission of fault; a justification is typically a denial of fault and an assertion of innocence" (1973, 170, per Coady §4). Tamar Meisels "rejects the account in terms of justification [...] but she accepts dirty hands as providing grounds for excuse" (Meisels 2008a, 213–221, per Coady §4).

**When the hands may be dirtied.** Walzer 1973: "It is easy to get one’s hands dirty in politics and it is often right to do so" (174, per Coady §2). Walzer later: "dirty hands aren’t permissible (or necessary) when anything less than the ongoingness of the community is at stake" (2004a, 46, per Coady §1). Levy: "dirty actions are part and parcel of ordinary political life" (per Coady §2).

**Only in politics?** Walzer: "No doubt we can get our hands dirty in private life also [...] But the issue is posed most dramatically in politics" (1974, 76, per Coady §5); Stocker "rejects this restriction (Stocker 2000, 32–3)".

**Citizens' hands (democratic dirty hands).** Dennis Thompson: the politician acts "not only for us, but with our consent—not only in our name but on our principles" (Thompson 1987, 18, per Coady §6). Hollis: "When their hands get dirty, so do ours" (1996, 146–7, per Coady §6). Archard: "there has been little discussion of the putative complicity, and thus also dirtying of hands, of a democratic public" (2013, 777). de Wijze: all citizens, "even those dissenting from the decision, must “share in the moral burden, and contribute to the appropriate restitution”" (2018, 144, per Coady §6). Walzer: we cannot punish the agents "“without getting our own hands dirty, and then we must find some way of paying the price ourselves”" (1973, 180, per Coady §6); the penalty view is "criticized by, for example, Tamar Meisels and Neil Levy" (per Coady §6).

## Arguments in play

**The case for** (as its proponents put it). Machiavelli, Weber and Walzer 1977/2004a, quoted under Thinkers; Walzer: "No government can put the life of the community and all its members at risk, so long as there are actions available to it, even immoral actions, that would avoid or reduce the risk….That is what political leaders are for; that is their first task" (2004a, 42, per Coady §4). Against absolutism: "“absolutism represents, it seems to me, a refusal to think about what it means for the heavens to fall”" (Walzer 2004a, per Coady §7).

**The case against** (as its proponents put it). Nielsen's incoherence charge and the absolutist reply, above. Shue, having conceded torture *in extremis*, later "criticises his own admission [...] as a resort to “dreamland” (Shue 2006)"; Brecher "from a purely consequentialist perspective endorses the need for an absolute condemnation of torture" (per Coady §7).

**Coady's own assessments** (attributed; §§2–7): the regret/remorse criterion is "at best inconclusive"; role morality is "underpinned by general moral considerations"; Walzer's two supreme-emergency quotes "stress significantly different values that he has a tendency to conflate"; the assimilation to moral dilemma "should be resisted" because in a dilemma "morality speaks equally against both" horns while "the dirty hands choice is always dictated as the “necessary” course"; Thompson's answer "is not however supported by argument or even survey"; authorisation arguments would let a debt collector "torture the debtor"; spreading responsibility to citizens "merely adds to worries about the coherence of the concept"; "The dirty hands position might then be coherent but false"; "The moral remainder and the remorse it generates signals the tribute that dirty hands pays to moral absolutism"; and "It is difficult in the nature of the case to see what could resolve a dispute between absolutists and non-absolutists."

**A logic check (structural note, not a source claim).** Write W for "doing X is wrong", R for "doing X is right", and take Coady's gloss that right means not wrong (R -> ~W). `python3 _implant/skills/tools/logic.py check --premises "R" "W" "R -> ~W" --conclusion "W & ~W"` returns `VALID` with "premises are jointly inconsistent — argument is vacuously valid". Each pair of the three premises is consistent: the tool returns `INVALID` with counterexample rows for the conclusion `Z & ~Z` from {W, R -> ~W}, {R, R -> ~W} and {R, W}. The three responses Coady (§3) lists match the three pairs: the lesser-evil view keeps R and drops W ("feeling guilty is not [...] being guilty"); the absolutist keeps W and drops R; the "other determinant" view reads R as right all things considered and so drops R -> ~W. Stocker's "double-counting" is the reading under which all three are held in different senses.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Niccolò Machiavelli** — *The Prince* ch. XV (1513; Marriott trans., `raw/machiavelli-1513-prince-15-18-marriott.md`): "Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity." Ch. XVIII: "a wise lord cannot, nor ought he to, keep faith when such observance may be turned against him"; "it is unnecessary for a prince to have all the good qualities I have enumerated, but it is very necessary to appear to have them." Coady (§2), from Bondanella's translation: the Prince "must learn how not to be good".
- **David Hume** — *Treatise* III.ii.11 (1739–40; `raw/hume-1739-treatise-3-2-11-morals-calculated-for-princes.md`) reports the maxim "that there is a system of morals calculated for princes, much more free than that which ought to govern private persons", reading it as: "the morality of princes has the same extent, yet it has not the same force as that of private persons".
- **Immanuel Kant** — *Perpetual Peace*, Appendix I (1795; Campbell Smith trans. 1903, `raw/kant-1795-perpetual-peace-appendix-1-morals-and-politics-smith.md`): "there can be no quarrel between politics, as the practical science of right, and morals"; "I can thus imagine a moral politician [...] but I cannot conceive of a political moralist who fashions for himself such a system of ethics as may serve the interest of statesmen"; "_Fiat justitia, pereat mundus._ This saying has become proverbial, and although it savours a little of boastfulness, is also true." Coady lists Kant among explicit absolutists (§3).
- **Max Weber** — *Politik als Beruf* (1919, pp. 56–66; `raw/weber-1919-politik-als-beruf-gesinnungs-verantwortungsethik.md`): action can be "„gesinnungsethisch“ oder „veranwortungsethisch“ orientiert" [sic]; "keine Ethik der Welt kann ergeben: wann und in welchem Umfang der ethisch gute Zweck die ethisch gefährlichen Mittel und Nebenerfolge „heiligt“"; whoever takes up politics "mit diabolischen Mächten einen Pakt schließt"; yet the two ethics are "nicht absolute Gegensätze, sondern Ergänzungen". In English (Gerth & Mills, 120, quoted by Coady §2): "an abysmal contrast between conduct that follows the maxim of an ethic of ultimate ends [...] and conduct that follows the maxim of an ethic of responsibility". Coady: "Weber does not seem to hold anything like Walzer’s mature view."
- **Jean-Paul Sartre** — *Les mains sales* (1948 per Reynolds; 1946 per McConnell); source of the name only, per both SEP entries.
- **Michael Walzer** — "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands", *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 2(2): 160–180 (1973; citation from both SEP bibliographies, journal DOI not verified; reprint 1974 [doi:10.1515/9780691238234-005](https://doi.org/10.1515/9780691238234-005)); *Just and Unjust Wars* (1977); "Emergency Ethics" (2004a); "Terrorism and Just War" (2006, [doi:10.1007/s11406-006-9004-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11406-006-9004-1)): "‘Do justice though the heavens fall’ has never seemed to me a plausible moral position" (7, per Coady §7).
- **[Bernard Williams](../thinkers/williams.md)** — "Politics and Moral Character" (1978, [doi:10.1017/cbo9780511625329.004](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511625329.004)): the disagreeable includes "“lying, or at least concealment and the making of misleading statements; breaking promises; special pleading; temporary coalition with the distasteful; sacrifice of the interests of worthy persons to those of unworthy persons; and (at least if in a sufficiently important position) coercion up to blackmail”" (59, per Coady §2). Coady: most of these are "“normally morally wrong but morally permissible in certain circumstances”". McConnell (§6) names Williams 1965 and Marcus 1980 as "The best known proponents of arguments for dilemmas that appeal to moral residue".
- **Martin Hollis** — "Dirty Hands", *BJPolS* 12(4) (1982, [doi:10.1017/s0007123400003033](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007123400003033), registry only); *Reason in Action* (1996), quoted above via Coady.
- **Michael Stocker** — PPQ 67 (1986, [doi:10.1111/j.1468-0114.1986.tb00263.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0114.1986.tb00263.x), registry only); "Dirty Hands and Ordinary Life" (2000), via Coady.
- **Dennis F. Thompson** — *Political Ethics and Public Office* (Harvard UP 1987), via Coady.
- **Kai Nielsen** — "There is No Dilemma of Dirty Hands" (2000; reprinted 2007, [doi:10.1057/9780230625341_2](https://doi.org/10.1057/9780230625341_2)), via Coady.
- **Neil Levy** (2007), **Tamar Meisels** (2008), **David Archard** (2013, [doi:10.1007/s10677-012-9387-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10677-012-9387-y)), **Rubenstein** (2014), **Stephen de Wijze** (2007, 2018, [doi:10.1093/monist/onx039](https://doi.org/10.1093/monist/onx039)), **Evan Sandsmark** (2021, [doi:10.1111/jore.12371](https://doi.org/10.1111/jore.12371)) — positions as above, via Coady.

## Framings and reframings

- **Threshold deontology.** "Using terminology of Thomas Nagel’s, dirty hands would instantiate “threshold deontology” with a very high threshold for certain action types" (Coady §7; Nagel 1979, 62, per note 7); "The idea of a utilitarian version of the threshold story was suggested by Peter Singer" (note 8).
- **Ross's residue.** Ross allows "“compunction”" at an overruled prima facie duty "but “not indeed shame or repentance”" (Coady, note 6).
- **Spattered hands.** Rubenstein's category, "to emphasise that the soiling is contributed by the actions of others" (2014, ch. 4, per Coady §2).
- **Extrication.** Coady (§5) names compromise, moral isolation and extrication as situations for "further exploration"; Fabre's "moral taint" on second-best justified acts is reported there.
- **A dilemma of the individual agent.** The related case of an agent offered a way to save many by killing one is on [Jim and the Indians](../problems/jim-and-the-indians.md); the case of killing to redirect harm is on [The trolley problem](../problems/trolley-problem.md).

# Famine, affluence and morality (Singer's drowning child)

Written under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md), graded as in
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md). No Stanford Encyclopedia entry is

## The question

Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(3): 229–243 (1972; volume,
issue and pages as the SEP bibliographies give them; the copy read heads itself "vol. 1, no. 1 (Spring 1972)",
Timmerman's reference list gives volume 3; no DOI for the article was found in Crossref, and the journal
page was not read). It opens: "As I write this, in November 1971, people are dying in East Bengal from lack of food, shelter, and medical care."
He begins "with the assumption that suffering and death from lack of food, shelter, and medical care are bad", then states a principle in two strengths (the copy read has no page numbers; Hurka and Timmerman cite p. 231):

- **Strong:** "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it."
- **Moderate ("qualified"):** "if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything morally significant, we ought, morally, to do it."

The case, offered as an application: "if I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning in it, I ought to wade in and pull the child out. This will mean getting my clothes muddy, but this is insignificant, while the death of the child would presumably be a very bad thing."
The principle, he says, takes "no account of proximity or distance" and "makes no distinction between cases in which I am the only person who could possibly do anything and cases in which I am just one among millions in the same position."
His conclusion: "The traditional distinction between duty and charity cannot be drawn, or at least, not in the place we normally draw it." Of money spent on clothes not needed for warmth: "On the contrary, we ought to give the money away, and it is wrong not to do so."

Timmerman's premise form ([2015, p. 204](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/anv015); excerpt:
`raw/timmerman-2015-letting-a-child-drown.md`): (1) suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and
medical care are bad; "(2) If it is in your power to prevent something bad from happening, without sacrificing anything nearly as important, it is wrong not to do so."
(3) donating prevents such suffering without sacrificing anything nearly as important; "(4) Therefore, if you do not donate to aid agencies, you are doing something wrong (Singer 1972: 231–3; Singer 2009: 15–16)."
**Structural note (this implant).** Read this way, the question is which premise, if any, to give up; the case
is an analogy offered for a principle, not a [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md) between two options.

## Why it matters

- **Demandingness.** Hurka (§1) cites it for the claim that consequentialism "says you must keep giving as long as the benefit to them is greater than the cost to you, which can be until you’ve reduced yourself to their welfare level (Singer 1972: 241)."
  See [the demandingness objection](demandingness-objection.md).
- **Supererogation.** Hurka (§2): if the pond defence works it supports "a stronger duty of aid than everyday morality recognizes, one that makes substantial contributions to charity not just generous or supererogatory but in the strict sense required."
- **Global justice.** Blake ([SEP "International Distributive Justice"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/international-justice/),
  rev. 2020; excerpt: `raw/sep-international-justice-fall-2024-pogge-poverty-and-causation.md`) (§1): "The modern period, too, has seen some sustained discussion of global wealth and poverty, most notably in Peter Singer’s discussion of the ethics of global famine relief (Singer 1972; see also Singer 2002)."
- **Impartiality.** Jollimore ([SEP "Impartiality"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/impartiality/),
  rev. 2021, §2.3; excerpt: `raw/sep-impartiality-fall-2024-singer-effective-altruism.md`) reads Singer as implying "that we are always in the situation of choosing who lives and who dies – whether or not we explicitly acknowledge the fact."
- **Reception, as reported.** Beauchamp ([SEP "The Principle of Beneficence in Applied Ethics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/principle-beneficence/),
  rev. 2019, §4; excerpt: `raw/sep-principle-beneficence-fall-2024-over-demanding.md`): "Peter Singer’s theory has been the most widely discussed account."
  Timmerman (p. 204) reports, without a survey, that "the majority of philosophers (including ethicists) reject Singer’s conclusion". No survey figure is recorded here.

## Positions taken

Not ranked. Grouped by the premise each gives up, following Timmerman's (p. 205) sorting and Hurka's §2.
For each: the case for, in its holders' words, and the case against, in its critics'.

**1. Accept the argument: affluent people are required to give (strong or moderate version).** Singer 1972;
Unger 1996; Hurka (§2) adds Kagan 1998 and Pummer 2023 among those who deny that the pond/charity differences matter.
Singer: "I should also say that the strong version seems to me to be the correct one." The moderate version was put forward "only in order to show that, even on this surely undeniable principle, a great change in our way of life is required."
*For:* on distance, "If we accept any principle of impartiality, universalizability, equality, or whatever, we cannot discriminate against someone merely because he is far away from us (or we are far away from him)."
On numbers: "One has only to ask this question to see the absurdity of the view that numbers lessen obligation."
Sinnott-Armstrong ([SEP "Consequentialism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/consequentialism/) §6; excerpt: `raw/sep-consequentialism-fall-2024-singer-unger-partiality.md`), his own assessment: "it is not obvious that their counter-utilitarian intuitions are reliable or well-grounded (Murphy 2000, chs. 1–4; cf. Mulgan 2001, Singer 2005, Greene 2013)."
*Against:* Beauchamp (§4): "Singer’s proposals have struck many as overly demanding, impracticable, and a significant departure from the demands of ordinary morality." Critics, as he reports them: "They argue that the line between the obligatory and the supererogatory has been unjustifiably erased by such a principle."
Hurka (§2): "As Singer recognizes, a defence starting from his pond case can’t support a duty as strong as the fully maximizing one he himself prefers."

**2. Reject premise 2 by the demandingness of repeated sacrifice.** Timmerman 2015; Hurka (§6) lists
"Kuper 2002; Timmerman 2015; Woollard 2015: 126; J. A. Thomson 2022" for the series of ponds.
Timmerman's case: "Lisa notices a vast space of land covered with hundreds of newly formed shallow ponds, each of which contains a small child who will drown unless someone pulls them to safety."
"For the remainder of her life, the hackers repeat their actions on a daily basis and, every day, the ponds adjacent to Lisa’s bank are filled with drowning children."
*For:* "Given the totality of the sacrifice Lisa is making, morality intuitively permits Lisa to indulge in theatre at least one time in, let’s say, the remaining 80 years of her life."
He grants the one-off rescue: "the implicit assumption is that Drowning Child is an anomalous event"; "It is much less clear that one is morally obligated to spend one’s entire life making repeated $200 sacrifices to constantly prevent children from drowning."
On reliability of intuitions: "Either our judgments in both cases are reliable and, consequently, we have reason to reject premiss two or neither is reliable and, consequently, Singer has provided no justification for premiss two."
*Against:* Timmerman states the reply himself: "To be sure, impartial consequentialists will not be convinced that premiss two is false and their theory-laden intuitions may conflict with the commonsense intuition in my Drowning Children case."
Singer 1972, on the same kind of objection: "I cannot see, though, why it should be regarded as a criticism of the position for which I have argued, rather than a criticism of our ordinary standards of behavior."
Hurka (§6) reports a middle view: "the more you’ve sacrificed or will sacrifice through time, the larger the difference between the benefits and costs of a further contribution must be for you to be required to make it now (Pummer 2023: 138–44)".

**3. The pond is a distinct duty of rescue.** "Kagan 1998; Igneski 2006"; Woollard 2015 (Hurka §2).
*For:* "More specifically, they argue that the pond case invokes a duty of immediate or emergency rescue that’s distinct from, and stronger than, any general duty of beneficence (Kagan 1998; Igneski 2006)."
The test case: "Imagine that you encounter a drowning child while on your way to make a charitable donation that will save two lives and must be made without delay." "But many think that would be wrong; your stronger duty is to save the child, which implies that it’s a distinct duty (e.g., Woollard 2015: 132, 142)."
*Against:* those who "deny that these differences, either individually or together, are morally significant" (Hurka §2, naming Singer 1972, Unger 1996, Kagan 1998: 134–35, Pummer 2023: 99–125).

**4. Distance matters morally.** Kamm ([2000, *Law and Philosophy* 19(6): 655–681](https://doi.org/10.1023/a:1006442305640);
not read; bibliographically verified) is Hurka's contrast: those who defend a strong duty "deny that this duty is weakened by physical distance (contrast Kamm 2000)". Timmerman (p. 206): "Although some deny this assumption (e.g. Kamm 1999, 2007), I will happily grant it for the sake of argument."
*For:* Kamm's argument is not reported here beyond these citations (not read). *Against:* Singer: "There would seem, therefore, to be no possible justification for discriminating on geographical grounds."

**5. Partiality and special obligations.** Jollimore (§2.3): "Many have resisted the extreme position defended by Godwin, Singer, etc. If nothing else, such positions are quite clearly incompatible with the apparent existence of morally admirable partiality."
*For:* critics reported by Sinnott-Armstrong (§6) "hold that friendship requires us to do certain favors for friends without weighing our friends’ welfare impartially against the welfare of strangers"; Williams's "one thought too many" (1981). In the Postscript Singer reports Arthur's view "that we are morally entitled to give greater weight to our own interests and purposes simply because they are our own".
*Against:* Singer, same passage: "In either case, I would argue against Arthur's view"; he allows that "it may be better for everyone if we recognize that each of us will be primarily responsible for running our own lives and only secondarily responsible for others", calling this a secondary principle. Sinnott-Armstrong (§6): consequentialists "can also allow the special perspective of a friend or spouse to be reflected in agent-relative value assessments (Sen 1982, Broome 1991, Portmore 2001, 2003) or probability assessments (Jackson 1991)", adding: "It remains controversial, however, whether any form of consequentialism can adequately incorporate common moral intuitions about friendship."

**6. Keep the duty/charity line where it is (Urmson, Sidgwick).** As Singer reports it: "J. O. Urmson suggests that the imperatives of duty, which tell us what we must do, as distinct from what it would be good to do but not wrong not to do, function so as to prohibit behavior that is intolerable if men are to live together in society." And: "It has been argued by some writers, among them Sidgwick and Urmson, that we need to have a basic moral code which is not too far beyond the capacities of the ordinary man, for otherwise there will be a general breakdown of compliance with the moral code."
*Against:* Singer: "If this is an explanation of our common distinction between duty and supererogation, however, it is not a justification of it."; the compliance question "would seem to be an empirical question, although a very difficult one." (Urmson and Sidgwick not read here.)

**7. Fair share (cooperative principle).** Liam Murphy, as Beauchamp (§4) reports him: "An individual is only required to aid others beneficently at the level that would produce the best consequences if all in society were to give their fair share."
*For and against,* both Beauchamp's own assessment: "Murphy’s cooperative principle is intuitively attractive, but it may not have the necessary moral punch to address issues such as global poverty." And: "This is not to say that Singer’s approach is superior. The more demanding a principle is, the less likely are people to comply with its demands."
Hurka (§5) notes that cost-based views, "Unlike co-operative views", require a rescuer to pay even when another declines to share the cost. Singer 1972 on the related numbers argument: see Arguments in play.

**8. From beneficence to harm: institutional responsibility (Pogge).** Blake (§4.2): "Pogge’s recent work (see, especially, 2002) has argued that the institutions we have built in the world are, in fact, directly violating the rights of the poor." Poverty is then "not simply a fact in the world, to be dealt with by comparatively weak moral notions of humanitarian benefit and charity; it is, instead, a violation of the rights of the poor." "It is a violation of their negative rights to be free from harm."
*For:* the mechanism Blake reports: "One significant mechanism for this violation is the ascription of the resource and borrowing privilege to any organization able to take effective political control of a territorial jurisdiction." Blake's own assessment: "Pogge’s analysis is powerful, and includes a number of valuable positive suggestions about how we might begin to rectify these defects".
*Against:* "Alan Patten and Mathias Risse have each provided a version of this critique" of the causal claim; "Cohen’s conclusion is that Pogge has not adequately come to terms with the empirical complexity of how poverty is to be explained, and that as a result his conclusions about international culpability for poverty are simply unmotivated." Pogge's rejoinder: "the baseline with which his analysis proceeds is one of a reasonable alternative set of arrangements that would not generate this massive human rights deficit." Timmerman (p. 205) links the two routes: "there are many ways people in affluent nations do causally contribute to the suffering of those in extreme poverty (Singer 1999; Pogge 2001; Pogge 2008)." (Pogge's books not read.)

**9. Reject premise 3 (whether giving does prevent the harm), or reject positive duties.** Timmerman (p. 205): "Those that have commonly reject premiss three (e.g. Fagelson 2009; Gomberg 2002; Schmidtz 2000: 684–9) and moral libertarians must reject premiss two (e.g. Narveson 2003; Pogge 2008)." He calls the latter's basis "the highly controversial claim that we only have a negative duty to not inflict harm on others and no positive duty to help others, even when we can do so at little or no cost to ourselves." (His characterisation; the works named are not read here.)
Singer's own replies to two practical objections: "It is sometimes said that overseas aid should be a government responsibility, and that therefore one ought not to give to privately run charities." — he answers that "giving privately is not enough, and that we ought to be campaigning actively for entirely new standards for both public and private contributions to famine relief." And "until there is effective population control, relieving famine merely postpones starvation", on which his Postscript adds: "I now think that there is a serious case for saying that if a country refuses to take any steps to slow the rate of its population growth, we should not give it aid."

**Effective altruism, as the SEP describes it.** Jollimore (§2.3): "Singer’s work has given rise to the movement popularly known as ‘effective altruism,’ composed of people who attempt to dedicate their lives to doing as much practical good as possible. One popular strategy is to donate as much as possible to those charitable organizations judged to be most effective."
Heyd ([SEP "Supererogation"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/supererogation/) §5; excerpt: `raw/sep-supererogation-fall-2024-effective-altruism.md`) reports the debate: "once the bounds of duty are crossed and one’s action is supererogatory, it ought to be optimal" (Pummer 2016), against "the unqualified, non-consequentialist argument that one needs no excuse or exemption from avoiding entering the burning house and that optimization is not a duty" (Sinclair 2018). Beauchamp (§4) reports Singer's later proposal "that we should strive for donations of a percentage of income, such as 10 per cent". Singer's Postscript: "my view is that the aid should be of whatever type is most effective."

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Thomas Aquinas** (*ST* II-II q. 66 a. 7), quoted by Singer (1972): "whatever a man has in superabundance is owed, of natural right, to the poor for their sustenance." (tr. Dawson, 1948, per Singer's note 4).
- **Henry Sidgwick** (*Methods of Ethics*, 7th ed. 1907) and **J. O. Urmson** ("Saints and Heroes", 1958) — the duty/supererogation line, as Singer reports and cites them in his note 3 (position 6).
- **Peter Singer** (1972; Postscript to a reprint; later work per Beauchamp) — positions 1 and the 10 per cent proposal.
- **John Arthur** — "Rights and the Duty to Bring Aid", answered in Singer's Postscript: "Arthur is right to say that people could take the opposite view without being obviously irrational."
- **Peter Unger** (1996, [*Living High and Letting Die*](https://doi.org/10.1093/0195108590.001.0001); not read) — the publisher's abstract (excerpt: `raw/unger-1996-living-high-and-letting-die-abstract.md`): "a moral life is extremely costly for well‐off people given how much we efficiently may do to alleviate the distant serious suffering of others."
- **Shelly Kagan** (1989, 1998), **Violetta Igneski** (2006), **Fiona Woollard** (2015), **Theron Pummer** (2016, 2023) — positions 1–3 (Hurka; Heyd).
- **F. M. Kamm** (1999, 2000, 2007) — distance (position 4). **Liam Murphy** — fair share (position 7, as Beauchamp reports).
- **Thomas Pogge** (2002, 2008) and critics **Alan Patten** (2005), **Mathias Risse** (2005), **Joshua Cohen** (2011); **Christian Barry & Gerhard Øverland** (2016) — position 8 (Blake).
- **Andrew Kuper** (2002, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1747-7093.2002.tb00378.x)), **Travis Timmerman** (2015) — the series of ponds (position 2).

## Framings and reframings

- **A revision of moral concepts, not a neutral description.** Singer: "In arguing for this conclusion I will not, of course, claim to be morally neutral." He argues that "our moral conceptual scheme - needs to be altered".
- **Not only for utilitarians.** Postscript: "while I am myself inclined toward a utilitarian view", the aim was "to produce an argument which would appeal not only to utilitarians, but also to anyone who accepted the initial premises of the argument, which seemed to me likely to have a very wide acceptance." He concedes "the weak principle is nonetheless too weak, because it makes it too easy for the duty of benevolence to be avoided."
- **Intuitions on trial.** The OUP abstract of Unger 1996 frames the dispute as Liberationism, "which holds that moral intuitions are often unreflective of basic values", against Preservationism, "which maintains that our moral intuitions accord with our basic moral values."
- **Charity to justice.** Blake's report of Pogge (position 8) and Kuper's title, "More Than Charity: Cosmopolitan Alternatives to the ‘Singer Solution’" (2002; Hurka's bibliography; not read).
- **No agreed limit.** Beauchamp (§4): "A variety of proposals regarding the limits of beneficence have been made by philosophers, but no agreement exists on even a single general principle."
- **Related cases.** [Trolley problem](trolley-problem.md); [moral dilemmas](moral-dilemmas.md); [ticking bomb](ticking-bomb.md); [moral status of animals](moral-status-of-animals.md).

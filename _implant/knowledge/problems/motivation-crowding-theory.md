## The question

Wikipedia's list entry: "Motivation crowding theory: Adding incentives for some behavior can sometimes backfire and actually result in less of that behavior." (List of paradoxes, rev. 1376699902).
Frey & Jegen's statement: "The Motivation Crowding Effect suggests that external intervention via monetary incentives or punishments may undermine, and under different identifiable conditions strengthen, intrinsic motivation." (abstract, 2001).
The term the question depends on is *intrinsic motivation*, which Frey & Jegen give in Deci's words: "Following Deci (1971, p. 105), “one is said to be intrinsically motivated to perform an activity when one receives no apparent reward except the activity itself”." (§2, p. 4; Deci 1971 is [doi:10.1037/h0030644](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0030644), *JPSP* 18(1), 105–115 — checked bibliographically; the scan has no text layer and was not read here).
Gneezy, Meier & Rey-Biel give the mechanism in two effects: "Monetary incentives have two kinds of effects: the standard direct price effect, which makes the incentivized behavior more attractive, and an indirect psychological effect. In some cases, the psychological effect works in an opposite direction to the price effect and can crowd out the incentivized behavior." (*JEP* 25(4), 2011, p. 192, [doi:10.1257/jep.25.4.191](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.25.4.191); excerpt: `raw/gneezy-meier-rey-biel-2011-when-and-why-incentives-dont-work.md`).
Frey & Jegen on how the two combine: "(c) In general, both the relative price effect and the crowding-out effect are active, so that external intervention has two opposite effects on the agent's performance. Whether intervening is beneficial from the principal's point of view depends on the relative size of the two countervailing effects." (§2.1.2, p. 6).

**Propositional check (this implant, 2026-10-09; logic, not a position).**
Gneezy & Rustichini state the prediction they test as conditional on a ceteris-paribus clause: "The deterrence hypothesis predicts that the introduction of a penalty that leaves everything else unchanged will reduce the occurrence of the behavior subject to the fine." (*J. Legal Stud.* 29(1), 2000, abstract; [doi:10.1086/468061](https://doi.org/10.1086/468061); excerpt: `raw/gneezy-rustichini-2000-a-fine-is-a-price.md`).
Let *F* = a fine is introduced, *U* = everything else is left unchanged, *R* = the fined behaviour falls.
`logic.py check --premises "(F & U) -> R" "F" "~R" --conclusion "~U"` outputs `VALID`;
`logic.py check --premises "(F & U) -> R" "F" --conclusion "R"` outputs `INVALID` with counterexample row `F=T, R=F, U=F`.
The authors draw the corresponding conclusion in words: "If this is true, the deterrence hypothesis loses its predictive strength, since the clause ‘‘everything else is left unchanged’’ might be hard to satisfy." (abstract). The tool checks only the form; whether *U* was false in the day-care centres is the authors' empirical reading.

## Why it matters

- **For economic theory.** Frey & Jegen: "Arguably, the „crowding-out effect“ (as it will subsequently be called), is one of the most important anomalies in economics, as it suggests the opposite of the most fundamental economic „law“, that raising monetary incentives increases supply." (§1, p. 3).
- **For blood-supply policy.** Niza, Tung & Marteau report: "In 1997, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that all blood donations should come from unpaid voluntary donors." (*Health Psychology* 32(9), 2013, introduction; [doi:10.1037/a0032740](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032740), CC BY; excerpt: `raw/niza-tung-marteau-2013-incentivizing-blood-donation-titmuss.md`), and they connect Titmuss's work with the later crowding literature: "Despite the paucity of evidence, Titmuss’ manifesto for altruistic blood donation marked the start of discussions about perverse effects of incentivizing behavior that became known as motivational crowding-out (Promberger &amp; Marteau, this issue)." (introduction; "&amp;" as in the source XML).
- **For law and deterrence.** Gneezy & Rustichini set their study against the deterrence hypothesis of legal and criminal studies (abstract and §I).
- **For incentive design generally.** Gneezy, Meier & Rey-Biel: "Of course the crowding-out evidence discussed does not mean that using incentives to obtain behavioral changes will always be counterproductive." (p. 194).

## Positions taken

No grouping of positions was found in the sources read; the families below follow the lines along which the sources themselves disagree, listed by owner, unranked.

**On whether the effect exists (the psychology meta-analyses).** The 1999 comment by Lepper, Henderlong & Gingras describes the reviews as having "offered dramatically opposed bottom-line conclusions" (abstract, below). Abstracts in `raw/reward-intrinsic-motivation-meta-analyses-1994-1999-abstracts.md`.
- **Cameron & Pierce (1994): overall, no decrease.** "The main meta-analysis included 96 experimental studies that used between-groups designs to compare rewarded subjects to nonrewarded controls on four measures of intrinsic motivation. Results indicate that, overall, reward does not decrease intrinsic motivation." and "Under this condition, there is a minimal negative effect on intrinsic motivation as measured by time spent on task following the removal of reward." (*Rev. Educ. Res.* 64(3), 363–423, abstract; [doi:10.3102/00346543064003363](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064003363)); the condition is "when expected tangible rewards are given to individuals simply for doing a task."
- **Eisenberger & Cameron (1996): restricted, avoidable conditions.** "An analysis of a quarter century of research on intrinsic task interest and creativity revealed, however, that (a) detrimental effects of reward occur under highly restricted, easily avoidable conditions" (*Am. Psychol.* 51(11), 1153–1166, abstract; [doi:10.1037/0003-066x.51.11.1153](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066x.51.11.1153)).
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999): undermining for expected tangible rewards, enhancement for positive feedback.** "A meta-analysis of 128 studies examined the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. As predicted, engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, and -0.28, respectively), as did all rewards, all tangible rewards, and all expected rewards." and "Positive feedback enhanced both free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31)." (*Psychol. Bull.* 125(6), 627–668, abstract; [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)).
- **Lepper, Henderlong & Gingras (1999 comment).** "In this comment, the authors examine differences among these 3 reviews and conclude that the findings of this literature have been more accurately captured by the reviews of Deci et al. and Tang and Hall (1995) than by that of Cameron and Pierce (1994)." (abstract; [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.669](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.669)). Eisenberger, Pierce & Cameron replied in the same issue ([doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.677](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.677)), and Deci, Koestner & Ryan answered both under the title "The undermining effect is a reality after all" ([doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.692](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.692)); neither text was read here.
- *How Frey & Jegen report the dispute (their assessment, as a party to it):* "As a consequence, these findings were challenged by studies defending the behavioralistic psychological position, such as Cameron and Pierce (1994) and Eisenberger and Cameron (1996), on the basis of their own meta-analyses of studies published in the period 1971-1991 (the two analyses use a virtually identical set of studies)." and "The extensive meta-study by Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) today is the best available survey of the ‘hidden cost’ phenomenon in social psychology." (§3.2.1, p. 11). Reporting Deci et al.'s criticisms: "Cameron and Pierce (1994), for example, omit nearly 20 percent of the relevant studies, supposedly because of being outliers, use inappropriate control groups, or even misclassify several studies." (p. 11). On common ground: "But even the most critical opponents of cognitive evaluation theory acknowledge that there are conditions where rewards may prove detrimental to intrinsic motivation." (p. 12). Cameron and Pierce's own answer to these criticisms was not read here.

**On whether the effect matters in economic settings.**
- **Sceptical (Prendergast 1999, as quoted by Frey & Jegen):** "Such scepticism has, for instance, been raised in the survey of „Incentives in the Firm“ by Prendergast (1999, p. 18): “ While this idea [crowding-out, the authors] holds some intuitive appeal, it should be noted that there is little conclusive empirical evidence (particularly in workplace settings) of these influences“." (§1, p. 3).
- **Empirically relevant, not always dominant (Frey & Jegen 2001):** "Crowding effects thus are an empirically relevant phenomenon. But it does, of course, not always prevail over the traditional relative price effect." (§4, p. 23).
- **Wikipedia's article** reports that "consensus seems to have emerged" on conditions for crowding out, citing Gneezy, Meier & Rey-Biel 2011; its own editors have tagged that sentence "Dubious consensus statement" (June 2022), and the article adds that "However, not all workplace incentives show evidence of crowding out." (rev. 1373725816, "Debate and meta-analyses", "Applications").

**On blood donation (Titmuss's case).**
- **Payment would reduce donation (Titmuss 1970, as reported).** Frey & Jegen: "Thirty years ago, in his book The Gift Relationship, Titmuss (1970) argued that paying for blood undermines cherished social values and would therefore reduce or totally eliminate people′s willingness to donate blood." (§1, pp. 1–2). Gneezy et al.: Titmuss "argued that paying people to donate blood broke established social norms about voluntary contribution and could result in a reduction of the fraction of people who wish to donate." (p. 193).
- **A different reading of Titmuss (Niza, Tung & Marteau 2013):** "Objectives: Titmuss hypothesized that paying blood donors would reduce the quality of the blood donated and would be economically inefficient." and "Titmuss did not predict that blood quantity would decrease if incentives were introduced." (abstract; introduction). The book itself was not read here, so the page does not settle which report matches it.
- **Reception by economists.** Frey & Jegen: "As a consequence, two leading economists, and later Nobel-prize winners, Solow (1971) and Arrow (1972), when reviewing the book, were at a loss and could not detect any reason why increasing monetary incentives, or equivalently, the price that is paid for blood, should not increase the quantity supplied." (§1, p. 2). Niza et al.: "These arguments raised a heated discussion, particularly among economists, with criticism of Titmuss for his narrow view of market forces and lack of empirical support (Arrow, 1972; Solow, 1971)." (introduction).
- **Later evidence, as reviewers report it.** Niza et al. (seven studies, "The included studies involved 93,328 participants."): "Incentives had no impact on the likelihood of donation (OR = 1.22 CI 95% 0.91–1.63; p = .19). There was no difference between financial and nonfinancial incentives in the quantity of blood donated." and "Conclusion: The limited evidence suggests that Titmuss’ hypothesis of the economic inefficiency of incentives is correct. There is insufficient evidence to assess their likely impact on the quality of the blood provided." (abstract). Gneezy et al.: "Although recent evidence is mixed, it seems to show that noncash material incentives do not have detrimental effects on blood supply." and, on Mellström & Johannesson (2008) in Sweden, "However, the drop in blood supply is only statistically significant for women, decreasing from 52 to 30 percent when only cash incentives are offered." (p. 202).

## Arguments in play

(none recorded as separate argument pages yet). The cases the literature argues from, each as its authors or reporters give it:

- **The Haifa day-care fine (Gneezy & Rustichini 2000).** Design: "Our data include observations of 10 day-care centers over a period of 20 weeks. In the first 4 weeks we simply observed the number of parents who arrived late. At the beginning of the fifth week we introduced a fine in six of the 10 day-care centers." and "No fine was introduced in the four other day-care centers, which served as a control group." (§I, p. 3); the fine was "NIS" … "10 for a delay of 10 minutes or more." (§II.B). Finding: "We introduced a monetary fine for late-coming parents. As a result, the number of late-coming parents increased significantly. After the fine was removed no reduction occurred. We argue that penalties are usually introduced into an incomplete contract, social or private." (abstract); "The rate finally settled, at a level that was higher, and almost twice as large as the initial one." (§III, p. 7). The authors' limit: "We do not claim that the effect is similar every time a fine is introduced. It is easy to speculate that no parent would come late if a very large fine were involved." (§I, p. 3).
- **The Swiss nuclear-waste survey (Frey & Oberholzer-Gee 1997, as reported).** *AER* 87(4), 746–755 (no DOI found in Crossref; JSTOR 2951373 per OpenAlex; not read here). Frey & Jegen report: "While 50.8% of the respondents agreed to accept the nuclear waste repository without compensation, the level of acceptance dropped to 24.6% when compensation was offered." and "About one quarter of the respondents even seem to reject the facility simply because financial compensation is attached to it." (§3.3.3, p. 19), with "A corresponding effect was found for the siting of a nuclear repository in Nevada, where raising tax rebates failed to increase support (Kunreuther and Easterling 1990)." (p. 19).
- **Blood donation (Titmuss 1970; Upton 1973, as reported).** Frey & Jegen: "Though he was unable to come up with any serious empirical evidence, his thesis attracted much attention." (§1, p. 2) and "However, Titmuss‘ speculative suggestions were later supported by Upton (1973), who found systematic differences among a group of previous donors, depending on whether they were offered monetary rewards to donate again or not." (p. 2, note 1). See also the blood-donation family under Positions taken.
- **The laboratory reward experiments (Deci 1971 onward).** Frey & Jegen: "In contrast to Titmuss′ mere hunch, a great many laboratory experiments support this motivational effect" (§1, p. 2); the meta-analytic dispute over them is under Positions.

## Thinkers who addressed it

- **Richard Titmuss** (*The Gift Relationship*, 1970; reissue Policy Press 2018, ISBN 978-1-4473-4957-0) — paid versus voluntary blood donation; reported differently by Frey & Jegen and by Niza et al. (above); not read here.
- **Robert Solow** (1971) and **Kenneth Arrow** (1972) — reviewers of Titmuss, reported by Frey & Jegen (§1) and Niza et al. (introduction) as doubting that payment would lower supply.
- **Edward L. Deci** (*JPSP* 18, 1971, 105–115) — the rewards experiments and the definition of intrinsic motivation; with Koestner & Ryan, the 1999 meta-analysis.
- **Mark Lepper, David Greene, Richard Nisbett** — names for the effect: "For that reason, this effect has been termed „The Hidden Cost of Reward“ (see Lepper and Greene, 1978, for an account and extensive references), „Overjustification Hypothesis“ (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett, 1973) or „Corruption Effect“ (Deci 1975)." (Frey & Jegen §1, p. 2).
- **Judy Cameron, W. David Pierce, Robert Eisenberger** (1994, 1996, 1999) — the meta-analyses finding no overall decrease.
- **Bruno S. Frey** (*Not Just for the Money*, 1997) — Wikipedia's article says "The term "crowding out" was coined by Bruno Frey in 1997" (lead); with Oberholzer-Gee, the nuclear-waste survey; with Jegen, the 2001 survey.
- **Uri Gneezy & Aldo Rustichini** (2000) — the day-care fine and the incomplete-contract reading; with Meier and Rey-Biel (2011), the information and social-norm readings.
- **Roland Bénabou & Jean Tirole** (2000; 2006) — the signalling model, as reported by Frey & Jegen (§2.1.1) and Gneezy et al. (p. 192).
- **Claudia Niza, Burcu Tung & Theresa Marteau** (2013) — the systematic review of Titmuss's hypotheses.

## Framings and reframings

- **Preference change versus perception change.** Frey & Jegen distinguish two ways of modelling the effect: "1. A change in preferences. This is the position assumed in Crowding Theory as proposed in Frey (1997a)." and "This is e. g. the position of Bénabou and Tirole (2000), who model the influence of extrinsic factors on intrinsic motivation via their impact as signals on an agent’s state of information concerning the nature of a task, and his or her self-confidence." (§2.1.1, p. 5).
- **Self-determination and self-esteem.** Frey & Jegen's psychological account: "Impaired self-determination. When individuals perceive an external intervention as reducing their self-determination, intrinsic motivation is substituted by extrinsic control." and "External interventions crowd-in intrinsic motivation if the individuals concerned perceive it as supportive." (§2.1.2, p. 8).
- **Information and signalling.** Gneezy et al. read the nuclear-waste case so: "For example, Frey and Oberholzer-Gee (1997) show that in offering members of a community a large monetary compensation for a nuclear waste site’s presence, the principal signals that the risks involved are high, and thus community members may be less willing to accept the plant." (p. 193); and the day-care case: "One interpretation of this result is information: the parents did not initially know how important it was to arrive on time." and "The relatively small fine signaled to parents that arriving late was not that important." (p. 194).
- **Incomplete contracts and social norms.** Gneezy & Rustichini: "The introduction of the fine into this incomplete contract, however, reshapes the parents’ perception of this environment." (§I, p. 3); "So an additional norm seems to be needed: ‘‘A fine is a price.’’" and "Perhaps a third social norm is needed: ‘‘Once a commodity, always a commodity.’’" (§IV, p. 14), with their own caveat: "Overall, three norms seem necessary to explain three facts. This may not look optimal from a methodological point of view, but the real test of this explanation is in an independent verification of the fact that these norms are indeed active." (p. 14). Frey & Jegen read the same study as crowding-out: "The introduction of a monetary fine transforms the relationship between parents and teachers from a non-monetary into a monetary one." (§3.3.2, p. 18). In the extracted text of Gneezy & Rustichini 2000 the words "crowding" and "intrinsic" do not occur (grep, this implant, 2026-10-09).
- **Not the same as other "crowding-out".** Frey & Jegen separate it from interest-rate and subsidy crowding-out in monetary and public economics: "In both areas, individual preference functions, or the perceived nature of the situation, remain unaffected, indeed the effects observed are a particular manifestation of the relative price effect." (§2.2, p. 8).
- **"Paradox" or "anomaly".** Wikipedia's list files the theory among paradoxes; the word "paradox" does not occur in the Wikipedia article's wikitext at rev. 1373725816 (grep, this implant, 2026-10-09), and Frey & Jegen call the effect "one of the most important anomalies in economics" (§1, p. 3).

# Value (ethics): intrinsic and instrumental

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Value** is used here as a pointer term: the worth something is said to
have, in whichever sense a cited source gives it. **Intrinsic value** is
used in the sense Zimmerman and Bradley (SEP 2019, preamble) report as the
common one: "The intrinsic value of something is said to be the value that that thing has “in itself,” or “for its own sake,” or “as such,” or “in its own right.”"
**Extrinsic value** is its complement in their words: "Extrinsic value is value that is not intrinsic."
**Instrumental value** names value something has as a means. Where a
source splits "intrinsic" from "final" (Korsgaard 1983, below), the page
uses the source's terms and says so.

Why this definition: what has intrinsic value, whether the notion is
coherent, and whether *intrinsic* means for its own sake or in virtue of
intrinsic properties are what the sources dispute. Schroeder (SEP 2021,
§2.1.1) reports that the term "intrinsic" reflects "a common theory" and
that some therefore reserve it, calling non-instrumental value "telic" or
"final"; Zimmerman and Bradley (§3) report a "metaphysical challenge to
intrinsic value" from Dewey's pragmatism. Fixing Moore's, Korsgaard's,
Dewey's or a statute's sense as the implant's own would take a side, and
would prejudge the pages that depend on the term
([the moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md),
[moral patient](moral-patient.md)). Each sense is reported with its
holders ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

1. **Intrinsic vs extrinsic (and instrumental) value.** Zimmerman & Bradley
   (§2): "That which is intrinsically good is nonderivatively good; it is good for its own sake."
   "That which is not intrinsically good but extrinsically good is derivatively good; it is good, not (insofar as its extrinsic value is concerned) for its own sake, but for the sake of something else that is good and to which it is related in some way."
   On instrumental value (§6): "The term most often used to refer to this type of extrinsic value is “instrumental value,” although there is some dispute as to just how this term is to be employed."
   They add (§6, their assessment): "Many philosophers write as if instrumental value is the only type of extrinsic value, but that is a mistake."
   Schroeder (§2.1.1): "Paradigmatically, money is supposed to be good, but not intrinsically good: it is supposed to be good because it leads to other good things: HD TV’s and houses in desirable school districts and vanilla lattes, for example."
   and the regress he reports: "And those things, in turn, may be good only for what they lead to, but eventually, it is argued, something must be good, and not just for what it leads to."
   Schroeder also reports a third term: "Instrumental value is also sometimes contrasted with “constitutive” value." "The idea behind this distinction is that instrumental values lead causally to intrinsic values, while constitutive values amount to intrinsic values."
   Its role in ethics, as Zimmerman & Bradley (preamble) report it: "For example, according to a fundamental form of consequentialism, whether an action is morally right or wrong has exclusively to do with whether its consequences are intrinsically better than those of any other action one can perform under the circumstances."
2. **Moore's isolation test.** [Moore](../thinkers/g-e-moore.md),
   *Principia Ethica* §112: "In order to arrive at a correct decision on the first part of this question, it is necessary to consider what things are such that, if they existed by themselves, in absolute isolation, we should yet judge their existence to be good"
   (the question being "What things have intrinsic value, and in what
   degrees?"); §55 gives its purpose: "For this reason, as well as to avoid confusion between means and end, it is absolutely essential to consider each distinguishable quality, in isolation, in order to decide what value it possesses."
   Zimmerman & Bradley (§2): "In place of analysis, Moore proposes a certain kind of thought-experiment in order both to come to understand the concept better and to reach a decision about what is intrinsically good."
   They report Chisholm's development of it (§2): "He shifted from what may be called an ontological version of Moore’s isolation test—the attempt to understand the intrinsic value of a state in terms of the value that there would be if it were the only valuable state in existence—to an intentional version of that test—the attempt to understand the intrinsic value of a state in terms of the kind of attitude it would be fitting to have if one were to contemplate the valuable state as such, without reference to circumstances or consequences."
   Schroeder (§2.2) on Moore's list against Mill's: "For example, as important as he held the value of knowledge to be, Mill was committed to holding that its value is instrumental, not intrinsic." "Moore disagreed, holding that knowledge is indeed a value, but an intrinsic one, and this expanded Moore’s list of basic values."
3. **Korsgaard's two distinctions.** Korsgaard, "Two Distinctions in
   Goodness", *Philosophical Review* 92 (1983): 169–95
   ([doi:10.2307/2184924](https://doi.org/10.2307/2184924); DOI verified,
   text not read), as Zimmerman & Bradley (§3) report it: "Korsgaard notes that “intrinsic value” has traditionally been contrasted with “instrumental value” (the value that something has in virtue of being a means to an end) and claims that this approach is misleading."
   "She contends that “instrumental value” is to be contrasted with “final value,” that is, the value that something has as an end or for its own sake; however, “intrinsic value” (the value that something has in itself, that is, in virtue of its intrinsic, nonrelational properties) is to be contrasted with “extrinsic value” (the value that something has in virtue of its extrinsic, relational properties)."
   Her illustration, as they report it: "As an illustration of final value, Korsgaard suggests that gorgeously enameled frying pans are, in virtue of the role they play in our lives, good for their own sakes."
   Zimmerman & Bradley's corollary (§3, their assessment): "There is an important corollary to drawing a distinction between intrinsic value and final value (and between extrinsic value and nonfinal value), and that is that, contrary to what Korsgaard herself initially says, it may be a mistake to contrast final value with instrumental value."
   Schroeder (§2.1.1): "Consequently, sometimes the term “intrinsic” is reserved for what is good in virtue of its intrinsic properties, or for the view that goodness itself is an intrinsic property, and non-instrumental value is instead called “telic” or “final” (Korsgaard [1983])."
4. **Fitting-attitude and buck-passing accounts.** Zimmerman & Bradley
   (§2): "Such an analysis, which has come to be called “the fitting attitude analysis” of value, is supported by the mundane observation that, instead of saying that something is good, we often say that it is valuable, which itself just means that it is fitting to value the thing in question."
   Scanlon's label, as they report it: "Thomas Scanlon calls such an account of the relation between valuableness, goodness, and underlying properties a buck-passing account, since it “passes the buck” of explaining why something is such that it is fitting to value it from its goodness to some property that underlies its goodness (Scanlon 1998, pp. 95 ff.)."
   Blanshard's objection, as they report it: "Brand Blanshard [1892–1987], for example, argues that the analysis is to be rejected because, if we ask why something is such that it is fitting to value it for its own sake, the answer is that this is the case precisely because the thing in question is intrinsically good; this answer indicates that the concept of intrinsic goodness is more fundamental than that of the fittingness of some pro attitude, which is inconsistent with analyzing the former in terms of the latter (Blanshard 1961, pp. 284–86)."
   Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen's counterexample, "The Strike of the
   Demon", *Ethics* 114 (2004): 391–423
   ([doi:10.1086/381694](https://doi.org/10.1086/381694); DOI verified,
   text not read), as Zimmerman & Bradley report it: "Imagine that an evil demon wants you to value him for his own sake and threatens to cause you severe suffering unless you do."
   "It seems that you have good reason to do what he wants—it is appropriate or fitting to comply with his demand and value him for his own sake—even though he is clearly not intrinsically good (Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen 2004, pp. 402 ff.)."
   "This issue, which has come to be known as “the wrong kind of reason problem,” has attracted a great deal of attention."
   Zimmerman & Bradley (§3) also list Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen's
   "A Distinction in Value: Intrinsic and For Its Own Sake" among the
   final-value examples ([doi:10.1111/1467-9264.00064](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9264.00064);
   DOI verified, text not read).
5. **Dewey's means–ends continuum (the pragmatist challenge).**
   [Dewey](../thinkers/dewey.md), as Zimmerman & Bradley (§3) report the
   pragmatist view: "According to the pragmatist, the world is constantly changing in such a way that the solution to one problem becomes the source of another, what is an end in one context is a means in another, and thus it is a mistake to seek or offer a timeless list of intrinsic goods and evils, of ends to be achieved or avoided for their own sakes."
   Anderson (SEP, §2.5): "He rejected any conception of intrinsic value as some kind of existence or property that has value in itself, regardless of context, which is the object of practice to bring about, realize, or conform to."
   The objection and reply Anderson reports (§3): "The standard objection to Dewey’s instrumental theory of value judgments is that it concerns the value of things as means only, and not as ends."
   "Dewey’s reply to this objection goes to the heart of his moral philosophy. He argued that the character and value of means and ends was reciprocally determined."
   Zimmerman & Bradley (§3): "This theme has been elaborated by Monroe Beardsley, who attacks the very notion of intrinsic value (Beardsley 1965; cf. Conee 1982)."
6. **Intrinsic value in environmental ethics.** Brennan & Lo (§1): "In the literature on environmental ethics the distinction between instrumental value and intrinsic value (in the sense of “non-instrumental value”) is of considerable importance."
   "Because the intrinsically valuable is that which is good as an end in itself, it is commonly agreed that something’s possession of intrinsic value generates a prima facie direct moral duty on the part of moral agents to protect it or at least refrain from damaging it (see O’Neil 1992 and Jamieson 2002 for detailed accounts of intrinsic value)."
   On the utilitarian side (§4): "Unlike the environmental philosophers who attribute intrinsic value to the natural environment and its inhabitants, Singer and utilitarians in general attribute intrinsic value to the experience of pleasure or interest satisfaction as such, not to the beings who have the experience."
   Two conceptions, as they report the suggestion (§4): "One the one side, there is the intrinsic value of states of affairs that are to be promoted—and this is the focus of the consequentialist thinkers." "On the other (deontological) hand there is the intrinsic values of entities to be respected (see Bradley 2006, McShane 2014)."
7. **Intrinsic value in animal ethics: Regan's inherent value.** Regan,
   *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), as Brennan & Lo (§4) report it: "For instance, “animal rights” advocate Tom Regan (1983) argues that those animals with intrinsic value (or what he calls “inherent value”) have the moral right to respectful treatment, which then generates a general moral duty on our part not to treat them as mere means to other ends."
   "Regan’s answer is: those that meet the criterion of being the “subject-of-a-life”."
   Regan (1985: 24), quoted by Gruen & Monsó (SEP, §1.3.1): "they too must be viewed as the experiencing subjects of a life, with inherent value of their own."
   The criterion is also reported on [the moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).
8. **Taylor's inherent worth (biocentrism).** Taylor, *Respect for Nature*
   (1986), as Brennan & Lo (§4) report it: "He argues that each individual living thing in nature—whether it is an animal, a plant, or a micro-organism—is a “teleological-center-of-life” having a good or well-being of its own which can be enhanced or damaged, and that all individuals who are teleological-centers-of life have equal intrinsic value (or what he calls “inherent worth”) which entitles them to moral respect."
9. **Intrinsic value of the animal in Dutch law.** The Dutch *Wet dieren*
   (Animals Act, 2011), Art. 1.3 "Intrinsieke waarde", lid 1: "1 De intrinsieke waarde van het dier wordt erkend."
   Lid 2 (first sentence): "2 Onder erkenning van de intrinsieke waarde als bedoeld in het eerste lid wordt verstaan erkenning van de eigen waarde van dieren, zijnde wezens met gevoel."
   (Dutch statutory text; no translation is given here.) The scholarly literature on this notion was located but not read:
   Meijboom 2012 ([doi:10.3920/978-90-8686-753-0_68](https://doi.org/10.3920/978-90-8686-753-0_68))
   and Swart & Keulartz 2011, *Acta Biotheoretica* 59: 185–200
   ([doi:10.1007/s10441-011-9126-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10441-011-9126-0));
   DOIs verified, contents not reported.

## Collisions

- **Ethical value vs economic value.** Schroeder's paradigm of a merely
  instrumental good is money (sense 1). Kant, as Zimmerman & Bradley (§2)
  report him: "A little later on he says that all rational beings, even those that lack a good will, have “absolute value”; such beings are “ends in themselves” that have a “dignity” or “intrinsic value” that is “above all price” (Kant 1785, Ak. 64 and 77)."
  Their assessment (§2): "Nonetheless, it becomes clear on further inspection that Kant is in fact discussing a concept quite different from that with which this article is concerned."
  In environmental ethics Brennan & Lo (§5) report Sagoff: "He argues that “as citizens rather than consumers” people are concerned about values, which cannot plausibly be reduced to mere ordered preferences or quantified in monetary terms."
  "Sagoff’s distinction between people as consumers and people as citizens was intended to blunt the use of cost-benefit analysis as the final arbiter in discussions about nature’s value."
  Structural note (this implant): none of the sources read defines
  economic value itself; the page records only that price, money and
  monetary measures appear in them as contrasts to intrinsic value, and
  keeps claims about market price apart from claims about value in senses
  1–9.
- **Intrinsic as for its own sake vs intrinsic as resting on intrinsic
  properties.** Senses 1 and 3 use one word for two notions on Korsgaard's
  account; a claim that something has "intrinsic value" is read in the
  source's sense, not converted.
- **Intrinsic value vs inherent value or worth.** Regan's "inherent value"
  and Taylor's "inherent worth" are reported by Brennan & Lo (§4) as their
  names for intrinsic value of individuals (senses 7–8); the Dutch statute
  glosses its term as "eigen waarde" (sense 9). Structural note: whether
  these are the same notion as Moore's intrinsic value of states (sense 2)
  is the two-conceptions question Brennan & Lo report (sense 6).
- **Value vs attributive "good".** Schroeder (§2.1.2): "It does not make sense, for example, to say that something is a good can opener, but only instrumentally, or that Sue is a good dancer, but only instrumentally."
  The varieties of "good" are on [good and evil](good-and-evil.md).
- ***Value theory*.** Schroeder (preamble): "In its narrowest sense, “value theory” is used for a relatively narrow area of normative ethical theory particularly, but not exclusively, of concern to consequentialists."
  "In this narrow sense, “value theory” is roughly synonymous with “axiology”."
  In its broadest sense it is, in his words, "a catch-all label" for
  evaluative areas of philosophy.

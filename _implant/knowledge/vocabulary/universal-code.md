# Universal code (ethics)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Universal code (ethics)** is used here as a pointer term: a set of
moral standards that some named source claims applies to all people, or
claims is found among all people, in whichever sense that source gives
it. On every page the sense is named: universal in scope (a metaethical
claim), universal in occurrence (a descriptive claim about societies), or a
proposed common code (a declaration that asks for assent).

Why this definition: whether there is such a code is the open question
the sources dispute. Gowans (SEP "Moral Relativism", Fall 2024 archive,
rev. 2021, §2) defines the relativist theses against which the claim is
made, among them "Metaethical Moral Relativism (MMR). The truth or falsity of moral judgments, or their justification, is not absolute or universal, but is relative to the traditions, convictions, or practices of a group of persons."
He reports objectors to the descriptive thesis (§4) and closes that
section with "In the final analysis, there may be significant agreements as well as disagreements in people’s moral values."
A definition that asserted a universal code exists, or that none does,
would take a side in that debate; the implant therefore reports each
proposal with its holders and its critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).
The term itself is thin as a title: the Wikipedia article of that name
has been a redirect since 2023 (below), so this page is a map of what the
Outline entry points to, not a concept with its own literature.

Fixed 2026-10-15 ([journal](../../journals/archive/2026/10/2026-10-15.md)).

1. **The Wikipedia entry (a redirect).** The Outline of ethics (rev.
   1369745706, "Guidelines and basic concepts") lists "Universal code
   (ethics)" with no gloss. Since 12 May 2023 that title is a one-line
   redirect to "Categorical imperative"; the editor's summary gives the
   reason as "entire article in a mixture of WP:SYNTH and farcicial misattribution of beliefs to commonly misunderstood philosophers, redirect to categorical imperative on the basis of what incoming links appear to be aiming for"
   (spelling as in the summary; wiki link brackets removed). The replaced article, tagged for missing
   citations, opened: "In ethics, a "universal code of ethics" is a system of ethics that can apply to every sentient being."
   The redirect target is Kant's principle, which the target article quotes as "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
   — see [categorical imperative](../positions/categorical-imperative.md).
2. **Universal in scope: moral universalism (metaethics).** The
   Wikipedia article "Moral universalism" (rev. 1375538627, citing
   Gowans) defines it as "the meta-ethical position that some system of ethics, or a universal ethic, applies universally, that is, for "all similarly situated individuals", regardless of culture, disability, race, sex, religion, nationality, sexual orientation, gender identity, or any other distinguishing feature."
   Gowans's MMR (quoted above) is the denial that moral truth or
   justification is "absolute or universal".
3. **Universal in occurrence: shared moral content across societies.** Gowans
   (§4) reports this as an objection to Descriptive Moral Relativism,
   which he defines (§2) as "Descriptive Moral Relativism (DMR). As a matter of empirical fact, there are deep and widespread moral disagreements across different societies, and these disagreements are much more significant than whatever agreements there may be."
   The objectors' candidates, as he lists them: the Golden Rule's
   role-reversal test, endorsed "in The Analects of Confucius, some traditional Buddhist texts, and elsewhere (see Wattles 1996)"
   ([golden rule](../positions/golden-rule.md)); prohibitions "against lying, stealing, adultery, killing human beings, etc."; and the claim that "the international human rights movement indicates substantial moral agreement (see Donnelly 2013: ch. 4)."
   ([human rights](../positions/human-rights.md)).
4. **A universal minimal morality (Bok 1995, Walzer 1994), as Gowans
   reports it.** "On the basis of evidence of this kind, some such as Sissela Bok (1995) and Michael Walzer (1994) have proposed that there is a universal minimal morality, whatever other moral differences there may be."
   Bok's *Common Values* (University of Missouri Press, 1995) was not
   read; it is cited here only through Gowans.
5. **A global ethic of the religions (Küng; Parliament of the World's
   Religions 1993).** Gowans (§4): "In a similar vein, Hans Küng (1996) and others have maintained that there is a common “global ethic” across the world’s major religious traditions regarding respect for human life, distributive justice, truthfulness, and the moral equality of men and women."
   The Declaration defines its term: "By a global ethic we mean a fundamental consensus on binding values, irrevocable standards, and personal attitudes."
   Its basis is the Golden Rule in both forms: "There is a principle which is found and has persisted in many religious and ethical traditions of humankind for thousands of years: What you do not wish done to yourself, do not do to others. Or in positive terms: What you wish done to yourself, do to others! This should be the irrevocable, unconditional norm for all areas of life, for families and communities, for races, nations, and religions."
   Five directives follow, from "1. Commitment to a Culture of Non-violence and Respect for Life."
   to "5. Commitment to a Culture of Sustainability and Care for the Earth."
   Wikipedia (rev. 1349928002, an article carrying a conflict-of-interest
   tag) reports that Küng "wrote an initial draft in consultation with fellow scholars and religious leaders.",
   that it "was then signed by more than 200 leaders from 40+ different faith traditions and spiritual communities during the Parliament's 1993 gathering in Chicago."
   and that "The fifth directive became official in July 2018."
6. **An intergovernmental project (UNESCO Universal Ethics Project,
   1997–1999), as reported.** Han (2024, a biography drawing on Yersu
   Kim's memoir) reports the UNESCO division's mandate as "an ethical minimum: a shared substratum of values that would help make viable countries’ coexistence economically, socially, and culturally on a world-wide scale."
   and that "During his tenure, Kim launched UNESCO’s Universal Ethics Project in March 1997."
   The report, *A Common Framework for the Ethics of the 21st Century*
   (October 1999), could not be fetched (UNESCO returned 403) and is not
   quoted here.
7. **Human universals (Brown, anthropology).** Brown (2004, *Daedalus*
   133(4)) defines the wider category, of which moral items are a part:
   "Human universals--of which hundreds have been identified--consist of those features of culture, society, language, behavior, and mind that, so far as the record has been examined, are found among all peoples known to ethnography and history."
   His social examples include "exchange, cooperation, and reciprocity",
   and he discusses "the incest taboo" ([taboo](taboo.md)). His 1991 book
   *Human Universals* (McGraw-Hill) was not read; the Wikipedia stub
   (rev. 1362599336) reports that "The list is seen by Brown (and Pinker) to be evidence of mental adaptations to communal life in our species' evolutionary history."
8. **Candidate universal moral rules (Curry, Mullins & Whitehouse 2019).**
   The authors' abstract: "We find that the moral valence of these behaviors is uniformly positive, and the majority of these cooperative morals are observed in the majority of cultures, with equal frequency across all regions of the world."
   and "We conclude that these seven cooperative behaviors are plausible candidates for universal moral rules, and that morality-as-cooperation could provide the unified theory of morality that anthropology has hitherto lacked."
   In a 2018 debate Curry listed the seven as "love your family, help your group, return favours, be brave, defer to your superiors, be fair, and respect others' property"
   and added: "MAC does not predict that moral values will be identical across cultures."
   Critics, side by side (abstracts and debate text only; full articles
   not read):
   - The opposing speaker in the same debate (JRAI 2020; the portion
     read does not name the speaker): "My objections are empirical (I don't think that morality is 'like that'), theoretical (I don't find the argument convincing), and moral (I don't think that people should think of morality in this way)."
   - Spodniak (*Filozofia* 75(7), 2020, [doi:10.31577/filozofia.2020.75.7.6](https://doi.org/10.31577/filozofia.2020.75.7.6)) concludes "that their theory may be able to explain why it is advantageous with respect to natural selection to prefer certain behaviour, but not why we attribute moral evaluations to a given behaviour."
   - Uptake in philosophy: Fairhurst (*Synthese* 207, 2026, [doi:10.1007/s11229-026-05514-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-026-05514-z)) "resorts to the results obtained by Curry’s theory of Morality-as-Cooperation to argue that the goodness of cooperation may be a universal moral certainty."
   - The chair's report of the debate votes: "thirteen people were in favour of the proposition, six were against, and there were six abstentions."
     before, and "four people voted for the proposition, twenty-four against, and two abstained."
     after, with his caution: "It would be unwise to read too much into the votes, however."

## Collisions

- **Universal in scope vs universal in occurrence.** Gowans (§4, opening)
  separates the two theses: "Though this is not sufficient to establish MMR, the most common rationales for MMR would be undermined if DMR (or some descriptive thesis about significant moral disagreement or diversity) were incorrect."
  A finding that a rule is found in all
  societies (senses 3, 7, 8) and a claim that a rule binds all agents
  (sense 2, Kant in sense 1) are different claims; a page citing one as
  the other is affected. Spodniak's objection (sense 8) turns on a
  related gap, between explaining a behaviour and explaining its moral
  evaluation.
- **A global ethic vs one religion or ideology.** The Declaration denies
  the second reading in its own words: "By a global ethic we do not mean a global ideology or a single unified religion beyond all existing religions, and certainly not the domination of one religion over all others."
- **The Wikipedia title vs its target.** A link to "Universal code
  (ethics)" lands on the categorical imperative; the former article's
  claims (for example that "Marx and Engels believed that there could be
  no universal code of ethics") are uncited in that article and are not
  used here.
- **Relativism in anthropology.** Gowans (§1) reports the 1947 American
  Anthropological Association statement "declaring that moral values are relative to cultures and that there is no way of showing that the values of one culture are better than those of another."
  and adds: "Anthropologists have never been unanimous in asserting this, and more recently human rights advocacy on the part of some anthropologists has mitigated the relativist orientation of the discipline."
  The [human rights](../positions/human-rights.md) page reports the same
  statement through Nickel & Etinson.

# Liberty (positive and negative)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Liberty** (used interchangeably with **freedom**) names the political and
social ideal whose content is disputed between the senses listed below. On
this page the word is a pointer to that dispute, not a definition that
settles it: a sentence elsewhere in the implant that says "liberty" without
a qualifier refers to the ideal under dispute, and a sentence that relies on
one sense says which ("negative liberty", "positive liberty", "freedom as
non-domination") and whose it is.

Why a pointer: Carter (SEP 2024, preamble) reports that the two senses "can
be seen as rival, incompatible interpretations of a single political ideal"
and that "Political liberalism tends to presuppose a negative definition of liberty" while
critics of liberalism contest that definition; adopting any one sense as the
implant's own would therefore take a side in political philosophy. The
republican sense is listed beside the two because Carter (§3.2) judges it
open whether republicans "have successfully carved out a third concept",
and MacCallum's analysis is listed because it denies that there are two
concepts at all (§4). Granularity: the senses differ in where they locate
the agent, the constraint and the purpose (MacCallum's three variables), so
the pointer must be split at least that finely. "Liberty" in this sense is
kept apart from free will (see Collisions).

## Descriptive definitions

The senses side by side, each with its holders as the cited encyclopedias
report them. The table is a summary; the entries below it carry the
citations.

| Sense | Liberty is | Associated (as reported) |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | absence of interference or external obstacles | Constant, Humboldt, Spencer, Mill; Hobbes, Bentham |
| Positive | self-mastery, self-determination, self-realization | Rousseau, Hegel, Marx, T. H. Green; Spinoza |
| Republican | non-domination: not subject to arbitrary power | Pettit, Skinner, Lovett |
| Triadic (meta-analysis) | an agent free from constraints to do or become something | MacCallum, after Oppenheim |

- **Negative liberty.** Carter (SEP "Positive and Negative Liberty", Fall
  2024, preamble): "Negative liberty is the absence of obstacles, barriers or constraints."
  In Berlin's formulation, as Carter quotes it, the negative concept answers the
  question "What is the area within which the subject — a person or group of persons — is or should be left to do or be what he is able to do or be, without interference by other persons?"
  (Berlin 1969, pp. 121–22, via Carter §1). Lovett (SEP "Republicanism",
  Fall 2024, §1.1): "According to the first, ‘negative’ conception of liberty, people are free simply to the extent that their choices are not interfered with."
  Carter (§1) reports that "theorists in the classical liberal tradition, like Benjamin Constant, Wilhelm von Humboldt, Herbert Spencer, and J.S. Mill, are typically classed as answering ‘no’ and therefore as defending a negative concept of political freedom"
  (the question being whether the state can promote citizens' positive
  freedom on their behalf). Lovett (§1.1) reports that Berlin associates the
  negative idea: "This idea of negative liberty Berlin associates especially with the classic English political philosophers Hobbes, Bentham, and J. S. Mill, and it is today probably the dominant conception of liberty, particularly among contemporary Anglo-American philosophers."
  (Lovett's assessment). [Mill](../thinkers/mill.md)'s limit on interference is the
  [harm principle](../positions/harm-principle.md): "the sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection"
  (*On Liberty*, 1859, ch. I; `raw/mill-1859-1879-on-liberty-utilitarianism-subjection.md`).
- **Positive liberty.** Carter (preamble): "Positive liberty is the possibility of acting — or the fact of acting — in such a way as to take control of one’s life and realize one’s fundamental purposes."
  Berlin's question for it, as Carter quotes it: "What, or who, is the source of control or interference that can determine someone to do, or be, this rather than that?"
  Lovett (§1.1): "Roughly speaking, a person or group is free in the positive sense to the extent that they exercise self-control or self-mastery."
  Carter (§1) reports that critics of the classical liberal tradition, "like Jean-Jacques Rousseau, G.W.F. Hegel, Karl Marx and T.H. Green, are typically classed as answering ‘yes’ and as defending a positive concept of political freedom."
  Lovett (§1.1): "Berlin associates this second conception especially with such continental philosophers as Spinoza, Rousseau, and Hegel."
  Carter (preamble) notes that positive liberty "is sometimes attributed to collectivities", and
  that "Work on the nature of positive liberty often overlaps, however, with work on the nature of autonomy."
  A content-neutral version: "John Christman (1991, 2005, 2009, 2013), for example, has argued that positive liberty concerns the ways in which desires are formed — whether as a result of rational reflection on all the options available, or as a result of pressure, manipulation or ignorance."
  (Carter §3.1).
- **Republican liberty (freedom as non-domination).** Carter (§3.2): "Another increasingly influential group of philosophers has rejected both the negative and the positive conception, claiming that liberty is not merely the enjoyment of a sphere of non-interference but the enjoyment of certain conditions in which such non-interference is guaranteed (Pettit 1997, 2001, 2014; Skinner 1998, 2002; Weinstock and Nadeau 2004; Laborde and Maynor 2008; Lovett 2010, forthcoming; Breen and McBride 2015, List and Valentini 2016)."
  Pettit's own statement (publisher's abstract of *Republicanism*, 1997,
  [doi:10.1093/0198296428.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0198296428.001.0001)):
  "The long republican tradition is characterized by a conception of freedom as non‐domination, which offers an alternative, both to the negative view of freedom as non‐interference and to the positive view of freedom as self‐mastery."
  Carter (§3.2): "Quentin Skinner has called this view of freedom ‘neo-Roman’, invoking ideas about freedom both of the ancient Romans and of a number of Renaissance and early modern writers."
  (Skinner, *Liberty before Liberalism*, [doi:10.1017/CBO9781139171274](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139171274)).
  Lovett (§1.2): "we might equivalently say that freedom in the republican sense consists in the secure enjoyment of non-domination."
- **One triadic concept (MacCallum 1967).** Carter (§4): "MacCallum defines the basic concept of freedom — the concept on which everyone agrees — as follows: a subject, or agent, is free from certain constraints, or preventing conditions, to do or become certain things."
  and "Freedom is therefore a triadic relation — that is, a relation between three things: an agent, certain preventing conditions, and certain doings or becomings of the agent."
  Source: Gerald C. MacCallum, "Negative and Positive Freedom", *The
  Philosophical Review* 76(3), 1967, pp. 312–34,
  [doi:10.2307/2183622](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183622) (bibliographic data
  verified; the article was not read here, so its content is reported as
  Carter reports it). Carter (§4) adds: "The definition of freedom as a triadic relation was first put forward in the seminal work of Felix Oppenheim in the 1950s and 60s."
- **Ancient and modern liberty (Constant 1819).** Constant's lecture at the
  Athénée royal de Paris contrasts two kinds of liberty: "Le but des anciens était le partage du pouvoir social entre tous les citoyens d’une même patrie. C’était là ce qu’ils nommaient liberté. Le but des modernes est la sécurité dans les jouissances privées ; et ils nomment liberté les garanties accordées par les institutions à ces jouissances."
  (*De la liberté des anciens comparée à celle des modernes*; *Œuvres
  politiques*, 1874, pp. 258–286). Modern liberty for him begins with "C’est pour chacun le droit de n’être soumis qu’aux lois, de ne pouvoir être ni arrêté, ni détenu, ni mis à mort, ni maltraité d’aucune manière, par l’effet de la volonté arbitraire d’un ou de plusieurs individus."
  He names a danger in each ("Le danger de la liberté antique" and "Le danger de la liberté moderne")
  and concludes that one must "apprendre à les combiner l’une avec l’autre."
  Carter (§1) classes Constant with the negative tradition; Lovett (§3.2)
  reports the republican reading in which Constant, with Bentham and Paley,
  took part in "the tendency in the liberal tradition, especially beginning with Bentham, Paley, and Constant, to displace the robust conception of liberty as independence from arbitrary or uncontrolled power with a weaker conception of liberty as non-interference."
- **Origin of the labels.** Carter (preamble): "The idea of distinguishing between a negative and a positive sense of the term ‘liberty’ goes back at least to Kant, and was examined and defended in depth by Isaiah Berlin in the 1950s and ’60s."
  (see [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md)). Carter (note 1): "‘Two Concepts of Liberty’ was Berlin’s inaugural lecture as Chichele Professor of Political and Social Theory at Oxford University."
  and "Berlin’s influences must certainly have included T. H. Green (1895) and Bernard Bosanquet (1899), who made explicit use of the distinction between positive and negative liberty"
  (Carter's assessment). Berlin's essay is cited in Carter's bibliography
  as "Berlin, I., 1969, ‘Two Concepts of Liberty’, in I. Berlin, Four Essays on Liberty, London: Oxford University Press: 118–72. New ed. in Berlin 2002: 166–217."
  It was not read here; its sentences on this page are quoted through Carter.
- **"Liberty" and "freedom".** Carter (preamble): "This is only a difference of style, and the terms ‘liberty’ and ‘freedom’ are normally used interchangeably by political and social philosophers."

## Collisions

- **Positive liberty and authoritarianism (the "paradox").** Carter (§2):
  "Many liberals, including Berlin, have suggested that the positive concept of liberty carries with it a danger of authoritarianism."
  Berlin, as Carter quotes him: "“Once I take this view”, Berlin says, “I am in a position to ignore the actual wishes of men or societies, to bully, oppress, torture in the name, and on behalf, of their ‘real’ selves, in the secure knowledge that whatever is the true goal of man ... must be identical with his freedom” (Berlin 1969, pp. 132–33)."
  The other side, as Carter reports it (§3): "Critics, however, have objected that the ideal described by Humboldt and Mill looks much more like a positive concept of liberty than a negative one."
  Both are reported; the implant ranks neither. A page that cites one
  liberal or one critic of liberalism names which sense it uses.
- **Republican vs negative liberty.** Pettit's claim of distinctness is
  reported above. On the other side, Carter (§3.2): "Ian Carter (1999, 2008), Matthew H. Kramer (2003, 2008), and Robert Goodin and Frank Jackson (2007) have argued, along these lines, that republican policies are best defended empirically on the basis of the standard negative ideal of freedom, rather than on the basis of a conceptual challenge to that ideal."
  The republican reply (Carter §3.2): "In reply to the above point about the relevance of probabilities, republicans have insisted that freedom as non-domination is nevertheless distinct from negative liberty because what matters for an agent’s freedom is the impossibility of others interfering, not the mere improbability of their doing so."
  Lovett (§1.3), writing from the republican side, holds both that "Notice that the republican view of freedom is, at least in the broad sense, a negative conception of political liberty."
  and that "Despite these similarities, however, republican freedom is not equivalent to the received view of negative liberty as non-interference."
  Carter's own summary (§3.2): "The jury is still out on whether republicans have successfully carved out a third concept of freedom that is really distinct from those of negative and positive liberty."
- **Two concepts or one.** MacCallum's position, as Carter (§4) reports it,
  is that there is "only one basic concept of freedom, on which both sides in the debate converge."
  and that "The dichotomy between ‘freedom from’ and ‘freedom to’ is therefore a false one," (Carter's paraphrase of the MacCallum view).
  Against reading the distinction away, Carter (§7) reports C. Taylor's
  exercise/opportunity distinction: "If one thinks of freedom as involving self-direction, on the other hand, one has in mind an exercise-concept of freedom as opposed to an opportunity-concept (this distinction comes from C. Taylor 1979)."
  and Nelson's reply: "However, this defence of the positive-negative distinction as coinciding with the distinction between exercise- and opportunity-concepts of freedom has been challenged by Eric Nelson (2005)."
- **Political liberty vs free will.** Carter (preamble): discussions of
  positive and negative liberty "are distinct from, though sometimes related to, philosophical discussions about free will."
  The implant's [argument from free will](../problems/argument-from-free-will.md)
  and the liberty of indifference discussed under [Buridan's ass](../problems/buridans-ass.md)
  concern the will, the subject Carter separates from this one.
- **Freedom from and freedom to.** Carter (§4), presenting MacCallum's
  analysis, holds that "each claim about freedom assumes both that freedom is freedom from something (i.e., preventing conditions) and that it is freedom to do or become something."
  A page that uses the two phrases as names for negative and positive
  liberty therefore names whose usage it follows.

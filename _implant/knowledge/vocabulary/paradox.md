# Paradox

## Normative definition (this implant)

A **paradox** is an argument (or a statement that generates one) in which
apparently acceptable premises and apparently acceptable reasoning lead to
an apparently unacceptable conclusion. The sorites entry of the SEP puts it
for its own case: "The argument is a paradox because apparently impeccable
reasoning from apparently impeccable premises yields a falsehood" (Hyde &
Raffman, [SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/sorites-paradox/),
preamble; excerpt:
`raw/sep-sorites-and-liar-fall-2024-paradox-formulations-and-responses.md`).

Why this one: it makes the three things one can do about a paradox
separable and reportable — reject a premise, reject the reasoning
(the logic), or accept the conclusion. Those are the families under which
the SEP entries arrange the responses (see [the sorites paradox](../problems/sorites-paradox.md)
and [the liar paradox](../problems/liar-paradox.md)), and they map onto
the premise-by-premise structure of [analyse-an-argument](../../skills/analyse-an-argument.md).
Whether the premises are acceptable, whether the conclusion is, and which
to give up, is what the literature disputes; this implant reports each
party. Fixed 2026-09-26 ([journal](../../journals/archive/2026/09/2026-09-26.md)).

## Descriptive definitions

- **"Against common opinion".** "By “paradox” one usually means a statement claiming something which goes beyond (or even against) ‘common opinion’ (what is usually believed or held)" (Cantini, [SEP Fall 2024 "Paradoxes and Contemporary Logic"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/paradoxes-contemporary-logic/), preamble). This is closest to the Greek *para doxa*.
- **Antinomy.** Cantini uses "antinomy" "as alternative to, and synonymous
  with, “paradox”", and adds "Most paradoxes—but not all—involve
  contradictions" (preamble).
- **Insolubilia.** The medieval name: "Paradoxes—termed as Insolubilia—form
  also a substantial part of logical and philosophical investigations
  during the Middle Ages" (Cantini, preamble); the liar was "one of the
  insolubilia" studied by Buridan (Beall, Glanzberg & Ripley,
  [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/liar-paradox/),
  preamble).
- **Rhetorical sense.** A statement that seems self-contradictory but makes
  a point; Silva Rhetoricae lists "paradox" among the figures related to
  [oxymoron](oxymoron.md), which it calls "A compressed paradox"
  ([Silva Rhetoricae](http://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/O/oxymoron.htm)).
- **Everyday "paradox" for a surprise.** A result contrary to expectation,
  with no argument to diagnose. Sources that use "paradox" this way are
  reported in their own word; no page here files a surprising empirical
  result as a paradox on its own authority.

## Collisions

- **Paradox vs. contradiction.** A contradiction is a proposition of the
  form *P and not-P*. Some paradoxes derive one (the liar, per Beall,
  Glanzberg & Ripley §1.1); some derive a conclusion that is unacceptable
  but not contradictory (the sorites conclusion "1 million grains don’t
  make a heap"). Cantini's "not all" covers the difference.
- **"Solved" is a report, not a verdict.** Hyde & Raffman report that "most
  theorists of vagueness suppose that the paradox is solvable" and that
  others hold "the paradox is unsolvable; we are just stuck with it" (§3.4).
  Problem pages here list proposed solutions with their proponents and never
  mark one as the solution ([manifest](../../vision/manifest.md), G3–G4).
- **Paradox vs. dilemma.** A [dilemma](dilemma.md) in logic is a valid
  argument form with two horns; it may be used to generate a paradox, but
  is not one by definition.

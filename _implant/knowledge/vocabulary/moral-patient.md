# Moral patient

## Normative definition (this implant)

A **moral patient** is an entity whose interests matter morally for their
own sake, regardless of whether the entity can act morally or has any other
instrumental value. The term names the *recipient* side of moral concern;
a **moral agent** is an entity capable of acting on moral reasons. An entity
can be a patient without being an agent (infants, severely cognitively
disabled humans, many non-human animals on standard accounts), and an agent
without being a patient is a conceptual possibility debated in the
literature (e.g., Kant's view that rational beings are ends in themselves
because they are agents, not independently).

Why this definition: the grounds of moral patiency are contested — sentience,
rationality, relationality, species membership, divine creation, and other
properties have all been proposed. Adopting one as the implant's normative
sense would take a side in normative ethics. The implant uses "moral
patient" as a pointer to the debate and reports each proposed ground with
its proponents and critics.

## Descriptive definitions

- **Sentience-based:** entities that can experience pleasure or pain are
  moral patients. Bentham (1789), Singer (1975), Birch (2024). See
  [sentience](sentience.md).
- **Rationality-based:** entities capable of autonomous rational agency are
  moral patients (and agents). Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of
  Morals* (1785); Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity* (1996).
- **Relational / care ethics:** moral patiency arises from relationships of
  care and dependence, not from intrinsic properties. Noddings, *Caring*
  (1984); Held, *The Ethics of Care* (2006).
- **Species-membership:** all and only members of *Homo sapiens* are moral
  patients, regardless of capacities. This view is defended by some
  religious traditions and by species-norm accounts (e.g., Kaczor, *The
  Ethics of Abortion*, 2011); critics call it speciesism (Singer 1975).
- **Divine creation / imago Dei:** entities created in God's image are moral
  patients. Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I–II; contemporary Christian
  ethicists vary on scope.
- **Capabilities approach:** entities with certain basic capabilities
  (life, bodily health, affiliation, etc.) are moral patients. Nussbaum,
  *Frontiers of Justice* (2006).
- **Contractarian:** moral patiency extends to those with whom rational
  agents would contract under fair conditions. Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*
  (1971); Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998). Scope varies by
  formulation.

## Collisions

- **Patient vs. agent.** The primary distinction. An entity's moral status
  may differ depending on which role is in view: infants are patients but
  not agents; high-capability AIs may be agents (on some accounts) without
  being patients (on sentience-based accounts). The implant distinguishes
  them throughout.
- **Moral patient vs. rights-holder.** Having moral status does not entail
  having rights; rights are one way of encoding moral status, not the only
  way. Animals may be moral patients on sentience-based accounts without
  having legal or moral rights in the strict sense. The implant records
  rights claims when attributed.
- **Moral patient vs. moral object.** Everything is a moral object in the
  sense that actions toward it may be right or wrong; only moral patients
  are wronged *in their own right*. Destroying a painting may be wrong
  (to its owner, to culture) without wronging the painting. The implant
  uses "moral patient" for the narrower sense.

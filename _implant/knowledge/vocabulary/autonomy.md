# Autonomy (and self-determination)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Autonomy** is used here as a pointer term: self-government, of a will, a
person or a group, in whichever sense a cited source gives it. Christman
(SEP 2020, §1) states the common core: "Put most simply, to be autonomous is to govern oneself, to be directed by considerations, desires, conditions, and characteristics that are not simply imposed externally upon one, but are part of what can somehow be considered one’s authentic self."
**Self-determination** is used for the same idea where the cited source
applies it to a people or group (see the political sense below). On every
page the sense is named (Kantian, personal, bioethical, political).

Why this definition: which conditions make a will, person or people
self-governing is the question the sources dispute. Christman (§1.2) judges
that autonomy, "like many concepts central to contentious moral or political debate is itself essentially contested"
(his assessment); Buss and Westlund (SEP 2018, preamble) report on the
features of the self-governing relation that "Philosophers have offered a wide range of competing answers to this question."
Adopting Kant's, Frankfurt's, a relational, or a bioethical definition as
the implant's own would take a side in that debate, and in the debates that
depend on it ([euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md), paternalism,
secession). The implant reports each sense with its holders and critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Listed in the order of the brief, not ranked.

1. **Kantian autonomy of the will (moral autonomy).** [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md),
   *Groundwork* (1785), Second Section (Abbott trans.; Johnson & Cureton
   cite the heteronomy sentence as G 4:441):
   "Autonomy of the will is that property of it by which it is a law to itself (independently of any property of the objects of volition)."
   Its contrast, heteronomy: "If the will seeks the law which is to determine it anywhere else than in the fitness of its maxims to be universal laws of its own dictation, consequently if it goes out of itself and seeks this law in the character of any of its objects, there always results heteronomy."
   Christman (§2): "For Kant, the self-imposition of universal moral law is the ground of both moral obligation generally and the respect others owe to us (and we owe ourselves)."
   and "This self-imposition of the moral law is autonomy." Johnson &
   Cureton (SEP Kant's Moral Philosophy, §10): "At the heart of Kant’s moral theory is the idea of autonomy."
   Christman (§1.1) names this sense: "“Moral autonomy” refers to the capacity to impose the (putatively objective) moral law on oneself, and, following Kant, it is claimed as a fundamental organizing principle of all morality (Hill 1989)."
2. **Personal autonomy.** Christman (§1.1): "On the other hand, what can be called “personal autonomy” is meant as a trait that individuals can exhibit relative to any aspects of their lives, not limited to questions of moral obligation (Dworkin 1988, 34–47)."
   Buss & Westlund (preamble): "Autonomous agents are self-governing agents."
   Accounts of it, as the sources report them:
   - *Hierarchical (coherentist) accounts.* Christman (§1.2): "The most influential models of authenticity in this vein claim that autonomy requires second-order identification with first order desires."
     "For Frankfurt, for instance, such second-order desires must actually have the structure of a volition: wanting that the first order desires issue in action, that they comprise one’s will."
     Christman adds (note 1): "Frankfurt's view is not explicitly an account of autonomy, but rather of freedom of the will."
     On Gerald Dworkin (note 2): "For Dworkin, autonomy involves (among other things) the capacity to raise the question of whether one identifies with the desires in question (Dworkin 1988, 15)."
     Buss & Westlund (§2) call this family "coherentist": "According to one popular story, an agent’s point of view is constituted by her highest-order desires regarding which of her first-order desires moves her to act."
     Objections reported by Christman (§1.2): "The most prominent objections concern, on the one hand, the fatal ambiguities of the concept of “identification” and, on the other, the threat of an infinite regress of conditions."
   - *Relational accounts (the critics).* Christman (preamble): "For this reason, certain philosophical movements, such as certain strains of feminism, have resisted seeing autonomy as a value (Jaggar 1983, chap. 3)."
     Stoljar (SEP 2024, §1): "Feminist philosophers typically reject the Kantian and Rawlsian conceptions of autonomy."
     "The notion of self implicit in the Kantian and Rawlsian accounts is said to be “atomistic”; that is, it is abstracted from the social relations in which actual agents are embedded."
     "“Relational autonomy” is the name that has been given to feminist reconceptualizations of the notion of autonomy."
     Christman (§3.3): "Spurred by feminist critiques of traditional conceptions of autonomy and rights (Nedelsky, 1989, Code, 1991), relational conceptions of autonomy stress the ineliminable role that relatedness plays in both persons’ self- conceptions, relative to which autonomy must be defined, and the dynamics of deliberation and reasoning."
   - *Autonomy as a value in Mill's liberalism.* Christman (§2.1): "Following John Stuart Mill, for example, one can claim that autonomy is “one of the elements of well-being” (Mill 1859/1975, ch. III)."
     [Mill](../thinkers/mill.md), *On Liberty* (1859), ch. I: "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."
     (excerpt `raw/mill-1859-1879-on-liberty-utilitarianism-subjection.md`;
     the sentence closes the paragraph that states the
     [harm principle](../positions/harm-principle.md)).
3. **Respect for autonomy in bioethics.** Eyal (SEP 2019, §2.2): "The 1970s saw the ascent of a second rationale, autonomy, as the predominant justification of informed consent, through influential work by Ruth Faden, Tom Beauchamp, James Childress, and a U.S. President’s Commission"
   He quotes Beauchamp and Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*
   (6th ed., 2008), whose book was not read here: "Likewise, for bioethicists Beauchamp and Childress, “The autonomous individual acts freely in accordance with a self-chosen plan, analogous to the way an independent government manages its territories and establishes its policies” (Beauchamp and Childress 2008, 99–100)."
   and reports: "As the authors emphasize (Beauchamp and Childress 2008, 100–1), autonomy so understood differs from autonomy as having the will that one wants to have (Frankfurt 1988)."
   Christman (§2.2) places this outside his entry: "Not discussed here are areas of applied ethics, for example in medical ethics, where respect for autonomy grounds such principles as that of informed consent."
   In the [euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md) debate Young (SEP
   2024, §3) reports: "One central ethical contention in support of voluntary euthanasia is that respect for persons demands respect for their autonomous choices as long as those choices do not result in harm to others."
   (excerpt `raw/sep-euthanasia-voluntary-fall-2024-young.md`).
4. **Political and national self-determination.** Charter of the United
   Nations (1945), Art. 1(2), among the Purposes: "To develop friendly relations among nations based on respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples, and to take other appropriate measures to strengthen universal peace;"
   ICCPR (1966), Art. 1(1): "All peoples have the right of self-determination. By virtue of that right they freely determine their political status and freely pursue their economic, social and cultural development."
   Buchanan & Levinson (SEP 2021, §2.2) report: "According to the prevalent understanding of self-determination in mainstream political philosophy and international law, however, the right to self-determination includes only two features: freedom from external interference in self-governance and some minimally democratic form of government such that each member of the polity enjoys equal political participation rights."
   Two groundings of a right to secede in self-determination, as they
   report them: ascriptivist (§2) — "The most common form of ascriptivist theory holds that nations as such have a right of self-determination that includes the right to secede in order to have their own state."
   — and plebiscitary (§2.2) — "Plebiscitary theorists ground the right to secede in the right to self-determination (Wellman 2005: ch. 3)."
   "For them, self-determination is in itself of such great value that it can ground a right to complete political autonomy given certain conditions."
   Against ascriptivism, the authors' own objection (§2.1): "The point is not simply that the ascriptivist view is unfeasible; its support for the idea of the ethnically exclusive state is an incitement to ethnic cleansing if not genocide."
   Against the remedial-right-only view the authors discuss, critics'
   objection as they report it (§2.1): "Some critics have objected that the remedial right only approach to unilateral secession is disturbingly irrelevant to the concerns of many groups seeking self-determination."

Feinberg's list, as Christman reports it (§1.2): "Feinberg has claimed that there are at least four different meanings of “autonomy” in moral and political philosophy: the capacity to govern oneself, the actual condition of self-government, a personal ideal, and a set of rights expressive of one’s sovereignty over oneself (Feinberg 1989)."

## Collisions

- **Kantian vs personal autonomy.** In sense 1 the self imposes a moral law
  that "must be universal" (Christman §2); in sense 2 autonomy concerns any
  aspect of a life, "not limited to questions of moral obligation"
  (Christman §1.1). Structural note (this implant): a claim that a choice
  is autonomous in sense 2 is not thereby a claim that it conforms to
  Kant's moral law. The bioethical sense is a
  third: Beauchamp and Childress, as Eyal reports them (§2.2), distinguish
  their notion from Frankfurt's.
- **Autonomy vs freedom.** Christman (§1.1): "Personal (or individual) autonomy should also be distinguished from freedom, although again, there are many renderings of these concepts, and certainly some conceptions of positive freedom will be equivalent to what is often meant by autonomy (Berlin 1969, 131–34)."
  "Autonomy concerns the independence and authenticity of the desires (values, emotions, etc.) that move one to act in the first place."
  See [liberty, positive and negative](liberty-positive-and-negative.md).
- **Individual vs group self-government.** Buss & Westlund (§1) start from
  the group case: "When people living in some region of the world declare that their group has the right to live autonomously, they are saying that they ought to be allowed to govern themselves."
  Structural note (this implant): a people's self-determination (sense 4)
  and a person's autonomy (sense 2) share the idea of self-government;
  the page keeps claims about one apart from claims about the other.
- **Autonomy, heteronomy, theonomy.** [Tillich](../thinkers/tillich.md)
  (*Systematic Theology* I, p. 85) uses the pair in a theological sense
  that is not Kant's: "Autonomy and heteronomy are rooted in theonomy, and each goes astray when their theonomous unity is broken. Theonomy does not mean the acceptance of a divine law imposed on reason by a highest authority; it means autonomous reason united with its own depth."
  (excerpt `raw/tillich-1951-1963-systematic-theology-ultimate-concern-theonomy-agape-barth.md`).
- **"Moral autonomy" in the demandingness debate.** On the
  [demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md) page,
  "moral autonomy" (Slote 1985; Shiffrin 1991, as Hurka lists them) names a
  freedom to choose among permissible options, not Kant's self-legislation.
- **Self-determination in psychology.** *Self-determination* also names a
  construct in motivation research (Frey & Jegen 2001, as on the
  [motivation crowding](../problems/motivation-crowding-theory.md) page:
  "Impaired self-determination."); that is a psychological, not a
  political, sense.

## Related terms

- [Categorical imperative](../positions/categorical-imperative.md) —
  Christman (§2) on Kant: "Hence we have the (first formulation of the) Categorical Imperative, that by virtue of our being autonomous we must act only on those maxims that we can consistently will as a universal law." Johnson & Cureton (§7) quote the
  third formulation as "the Idea of the will of every rational being as a will that legislates universal law."
- [Harm principle](../positions/harm-principle.md) — Mill's anti-paternalist
  principle, whose paragraph ends with the sovereignty sentence quoted
  above; Christman (§2.2): "Respect for autonomy is meant to prohibit such interventions because they involve a judgment that the person is not able to decide for herself how best to pursue her own good."
- [Liberty, positive and negative](liberty-positive-and-negative.md) —
  freedom concerns the ability to act; autonomy, per Christman (§1.1), the
  authenticity of the motives.

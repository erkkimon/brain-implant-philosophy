# Archetype and collective unconscious

## Normative definition (this implant)

In this implant **archetype** and **collective unconscious** name C. G. Jung's terms, reported as
his, in the sense of *The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious* (Collected Works 9i, trans.
Hull, 2nd edn 1968; excerpts:
`raw/jung-1934-1954-archetypes-of-the-collective-unconscious-cw9i.md`). Jung writes that beneath
the personal unconscious lies "a deeper layer, which does not derive from personal experience and
is not a personal acquisition but is inborn. This deeper layer I call the collective unconscious"
(CW 9i, para. 3, in "Archetypes of the Collective Unconscious", Eranos-Jahrbuch 1934, revised
1954), and that "The contents of the collective unconscious, on the other hand, are known as
archetypes" (para. 4). A page here that uses either word without qualification means *what Jung
called by that name*; it does not assert that such a layer or such contents exist.

Why this one: the word "archetype" is older than Jung and carries other senses (below), and Jung's
claims about it are disputed inside and outside analytical psychology (see Collisions). Fixing the
sense as *Jung's term, reported* lets the [Jung](../thinkers/jung.md) page, [Psychological
Types](../works/psychological-types.md), [Answer to Job](../works/answer-to-job.md),
[synchronicity](synchronicity.md) and [individuation](individuation.md) share one referent while
every claim about it keeps an owner ([Reporting, not
endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md); [Words are
contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md); [How claims are
graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md)). Structural note (G4): this is a decision of
the batch that brought Jung and de Mello into the implant, 2026-10-01
([journal](../../journals/2026-10-01.md)). Other senses are marked where used.

## Descriptive definitions

- **Jung: the collective unconscious.** "It is, in other words, identical in all men and thus
  constitutes a common psychic substrate of a suprapersonal nature which is present in every one
  of us" (CW 9i, para. 3). In the 1936 London lecture "The Concept of the Collective Unconscious"
  Jung says its contents "have never been in consciousness, and therefore have never been
  individually acquired, but owe their existence exclusively to heredity" (para. 88), and that it
  "does not develop individually but is inherited. It consists of pre-existent forms, the
  archetypes" (para. 90).
- **Jung: the archetype as form, not content.** "There are as many
  archetypes as there are typical situations in life", engraved "not in
  the form of images filled with content, but at first only as forms
  without content" (para. 99). "It is not, therefore, a question of
  inherited ideas but of inherited possibilities of ideas" (para. 136).
  In 1938/1954: "The archetype in itself is empty and purely formal,
  nothing but a facultas praeformandi, a possibility of representation which is given a priori. The representations themselves are not inherited, only the forms" (para. 155).
  Jung also proposes "that the archetypes are the unconscious images of
  the instincts themselves, in other words, that they are patterns of
  instinctual behaviour" (para. 91).
- **Jung's own genealogy of the word.** Jung reports that "The term “archetype” occurs as early as
  Philo Judaeus, with reference to the Imago Dei (God-image) in man", that it is found in
  Irenaeus, the Corpus Hermeticum and Dionysius the Areopagite, and that "The term “archetype” is
  not found in St. Augustine, but the idea of it is", citing *De diversis quaestionibus LXXXIII*
  on "ideae principales" (para. 5). He writes: "“Archetype” is an explanatory paraphrase of the
  Platonic [εἶδος]" (para. 5), and that the word "was synonymous with “Idea” in the Platonic
  usage" (para. 149). He names further parallels: mythological "motifs", Lévy-Bruhl's
  "représentations collectives", Hubert and Mauss's "categories of the imagination", Bastian's
  "elementary" or "primordial thoughts" (para. 89). Of Kant he writes that the doctrine of
  categories "paves the way for a rebirth of the Platonic spirit" (para. 150), and that archetypes
  "form the “treasure in the realm of shadowy thoughts” of which Kant spoke" (para. 160). These
  attributions are Jung's; this page has not checked them against Philo, Irenaeus, Augustine or
  Kant.
- **Later Jungian: archetypal psychology (Hillman).** James Hillman's "archetypal psychology"
  (*The Myth of Analysis*, 1971; *Revisioning Psychology*, 1975; *Archetypal Psychology: A Brief
  Account*, 1983) is held here only bibliographically, as Roesler (2022) cites it; its text was
  not read. Roesler (2022, p. 258) judges that Hillman "never made the mistake to confuse the
  psychology of the archetype with the natural sciences, but made clear from the beginnings of his
  archetypal psychology that he was speaking of a world of imaginations, of psyche, of soul"
  (`raw/roesler-2022-iaap-report-archetype-theory.md`).
- **Later Jungian: four models.** Roesler (2022, p. 60) reports that Jean Knox (2003, p. 24)
  outlined "‘four models that repeatedly emerge in the debate about archetypes’, identifying these
  as biological entities, organising mental frameworks of an abstract nature, core meanings
  containing representational content and eternal metaphysical entities".
- **Literary criticism (Frye).** Northrop Frye, *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957, p. 99): "The symbol
  in this phase is the communicable unit, to which I give the name archetype: that is, a typical
  or recurring image. I mean by an archetype a symbol which connects one poem with another and
  thereby helps to unify and integrate our literary experience"
  (`raw/frye-1957-anatomy-of-criticism-archetype.md`).
- **Ordinary usage.** The Cambridge Dictionary gives "a typical example of something, or the
  original model of something from which others are copied", with the example "The United States
  is the archetype of a federal society." (`raw/cambridge-dictionary-archetype.md`). The entry
  does not refer to Jung.

## Collisions

- **Archetype vs. Platonic form.** Jung himself draws the parallel (paras. 5, 149) and himself
  draws the line: "Were I a philosopher, I should continue in this Platonic strain ... But I am an
  empiricist, not a philosopher" (para. 149), and "the concept of the collective unconscious is
  neither a speculative nor a philosophical but an empirical matter" (para. 92). Roesler (2022, p.
  46) reads him otherwise: "In his theories around the archetype concept, Jung is an outspoken
  Platonian." A page here that compares archetypes with Plato's forms reports both statements and
  does not decide between them.
- **Archetype vs. innate idea.** The innate/acquired distinction "played
  an important role in the history of philosophy as the locus of the
  dispute between Rationalism and Empiricism" (Griffiths & Linquist,
  [SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/innate-acquired/),
  preamble; `raw/sep-innate-acquired-fall-2024-griffiths-linquist.md`);
  the same entry reports that in scientific psychology "innate" and
  "instinctive" "have been used to refer to the same cluster of
  properties" (§1). Jung's archetype is inherited (para. 90) and likened
  to instinct (para. 91), but he denies that it is an inherited idea
  (para. 136). Sotirova-Kohli et al. (2013, §1 Introduction) write: "Perhaps one of the most controversial aspects of the notion of archetypes is that of innateness."
  (`raw/sotirova-kohli-et-al-2013-archetypal-memory-advantage.md`). The
  SEP entry does not mention Jung; the link between the two debates is
  this page's structural note (G4).
- **Jung's sense vs. Frye's.** Frye (1957, pp. 111–112) writes that in
  Jung's school "the communicability of archetypes is accounted for by a theory of a collective unconscious—an unnecessary hypothesis in literary criticism, so far as I can judge."
  A literary "archetype" on Frye's definition carries no claim about
  heredity.
- **Testability: what is said for and against.** Each side as its owners state it:
  - *Jung.* He calls the concept "empirical" (para. 92) and states the limit himself: "The
    existence of the instincts can no more be proved than the existence of the archetypes, so long
    as they do not manifest themselves concretely" (para. 155).
  - *An experimental test.* Sotirova-Kohli et al. (2013, abstract) write that the hypotheses
    "provoke, however, varying reactions among academic psychologists. Empirical studies which
    test these hypotheses are rare." They report that "presenting symbols matched with their
    accepted meanings exerts a statistically significant effect on learning and recall independent
    of language and culture" (§5), and add that "there may be other underlying factors that may
    have made the matched pairs easier to learn and recall than the mismatched pairs" (§4)
    ([doi:10.3390/bs3040541](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs3040541)).
  - *Neher (1996).* His abstract says his critique of Jung's belief in an
    "ancestral or universal essence" finds "These notions of Jung's are found to be seriously flawed. In spite of this, the critique suggests that Jung's belief in the genetic basis of certain unconscious content holds some promise."
    ([doi:10.1177/00221678960362008](https://doi.org/10.1177/00221678960362008);
    `raw/neher-1996-jungs-theory-of-archetypes-critique.md`). As Roesler
    (2022, p. 68) quotes him, demonstrating that an archetype "could not
    have derived from personal or common cultural experience, and therefore
    must be genetic in origin, is exceedingly difficult, if not impossible"
    (Neher 1996, p. 86).
  - *Roesler (2022).* He judges that Jung's conceptions conflict: "a concept that is thought to be transcendental and having no place in this world cannot be at the same time a biological entity and part of the genetic code" (p. 60).
    He quotes Hogenson (2003, p. 19): the archetypes "cannot be said to be
    some place. They are not in the genome." (p. 87). He proposes that
    "archetype theory could be reformulated as being a theory of cultural
    symbolization processes of psychological transformations" (p. 258).
  The positions are listed, not ranked
  ([manifest](../../vision/manifest.md), G3–G4).

## Related terms

- [Synchronicity](synchronicity.md) — Jung's term for meaningful coincidence; Neher's 1996
  abstract treats the collective unconscious's "paranormal or synchronistic" connection with the
  world as a related notion. Synchronicity names a kind of event, the archetype a content of the
  psyche.
- [Individuation](individuation.md) — Jung's term for a developmental process (see that page); it
  names a process, while Jung's "archetype" names a content of the collective unconscious (para. 4).
- Thinker and work pages: [Jung](../thinkers/jung.md), [Psychological Types](../works/psychological-types.md) (1921), [Answer to Job](../works/answer-to-job.md)
  (1952).
- [Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md) — Markie & Folescu (SEP
  2021, §3), as recorded on that page, write: "Plato presents an early version of the Innate Knowledge thesis in the Meno as the doctrine of knowledge by recollection."
  (`raw/sep-epistemic-paradoxes-and-plato-entries-fall-2024-menos-paradox.md`).
  Jung's own wording, "not ... a question of inherited ideas" (para. 136), is reported above;
  this page does not decide whether it separates his view from Plato's.

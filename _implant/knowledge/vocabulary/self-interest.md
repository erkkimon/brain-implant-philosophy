# Self-interest

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Self-interest** is used here as a pointer term: what is in a person's own
interest, in whichever sense a cited source gives it. Crisp (SEP "Well-Being", 2021, §1) gives the common core as a correlate of well-being:
"One correlate term worth noting here is ‘self-interest’: my self-interest is what is in the interest of myself, and not others."
On every page the sense is named: the account of what the interest
consists in (hedonist, desire, objective-list, hybrid) and, where egoism is
at issue, which egoism (psychological, ethical, rational).

Why this definition: both what self-interest consists in and what role it
plays are the questions the sources dispute. Shaver (SEP "Egoism", 2023,
§1) reports that "All forms of egoism require explication of “self-interest” (or “welfare” or “well-being”). There are three main theories."
Whether people only ever aim at it, whether they ought to, and whether
reason requires it are the three egoisms, each with defenders and critics
(below). Adopting one account of well-being, or treating self-interest as
the only motive, the moral standard or the rational one, would take a
side in those debates and in the debates that depend on them (the
[prisoner's dilemma](../problems/prisoners-dilemma.md), the
[paradox of hedonism](../problems/paradox-of-hedonism.md)). The implant
reports each sense with its holders and critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Listed by topic, not ranked.

1. **What self-interest consists in.** Crisp (§1): "Well-being is a kind of value, sometimes called ‘prudential value’, to be distinguished from, for example, aesthetic value or moral value."
   He adds: "But it remains an open question whether my being morally good is good for me; and, if it is, its being good for me is still conceptually distinct from its being morally good."
   The accounts, side by side, in Shaver (§1): "Preference or desire accounts identify self-interest with the satisfaction of one’s desires."
   "Objective accounts identify self-interest with the possession of states (such as virtue or knowledge) that are valuable independently of whether they are desired."
   "Hybrid accounts give a role to both desires (or pleasure) and states that are valuable independently of whether they are desired."
   Crisp (preamble): "It has become standard to distinguish theories of well-being as either hedonist theories, desire theories, or objective list theories."
2. **Psychological egoism (descriptive).** Shaver (preamble): "Egoism can be a descriptive or a normative position. Psychological egoism, the most famous descriptive position, claims that each person has but one ultimate aim: her own welfare."
   Kraut (SEP "Altruism", §2.1) words it: "According to a doctrine called “psychological egoism”, all human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest."
   Shaver (§1) names a support: "Psychological egoism is supported by our frequent observation of self-interested behavior."
   He reports a weakened variant: what Kavka (1986, 64–80) calls "predominant egoism:” we act unselfishly only rarely, and then typically where the sacrifice is small and the gain to others is large or where those benefiting are friends, family, or favorite causes."
3. **Ethical egoism (normative, moral).** Shaver (preamble): "Ethical egoism claims I morally ought to perform some action if and only if, and because, performing that action maximizes my self-interest."
4. **Rational egoism (normative, rational).** Shaver (preamble): "Rational egoism claims that I ought to perform some action if and only if, and because, performing that action maximizes my self-interest. (Here the “ought” is not restricted to the moral “ought”.)"
   [Parfit](../thinkers/parfit.md) (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, §1)
   names the corresponding theory of rationality: "I shall first discuss the Self-interest Theory, or S. This is a theory about rationality. S gives to each person this aim: the outcomes that would be best for himself, and that would make his life go, for him, as well as possible."
   He separates S from accounts of the interest: "To apply S, we must ask what would best achieve this aim. Answers to this question I call theories about self-interest. As Appendix I explains, there are three plausible theories."
   And he identifies it with Sidgwick's method (§54): "Sidgwick’s Egoistic Prudence is the Self-interest Theory about rationality."
5. **Self-interest in the eudaimonist tradition.** Kraut (§4) reports a strategy on which self-interested motivation is fundamental: "That strategy is often attributed to the Greek and Roman philosophers of antiquity—Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, and the Epicureans (see Annas 1993)."
   In [Plato](../thinkers/plato.md)'s *Republic*, Glaucon's Gyges story
   puts the question whether justice is in one's interest (360c, Shorey
   tr.): "And yet this is a great proof, one might argue, that no one is just of his own will but only from constraint, in the belief that justice is not his personal good, inasmuch as every man, when he supposes himself to have the power to do wrong, does wrong."

## Collisions

**Butler against the reduction to self-love.** Shaver (§1): "A common objection to psychological egoism, made famously by Joseph Butler, is that I must desire things other than my own welfare in order to get welfare."
"Welfare results from my action, but cannot be the only aim of my action."
Butler (*Fifteen Sermons*, 1726, Sermon XI, public domain): "Every particular affection, even the love of our neighbour, is as really our own affection as self-love; and the pleasure arising from its gratification is as much my own pleasure as the pleasure self-love would have from knowing I myself should be happy some time hence would be my own pleasure."
And: "Happiness does not consist in self-love. The desire of happiness is no more the thing itself than the desire of riches is the possession or enjoyment of them."
The egoist reply Shaver reports (§1): "My welfare might consist simply in the satisfaction of self-regarding desires."
Shaver also reports a trivial version: "If self-interest is identified with the satisfaction of all of one’s preferences, then all intentional action is self-interested (at least if intentional actions are always explained by citing preferences, as most believe). Psychological egoism turns out to be trivially true."

**Hobbes: egoist or not.** Butler's Sermon I footnote, keyed by his own
note to Hobbes's "Of Human Nature" c. ix. 7, describes a writer who "asserts the principle in the mind to be only the love of power, and delight in the exercise of it."
Hobbes's own sentences on which such readings draw (*Leviathan*, 1651,
ch. XIV): "For it is a voluntary act: and of the voluntary acts of every man, the object is some Good To Himselfe."
And ch. XV: "For no man giveth, but with intention of Good to himselfe; because Gift is Voluntary; and of all Voluntary Acts, the Object is to every man his own Good;"
Lloyd & Sreedhar (SEP, preamble) report the present state of scholarship: "The formerly dominant view that Hobbes espoused psychological egoism as the foundation of his moral theory is currently widely rejected, and there has been to date no fully systematic study of Hobbes’s moral psychology."
They list the readings side by side: "Most scholars have taken Hobbes to have affirmed some sort of personal relativism or subjectivism; but views that Hobbes espoused divine command theory, virtue ethics, rule egoism, or a form of projectivism also find support in Hobbes’s texts and among scholars."

**The empathy-altruism research and its critics.** Doris, Stich & Walmsley
(§2) give the psychologists' contrast: "On the standard account, defenders of altruism insist that, sometimes at least, people are motivated by an ultimate desire for the well-being of another person, while defenders of egoism maintain that all ultimate desires are self-interested."
They report (§5): "Batson (1991: 58) labels this response “empathy” which he characterizes as “an other-oriented emotional reaction to seeing someone suffer”, and calls the traditional idea that empathy leads to altruism the empathy-altruism hypothesis."
Shaver (§1) reports the findings: "First, Daniel Batson and colleagues found that increased empathy leads to increased helping behaviour."
"He found that the altruistic hypothesis always made superior predictions."
Batson's own conclusion (2011: 160, as quoted by Doris, Stich & Walmsley §5.4): "Pending new evidence or a plausible new egoistic explanation of the existing evidence, we seem forced to accept this improbable hypothesis as true."
The challenge: Cialdini, Brown, Lewis, Luce & Neuberg (1997,
doi:10.1037/0022-3514.73.3.481), as Doris, Stich & Walmsley (§5.3) report,
"report three studies indicating that empathy, though it does occur, is not playing any causal role in the process that leads to helping."
The reply: Batson, Sager, Garst, Kang, Rubchinsky & Dawson (1997,
doi:10.1037/0022-3514.73.3.495) "pointed out a number of methodological problems."
"When they conducted a pair of experiments that avoided these methodological problems, the role of empathy in producing helping behavior was clearly evident."
A replication: McAuliffe, Forster, Philippe & McCullough (2018,
doi:10.1037/emo0000375; 680 participants, pre-registered), whose findings
the entry reports as "“unambiguously supportive” (2018: 504) of the link between empathy and helping behavior."
The encyclopedia authors' assessments, side by side and reported as
theirs: Shaver (§1), "But on the whole, Batson’s experiments are very bad news for psychological egoism."
Doris, Stich & Walmsley (§5.4), "Batson’s research program is compelling, and he certainly has shown that the empathy-altruism hypothesis is “in the hunt”, but his findings are not conclusive."
They name an untested alternative, a "divine punishment hypothesis": "There is a family of egoistic hypotheses invoking beliefs in supernatural punishments—or supernatural rewards—that still needs to be ruled out before we accept the “improbable hypothesis” as true."
(The 1997 and 2018 studies are reported as the entry reports them.)

**Ethical egoism and its objectors.** The cooperation argument, as Shaver
(§2) reports it: "Ethical egoists can reply, however, that egoism generates many of the same duties to others."
His objection: "In dealings with others who lack these abilities, the egoist has no reason to cooperate."
Moore: "G. E. Moore argued that ethical egoism is self-contradictory."
A reply Shaver reports: "One might hold that “my good” should not be analyzed in terms of what Moore calls “good absolutely” but rather is a distinct concept, good-for-me, linked only to agent-relative reasons (Mackie 1976, Smith 2003)."
Prichard: "A second argument against ethical egoism was made by H. A. Prichard. He argues that self-interest is the wrong sort of reason."
Rand's version, as Badhwar & Long (SEP "Ayn Rand", preamble) report it: "In her non-fiction, Rand developed a conception of metaphysical realism, rationality, ethical egoism (rational self-interest), individual rights, laissez-faire capitalism, and art, and applied her philosophy to social issues."
They report (§2.6): "According to Rand, there is only one alternative to being rationally self-interested: sacrificing one’s proper interests, either for the sake of other people (which she equates with altruism) or for the sake of the supernatural (which she calls mysticism)"
Their interpretation (§2.5): "In its structure and much of its content, Rand’s ethical egoism is thus of a piece with the egoism of ancient eudaimonistic theories."
Their criticism (§2.5): "The root of the problem is that, although Rand rejects psychological egoism, she seems to regard genuine goodwill towards, or love of, others as an offshoot of proper self-love, as though there were no independent source of love for others in human nature."
A criticism they report (§3): "Another criticism of Rand’s theory of rights is that it is incompatible with her ethical egoism, because respect for others’ rights cannot be justified as a means to the ultimate end of one’s survival (Mack 1984; Flew 1984)."
Their reply: "On our eudaimonistic interpretation of Rand’s ethical egoism and theory of rights, there is no incompatibility between the two."

**Rational egoism: Sidgwick's dualism and Parfit's challenge.**
[Sidgwick](../thinkers/sidgwick.md) (*Methods of Ethics*, 7th ed. 1907,
Concluding Chapter §1): "It would be contrary to Common Sense to deny that the distinction between any one individual and any other is real and fundamental,"
and "I do not see how it can be proved that this distinction is not to be taken as fundamental in determining the ultimate end of rational action for an individual."
Schultz (SEP "Henry Sidgwick", §3): "In fact, the first edition of the Methods concludes that rational egoism and utilitarianism end up in a stand-off, that there is a “dualism of the practical reason”,"
Sidgwick names his ally (Preface to the Second Edition): "I hold with Butler that “Reasonable Self-love and Conscience are the two chief or superior principles in the nature of man,” each of which we are under a “manifest obligation” to obey:"
Shaver (§3) reports that Sidgwick's passage answers a charge: "A different problem for rational egoism is that it appears arbitrary."
A second problem Shaver reports (§3): "According to the instrumental theory, I ought to perform some action if and only if, and because, performing that action maximizes the satisfaction of my preferences."
"This conflict with the instrumental theory is a major problem for rational egoism." (Shaver's assessment.)
Shaver (§3): "Debate over rational egoism was revitalized by Parfit 1984 pts. II-III."
Parfit (§55): "The Self-interest Theory is not pure. It is a hybrid theory. S rejects the requirement of personal neutrality, but requires temporal neutrality."
The rival Shaver reports: "According to the present-aim theory, I have most reason to do what maximizes the satisfaction of my present desires."
Parfit's conclusion (§150): "Part Two argued that we should reject the Self-interest Theory about rationality."
Schultz (§3) reports a later exchange: "Parfit, too, argued that Sidgwick’s two standpoints approach failed to capture how egoistic reasons can be weaker than omnipersonal ones (Parfit 2011a,b), though Smith (2009) has defended Sidgwick’s construction of the problem."
Shaver's own conclusions (§4), reported as his: "Prospects for psychological egoism are dim."
"Ethical egoists do best by defending rational egoism instead."
"Rational egoism faces objections from arbitrariness, Nagel and Rachels, Parfit, Ewing, and evolutionary debunking. These worries are not decisive."

## Related terms

- [Prisoner's dilemma](../problems/prisoners-dilemma.md) — Parfit (§41): "In Prisoner’s Dilemmas, the Self-interest Theory is directly collectively self-defeating. In these cases, if we all pursue self-interest, this will be worse for all of us."
  And (§32): "In Prisoner’s Dilemmas, S is individually successful. Since it is only collectively self-defeating, S does not fail in its own terms."
  Cudd & Eftekhari (SEP "Contractarianism", §3) summarise Gauthier: "However, moral constraint on the pursuit of individual self-interest is required because cooperative activities almost inevitably lead to a prisoner’s dilemma: a situation in which the best individual outcomes can be had by those who cheat on the agreement while the others keep their part of the bargain."

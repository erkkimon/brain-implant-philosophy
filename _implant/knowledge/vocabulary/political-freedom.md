# Political freedom

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Political freedom** is used here as a pointer term: freedom as it bears
on a person's relation to government and public life, in whichever sense a
cited source gives it. A sentence elsewhere in the implant that says
"political freedom" without a qualifier refers to the family of senses
listed below; a sentence that relies on one sense names it ("civil and
political rights" in the ICCPR's sense, "political freedom" in Hayek's
sense, freedom as action in Arendt's sense, Sen's "political freedoms") and
whose it is. The general dispute over what liberty is (negative, positive,
republican, triadic) is on [Liberty (positive and negative)](liberty-positive-and-negative.md)
and is not repeated here.

Why a pointer: the sources do not agree on what the phrase names. Hayek
(1960, ch. 1 §2, p. 61) uses it for one thing, participation in
government, and holds that it is distinct from individual freedom; the
ICCPR (1966) lists freedoms of thought, expression, assembly and
association beside the right to vote; Arendt (1961) locates freedom in
action among others; Sen (1999) counts political freedoms both as part of
development and as means to it; Marx (1844) calls political emancipation
"a big step forward" but not human emancipation (quotations and locators
below). Adopting any of these as the implant's own would take a side
between the liberal, participatory and Marxist positions reported
below ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).
Granularity: the senses differ in who is free (an individual, a citizen, a
people), from what, and whether participation is the freedom itself or a
means to something else, so the pointer is split at least that finely.

Fixed 2026-10-15 ([journal](../../journals/2026-10-15.md)).

## Descriptive definitions

| Sense | Political freedom is | Source (as reported) |
|---|---|---|
| Listed civil and political rights | freedoms of thought, expression, assembly, association; voting and taking part in public affairs | ICCPR 1966, Arts. 18–25 |
| Participation | sharing in the choice of government, legislation, control of administration | Hayek 1960 (who contrasts it with personal freedom) |
| Action in public | beginning something new together with others | Arendt 1961, 1963 |
| Instrumental freedom | opportunities to choose and criticise rulers, as one of five instrumental freedoms | Sen 1999 |
| Political emancipation | the grant of liberal rights and liberties, set against human emancipation | Marx 1844 |
| Measured freedom | a score on political-rights and civil-liberties indicators | Freedom House |

- **The rights listed in the ICCPR (1966).** The International Covenant on
  Civil and Political Rights names the ideal in its preamble: "Recognizing that, in accordance with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the ideal of free human beings enjoying civil and political freedom and freedom from fear and want can only be achieved if conditions are created whereby everyone may enjoy his civil and political rights, as well as his economic, social and cultural rights,"
  Its articles include freedom of thought, conscience and religion (Art.
  18) and "2. Everyone shall have the right to freedom of expression; this right shall include freedom to seek, receive and impart information and ideas of all kinds, regardless of frontiers, either orally, in writing or in print, in the form of art, or through any other media of his choice." (Art. 19(2));
  "The right of peaceful assembly shall be recognized." (Art. 21);
  "1. Everyone shall have the right to freedom of association with others, including the right to form and join trade unions for the protection of his interests." (Art. 22(1));
  and, for citizens, "(a) To take part in the conduct of public affairs, directly or through freely chosen representatives;"
  and "(b) To vote and to be elected at genuine periodic elections which shall be by universal and equal suffrage and shall be held by secret ballot, guaranteeing the free expression of the will of the electors;"
  (Art. 25). The freedoms are not unconditional in the text: of expression
  it says "It may therefore be subject to certain restrictions, but these shall only be such as are provided by law and are necessary:"
  (Art. 19(3)). Source: `raw/un-iccpr-1966-civil-and-political-rights-arts-18-25.md`.
- **Participation in government (Hayek's report of the common usage).**
  Hayek, *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960; definitive ed. 2011, ch. 1
  §2, p. 61): "It is what is commonly called “political freedom,” the participation of men in the choice of their government, in the process of legislation, and in the control of administration."
  Hayek's own term is individual or personal freedom: "The state in which a man is not subject to coercion by the arbitrary will of another or others"
  (ch. 1 §1, p. 58).
  He holds the two apart: "nor need one share in this collective freedom to be free as an individual" (pp. 61–62),
  and "Clearly, a slave will not become free if he obtains merely the right to vote, nor will any degree of “inner freedom” make him anything but a slave—however much idealist philosophers have tried to convince us to the contrary."
  (p. 71). The contrary view he names is Laski's, which he quotes in ch. 1
  n. 11: "the right . . . to the franchise is essential to liberty; and a citizen excluded from it is unfree."
  Source: `raw/hayek-1960-constitution-of-liberty-ch1-political-freedom.md`.
- **Freedom as action in public (Arendt).** Arendt, "What Is Freedom?"
  (*Between Past and Future*, 1961, p. 146), holds that freedom "is actually the reason that men live together in political organization at all."
  and that "its field of experience is action."
  She derives inner freedom from the public kind: "We first become aware of freedom or its opposite in our intercourse with others, not in the intercourse with ourselves."
  (p. 148), and identifies freedom with acting: "Men are free—as distinguished from their possessing the gift for freedom—as long as they act, neither before nor after; for to be free and to act are the same."
  (p. 153). Tömmel and d'Entreves (SEP "Hannah Arendt", Fall 2024, §4.3)
  report: "By freedom Arendt does not mean the ability to choose among a set of possible alternatives, but spontaneity, i.e. the capacity to begin, to start something new, to do the unexpected, with which all human beings are endowed by virtue of being born."
  On *On Revolution*, they report: "Her claim is that “revolutions are the only political events which confront us directly and inevitably with the problem of beginning,” (OR, 21) since they represent the attempt to found a new political space, a space where freedom can appear as a worldly reality."
  and (§5.1) the public sphere as "The first is the space of appearance, a space of political freedom and equality which comes into being whenever citizens act in concert through the medium of speech and persuasion."
  Tömmel and d'Entreves (§5.2) report her proposal: "Arendt proposed a federated system of councils through which citizens could effectively determine their own political affairs."
  Sources: `raw/arendt-1961-what-is-freedom-action-public.md`,
  `raw/sep-arendt-fall-2024-freedom-natality-public-sphere.md`,
  `raw/iep-arendt-yar-revolution-and-critics.md`.
- **Political freedoms as constitutive and instrumental (Sen).** Sen,
  *Development as Freedom* (1999, p. 36): "In this approach, expansion of freedom is viewed as both (1) the primary end and (2) the principal means of development."
  Political freedoms head his list (p. 38): "In particular, I shall consider the following types of instrumental freedoms: (1) political freedoms, (2) economic facilities, (3) social opportunities, (4) transparency guarantees and (5) protective security."
  His definition: "Political freedoms, broadly conceived (including what are called civil rights), refer to the opportunities that people have to determine who should govern and on what principles, and also include the possibility to scrutinize and criticize authorities, to have freedom of political expression and an uncensored press, to enjoy the freedom to choose between different political parties, and so on."
  An instrumental claim he makes (p. 51): "Indeed, no substantial famine has ever occurred in a democratic country—no matter how poor."
  Source: `raw/sen-1999-development-as-freedom-political-freedoms.md`.
- **Political emancipation (Marx) and "formal" freedom.** Marx, "On the
  Jewish Question" (1844, Part I, Marxists Internet Archive English text):
  "Political emancipation is, of course, a big step forward. True, it is not the final form of human emancipation in general, but it is the final form of human emancipation within the hitherto existing world order."
  Wolff and Leopold (SEP "Karl Marx", Fall 2024, §2.4) gloss the
  distinction: "Marx makes one of the most enduring arguments from his early writings, by means of introducing a distinction between political emancipation—essentially the grant of liberal rights and liberties—and human emancipation."
  The term "formal freedom" in this connection appears in Sen (1999, p. 29),
  quoting V. K. Ramachandran: "Marx distinguishes between (to use the term used by Jon Elster) the formal freedom of the worker under capitalism and the real unfreedom of workers in pre-capitalist systems:"
  Sources: `raw/marx-1844-on-the-jewish-question-political-emancipation.md`,
  `raw/sep-marx-liberalism-capability-fall-2024-formal-and-real-freedom.md`.
- **Freedom as measured by indices (Freedom House).** Freedom House
  describes its report: "Freedom in the World is an annual global report on political rights and civil liberties, composed of numerical ratings and descriptive texts for each country and a select group of territories."
  Its scoring: "A country or territory is awarded 0 to 4 points for each of 10 political rights indicators and 15 civil liberties indicators, which take the form of questions; a score of 0 represents the smallest degree of freedom and 4 the greatest degree of freedom."
  with the two totals equally weighted to give a status of Free, Partly
  Free or Not Free.
  It states its premise: "Freedom in the World operates from the assumption that freedom for all people is best achieved in liberal democratic societies."
  Source: `raw/freedom-house-2024-methodology-and-critics.md` (2024 edition
  page, Wayback snapshot).

## Collisions

- **Personal freedom vs political freedom (Hayek vs Laski, as Hayek
  reports).** Hayek's separation of the two and Laski's contrary
  statement are quoted above. Hayek (n. 10) traces the separation of
  ruling-and-being-ruled from living as one chooses to Aristotle,
  *Politics* 6.3. Hayek's account has its critics: his editor Hamowy writes of his 1971 article "in which I argue that Hayek’s claim that the rule of law is a sufficient condition for a free society is untenable inasmuch as the criteria he posits for the rule of law are, if analyzed, consistent with a regime that even Hayek would regard as clearly unfree."
  (Introductory Essay, n. 28). Both are reported; the implant ranks
  neither.
- **Liberal rights vs human emancipation (Marx and his readers).** Marx:
  "But, the right of man to liberty is based not on the association of man with man, but on the separation of man from man."
  Wolff and Leopold (§2.4) report the reach of the argument: "However, pushing matters deeper, in an argument reinvented by innumerable critics of liberalism, Marx argues that not only is political emancipation insufficient to bring about human emancipation, it is in some sense also a barrier."
  and that "Now we should be clear that Marx does not oppose political emancipation, for he sees that liberalism is a great improvement on the systems of feudalism and religious prejudice and discrimination which existed in the Germany of his day."
  They add (their assessment): "Unfortunately, Marx never tells us what human emancipation is, although it is clear that it is closely related to the ideas of non-alienated labour and meaningful community."
- **Arendt's public freedom and its critics.** Yar (IEP "Hannah Arendt")
  reports critics of her concept by group: "Feminists have pointed out that the confinement of the political to the realm outside the household has been part and parcel of the domination of politics by men, and the corresponding exclusion of women’s experiences of subjection from legitimate politics."
  "Marxists have likewise pointed to the consequences of confining matters of material distribution and economic management to the extra-political realm of the oikos, thereby delegitimating questions of material social justice, poverty, and exploitation from political discussion and contestation."
  and "Likewise, the emphasis she places upon direct citizen deliberation as synonymous with the exercise of political freedom excludes representative models, and might be seen as unworkable in the context of modern mass societies, with the delegation, specialization, expertise and extensive divisions of labor needed to deal with their complexity."
- **Measured freedom vs the concept (index critics).** Freedom House
  states: "Although an element of subjectivity is unavoidable in such an enterprise, the ratings process emphasizes methodological consistency, intellectual rigor, and balanced and unbiased judgments."
  Giannone (2010, *Democratization* 17(1): 68–97,
  [doi:10.1080/13510340903453716](https://doi.org/10.1080/13510340903453716);
  abstract read, article not read) states his aim as to delineate "the political background of FH, thus underlining its neoconservative bias." and concludes:
  "Our findings show that because of the changes in methodology and the strict interconnection between methodological and political aspects, FH data do not offer an unbroken and politically neutral time series, such that their use for cross-time analyses both for research and policy is questionable."
  Munck and Verkuilen (2002, *Comparative Political Studies* 35(1): 5–34,
  [doi:10.1177/001041400203500101](https://doi.org/10.1177/001041400203500101);
  abstract read) assess democracy indices in general: "The authors’ conclusion is that constructors of democracy indices tend to be quite self-conscious about methodological issues but that even the best indices suffer from important weaknesses."
  Structural note: Freedom House's civil-liberties list includes property
  and business ("G2: Are individuals able to exercise the right to own property and establish private businesses without undue interference from state or nonstate actors?"),
  an item not among the ICCPR articles quoted above.
- **Political freedom vs free will; ancient vs modern liberty.** Arendt
  (1961, p. 148) derives inner freedom from public freedom (quoted above). Carter's separation of political liberty from free will, and
  Constant's ancient/modern contrast, are reported on
  [Liberty (positive and negative)](liberty-positive-and-negative.md), not here.

# Commensurability (ethics)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Commensurability** is used here as a pointer term: whether two values,
or two bearers of value, can be measured or compared by a common measure,
in whichever sense a cited source gives it. Hsieh and Andersson (SEP 2021,
preamble) state the core: "Values, such as liberty and equality, are sometimes said to be incommensurable in the sense that their value cannot be reduced to a common measure. And options, such as studying to become a lawyer or studying to become a philosopher, are sometimes said to be incomparable in the sense that neither option seems to be at least as good as the other."
On every page the sense is named (cardinal, ordinal, Griffin's,
Richardson's strong or weak, Raz's, Chang's, Berlin's, or the scientific
sense of the Collisions section), and **incommensurability** and
**incomparability** are not used interchangeably without saying whose
usage is followed.

Why this definition: what "common measure" requires is itself disputed,
and so is the relation of incommensurability to incomparability. Hsieh and
Andersson (§1.2) report the split: "Some authors use the terms interchangeably (e.g., Raz 1986). Others use them to refer to distinct concepts (e.g., Chang 1997b)."
They also state their own recommendation (§1.1, their choice): "This entry encourages the use of “incommensurable” in the etymologically correct way i.e., to refer to the lack of a cardinal scale."
Adopting the cardinal, the ordinal or the umbrella sense as the implant's
own would take a side in that terminological debate and in the debates
that depend on it — whether monism or pluralism about value holds, whether
choice between incomparable options can be justified, and whether
[moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md) show incommensurability
(Hsieh & Andersson, §§3, 3.2, 4). The implant reports each sense with its
holders and critics ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Excerpts: `raw/sep-value-incommensurable-fall-2024-incommensurability-incomparability-parity.md`,
`raw/sep-value-pluralism-berlin-fall-2024-incommensurability.md`.
The books of Raz (*The Morality of Freedom*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1986),
Griffin (*Well-Being*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1986) and Chang (ed.,
*Incommensurability, Incomparability, and Practical Reason*, Cambridge,
MA: Harvard University Press, 1997) were not read; they are reported as
the SEP authors report them. Listed by the structure of the sources, not
ranked.

1. **Cardinal sense: no common unit.** Hsieh and Andersson (§1.1) trace
   the word to mathematics: "The term “incommensurable” suggests the lack of a common measure. This idea has its historical roots in mathematics."
   Their example is the side and diagonal of a unit square, which can be
   ranked ordinally but not, without irrational numbers, measured by a
   common unit (§1.1). Holders they name: "Given these historical roots, some authors reserve the term “incommensurable” for comparisons that can be made, but not cardinally (Stocker 1980, 176; Stocker 1997, 203; Chang 1997b, 2)."
   Chang's definition as Mason (SEP 2023, §4) reports it: "She provides clear definitions of each: incommensurability is the lack of a common unit of value by which precise comparisons can be made. Two items are incomparable, if there is no possible relation of comparison, such as ‘better than’, or ‘as good as’ (1997, Introduction)."
2. **Ordinal sense: neither better nor equally good.** The broader
   reading Hsieh and Andersson report (§1.1): "Values or bearers of value are then incommensurable only when not even an ordinal comparison or ranking is possible (e.g., Raz 1986; Rabinowicz 2021a)."
   "On this interpretation, incommensurability is defined as the relation that holds between two items when neither is better than the other nor are they equally as good."
   Raz's example, as they report it (§1.2): "Neither career seems better than the other, and they also do not appear to be equally good. If they were of equal value, then a slightly improved version of the legal career would be better than the musical career, but this judgment appears incorrect (Raz 1986, 332)."
   Raz distinguishes this from "indeterminacy" of value, which he takes to be vagueness: "Raz regards the indeterminacy of value to result from the “general indeterminacy of language” (1986, 324)." (§2.1)
3. **Umbrella sense.** Hsieh and Andersson (§1.1): "Others have not given an exact definition of the term, but use it as an inclusive umbrella term for comparability problems in general."
   Their own §3 follows each author's word (§3): "In this section, the terminology of the discussed authors will be respected, and thus “incommensurability” will sometimes be used, even though, the authors possibly refer to the phenomenon of incomparability."
4. **Griffin: trumping and discontinuity.** The first of three conceptions
   Hsieh and Andersson list (§1.3): "In a conflict between values A and B, A is said to trump B if “any amount of A, no matter how small, is more valuable than any amount of B, no matter how large” (Griffin 1986, 83)."
   The weaker form: "Two values, A and B, are incommensurable in this sense if “so long as we have enough of B any amount of A outranks any further amount of B; or that enough of A outranks any amount of B” (Griffin 1986, 85)."
   Mason (§2.1) reports that "Griffin denies that pluralism entails incommensurability (Griffin uses the term incomparability) whereas other pluralists embrace incommensurability, but deny that it entails that rational choice is impossible."
   Griffin's "super-scale", quoted by Mason (§4.2): "…it does not follow from there being no super-value that there is no super-scale."
5. **No general trade-off: strong and weak commensurability.** The second
   conception (§1.3), in Wiggins's words: "He writes that two values are incommensurable if “there is no general way in which A and B trade off in the whole range of situations of choice and comparison in which they figure” (1997, 59)."
   Richardson's two theses (1994, as reported): "Strong commensurability is the thesis that there is a true ranking of the realization of one value against the realization of the other value in terms of one common value across all conflicts of value."
   and "Weak commensurability is the thesis that in any given conflict of values, there is a true ranking of the realization of one value against the realization of the other value in terms of some value."
   "Donald Regan defends the thesis of strong commensurability (Regan 1997)." The
   third conception: "The third conception of value incommensurability denies both strong and weak commensurability (Richardson 1994, Wiggins 1997, Williams 1981)."
6. **Constitutive incommensurability (Raz).** Hsieh and Andersson (§3.1)
   report Raz's case of an offer of money to leave one's spouse for a
   month: "In this case, “what has symbolic significance is the very judgment that companionship is incommensurable with money” (1986, 350)."
   Critic side by side: "For example, Chang objects that incommensurability appears to become relative to one’s participation in social conventions (2001, 48)."
   They report Elizabeth Anderson's pragmatist argument for constitutive
   incommensurability (1997) and Chang's replies to each of its three
   points (2001) in the same section.
7. **Incomparability: no positive value relation.** Hsieh and Andersson
   adopt Chang's usage (§1.2, their choice): "This entry will use the term “incomparable” to refer to the possibility that no positive value relation holds between two value bearers (Chang 1997b)."
   It is relative to a respect: "Incomparability is often taken to be a three-place relation: A is incomparable with B with respect to V."
   and differs from noncomparability: "If no single covering consideration is applicable to the things we are comparing, then the things are noncomparable."
   The argument for it, which Chang names (§2): "The Small Improvement Argument takes the following general form: “if (1) A is neither better nor worse than B (with respect to V), (2) A+ is better than A (with respect to V), (3) A+ is not better than B (with respect to V), then (4) A and B are not related by any of the standard trichotomy of relations (relativized to V)” where V represents the relevant set of considerations for purposes of the comparison (Chang 2002b, 667–668)."
   They name Raz, Parfit (1984) and Sinnott-Armstrong (1985) among its
   advancers (§2).
8. **Rough equality and parity: comparable after all.** Against reading
   such cases as incomparable (§2.2): "James Griffin and Derek Parfit argue that items may in fact be “roughly equal” and hence comparable (Griffin 1986, 80–81, 96–98, and 104; 1997, 38–39; 2000, 285–289; Parfit 1987, 431)."
   Chang's fourth relation ("The Possibility of Parity", *Ethics* 112,
   2002, 659–688, doi:[10.1086/339673](https://doi.org/10.1086/339673)): "Two items are said to be on a par if neither is better than the other, their differences preclude their being equally good, and yet they are comparable."
   Her example: "Imagine comparing Mozart and Michelangelo in terms of creativity."
   Critics side by side: "The soundness of this so-called Chaining Argument has, however, been questioned (Boot 2009; Elson 2014a; Andersson 2016a)."
   and "In the case of “on a par,” Joshua Gert and Erik Carlson have argued that it can be defined in terms of the three standard comparative relations (Gert 2004; 2015; Carlson 2010)."
   A third reading treats the cases as vagueness (§2.1): "In contrast, other philosophers argue for interpreting alleged examples of incomparability as vagueness (Griffin 1986, 96; Broome 1997, 2000; Andersson 2017; Elson 2017; Dos Santos 2019)."
9. **Pluralist sense (Berlin).** Mason (§4.4): "One philosopher who is happy to accept that there may be situations where we just cannot make reasoned choices between plural values is Isaiah Berlin, who claimed that goods such as liberty and equality conflict at the fundamental level."
   Cherniss and Hardy (SEP 2022, §4.1): "In speaking of the incommensurability of values, Berlin seems to have meant that there is no common measure, no ‘common currency’ in terms of which the relative importance of any two values can be established in the abstract."
   Their reading of its strength (§4.4, their assessment): "One can make a three-way distinction, between weak incommensurability, moderate incommensurability and radical incommensurability."
   and "Berlin goes further than this, but it is not clear whether he presents a moderate or a radical version of incommensurability."
   On the radical reading, choice rests on what "Berlin sometimes calls ‘plumping’" (§4.4).
10. **Monist denial.** Hsieh and Andersson (§3): "After all, the most direct argument against the possibility that values are incommensurable or incomparable is based on value monism (e.g., Mill 1861 [1979] and Sidgwick 1874 [1981])."
    Mason (§4) reports the pluralist reply in Williams's words about the
    assumption that weighing needs a common consideration (Williams 1985,
    p. 17): "This assumption is at once very powerful and utterly baseless."
    Pages: [Mill](../thinkers/mill.md), [Sidgwick](../thinkers/sidgwick.md),
    [Williams](../thinkers/williams.md), [Parfit](../thinkers/parfit.md).

*Structural note (this implant):* the relation between the two words, as
Hsieh and Andersson (§1.2) state it, is a one-way entailment: "From the fact that two things are incommensurable i.e., lack a common unit of measurement, it does not follow that they are incomparable."
and "However, when there is incomparability there is also incommensurability."
Writing *C* for *a positive value relation holds* and *M* for *a common
cardinal unit exists*, their two sentences are ¬*C* → ¬*M* and the
denial that ¬*M* → ¬*C*. `logic.py check --premises "~C -> ~M"`: with
`--conclusion "M -> C"` it prints `VALID`; with `--conclusion "~M -> ~C"`
it prints `INVALID`, counterexample `C=T, M=F`. This reads the words in
the cardinal sense (1); in the ordinal sense (2) Raz uses them interchangeably.

## Collisions

- **Incommensurable vs incomparable.** Mason (§4): "Making a similar point, Ruth Chang points out that incommensurability is often conflated with incomparability."
  and "Chang points out that incommensurability is often thought to entail incomparability, but it does not."
  A page that writes "incommensurable" for Raz's career case is using the
  ordinal sense (2); in Chang's and Hsieh–Andersson's terms the case is
  one of alleged incomparability (7). Hsieh and Andersson (§1.2, their
  assessment) add: "Incommensurability on the other hand is mostly of theoretical interest."
- **Scientific incommensurability (Kuhn, Feyerabend).** Hsieh and
  Andersson (§1): "Incommensurability between values must be distinguished from the kind of incommensurability associated with Paul Feyerabend (1978, 1981, 1993) and Thomas Kuhn (1977, 1983, 1996) in epistemology and the philosophy of science."
  Oberheim and Hoyningen-Huene (SEP 2024, preamble;
  [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/incommensurability/);
  excerpt `raw/sep-incommensurability-fall-2024-kuhn-feyerabend-scientific-sense.md`): "The metaphorical application of the mathematical notion specifically to the relation between successive scientific theories became controversial in 1962 after it was popularised by two influential philosophers of science: Thomas Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend."
  On Kuhn (§1): "Kuhn developed and refined his initial idea over the following decades, repeatedly emphasizing that incommensurability neither means nor implies incomparability; nor does it make science irrational (e.g. Kuhn 2000 [1970], 155ff.)."
  On Feyerabend (§1): "By calling two rival theories incommensurable, Feyerabend meant that they are ‘deductively disjoint’ due to meaning variance in the concepts used to state them."
  This implant uses "commensurability" in that sense only where a page
  says so.
- **Value pluralism vs political pluralism.** Mason (preamble): "Moral value pluralism should be distinguished from political pluralism."
  Mason (§4.4) places Berlin across the line: "Berlin is primarily concerned with political pluralism, and with defending political liberalism, but his views about incomparability have been very influential in discussions on moral pluralism."
- **Incommensurable options vs identical options.** Raz, quoted by Mason
  (§4.3, Raz 1999, p. 48), sets choice among incommensurate goods beside
  the case of identical items: "When I choose one tin of soup from a row of identical tins in the shop, it would be wrong and misleading to say that I wanted that tin rather than, or in preference to, the others."
  Ullmann-Margalit (2006, p. 171; excerpt
  `raw/ullmann-margalit-2006-big-decisions-picking-and-1977-record.md`)
  puts both under picking: "One chooses for reasons; one picks when reasons cannot prevail. This happens when the alternatives are entirely symmetrical (or incommensurate)."
  The symmetrical case is the one of [Buridan's ass](../problems/buridans-ass.md);
  her sentence names the two cases side by side, joined by "or".

## Related terms

- [Value](value.md) — what is or is not commensurable, per Hsieh and Andersson (§1): "In contrast, contemporary inquiry into value incommensurability concerns comparisons among abstract values (such as liberty or equality) or particular bearers of value (such as a certain institution or its effects on liberty or equality)."
- [Moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md) — "Henry Richardson, for example, takes the situation confronting Sophie in the novel Sophie’s Choice—that one of her two children will be spared death, but only if she chooses which one to save—to point to the incommensurability of values (1994, 115–117)."
  (Hsieh & Andersson, §3.2); against: "James Griffin, for example, argues that the feature of “irreplaceability” in moral dilemmas often may be mistaken as evidence for incomparability (1997, 37)."
  See also [Dilemma](dilemma.md) for the term's senses.
- [Buridan's ass](../problems/buridans-ass.md) — choice between
  symmetrical options, which Ullmann-Margalit (2006) groups with
  incommensurate ones under "picking" (Collisions above).
- [Liberty (positive and negative)](liberty-positive-and-negative.md) —
  Berlin's "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1969) is the work Hsieh and
  Andersson cite for his value pluralism (§3, bibliography); liberty and
  equality are Berlin's example pair of conflicting goods (Mason, §4.4).
- **Justified choice** — whether incomparability blocks it is disputed:
  "Consequently, if two alternatives are incomparable no justified choice can be made between them." (comparativism, §4.1)
  against "For Joseph Raz, incomparability also does not pose a problem for the possibility of justified choice (1997)." (§4.3);
  Raz's classical conception "“regards reasons as rendering options eligible” (Raz 1999, p. 47)" (Mason, §4.3).

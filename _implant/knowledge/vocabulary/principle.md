# Principle (ethics)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Principle**, on ethics pages, is used as a pointer term: a general moral
claim that a cited source offers as explaining why actions are right or
wrong, as guiding action, or as both. The three roles are McKeever and
Ridge's (SEP 2023, §2): "At least three conceptions of principles are worth distinguishing. First, there are principles qua standards."
Their second and third conceptions: "Second, there are principles qua guides. These purport to be well suited to guiding action. Third, there are principles purporting to play both of these roles simultaneously—action-guiding standards."
On every page the sense is named (absolute or contributory; standard,
guide or both; the author whose principle it is).

Why this definition: whether moral thought needs principles at all is
itself the debate between generalists and particularists. Dancy (SEP 2017,
preamble) defines one side: "Ethical generalism is the view that the rationality of moral thought and judgement depends on a suitable provision of moral principles."
McKeever and Ridge (§2) state the split: "Particularists are united in their opposition to moral principles and generalists are united in their allegiance to them."
A definition that made principles exceptionless laws, or one that made
them mere rules of thumb, would settle that debate by definition, and with
it the pages that depend on it ([Dancy](../thinkers/dancy.md),
[Hare](../thinkers/hare.md), the principle pages listed under Related
terms). The implant reports each conception with its holders and critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Listed in the order of the brief, not ranked.

1. **Absolute and contributory principles.** Dancy (SEP, §1) separates two
   conceptions. The first: "The first conception, the ‘absolute’ conception, takes a moral principle to be a universal claim to the effect that all actions of a certain type are overall wrong (or right)."
   The second: "There is a very different way of understanding a moral principle, as ‘contributory’ rather than as absolute."
   Dancy (§4) argues against a morality of absolute principles only: "The first reason is that absolute principles cannot conflict, and that if they cannot conflict a vital aspect of our moral lives (that is, conflict) has been left out of account altogether by any theory that supposes that morality is entirely governed by absolute principles."

2. **Standards, guides, action-guiding standards.** McKeever and Ridge
   (§2): "Standards purport to offer explanations of why given actions are right or wrong, why a given consideration is a reason with a certain valence and weight, why a given character trait is a virtue, and the like."
   They place historical principles in these roles (their reading): "In the utilitarian tradition in moral philosophy, the principle of utility (however it is formulated) is characteristically understood as a standard."
   And: "Kant, on the other hand, seemed to have understood the categorical imperative as a kind of action-guiding standard."
   The [categorical imperative](../positions/categorical-imperative.md) is
   Kant's supreme principle on Johnson and Cureton's account (SEP "Kant's
   Moral Philosophy", preamble): "Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) argued that the supreme principle of morality is a principle of practical rationality that he dubbed the “Categorical Imperative” (CI)."

3. **Generalism and particularism, side by side.** The particularist,
   in Dancy's own words (preamble): "Moral Particularism, at its most trenchant, is the claim that there are no defensible moral principles, that moral thought does not consist in the application of moral principles to cases, and that the morally perfect person should not be conceived as the person of principle."
   Its core doctrine (§3): "This is the doctrine that what is a reason in one case may be no reason at all in another, or even a reason on the other side."
   The generalist, in McKeever and Ridge's book *Principled Ethics*
   (2006; [doi:10.1093/0199290652.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199290652.001.0001)),
   as its publisher abstract states: "It argues that in so far as moral knowledge and wisdom are possible, all of morality can and should be codified in a manageable set of principles, even if we are not yet in possession of those principles."
   The abstract names the view "generalism as a regulative ideal" and
   adds: "The book resists the excesses of hyper-generalist views according to which moral thought is constituted by allegiance to a particular principle or set of principles."
   McKeever and Ridge (SEP, §3) report the generalists' reply to the
   argument from holism: "Generalists have rejected the inference on the grounds that holism leaves open the possibility that the behaviour of reasons, defeaters, enablers, and intensifiers/attenuators is codifiable (see Väyrynen 2004; McKeever and Ridge 2005)."
   And the particularists' counter: "Particularists have countered that even if holism is logically consistent with principles it would nevertheless render them “cosmic accidents” (Dancy 2004: 82)."
   Dancy (§8) reports the objection that "a particularist morality is a lax morality: without principles, anything goes."
   His reply: "But really the remedy for poor moral judgement is not a different style of moral judgement, principle-based judgement, but just better moral judgement."

4. **Prima facie duties (W. D. Ross).** Skelton (SEP, preamble): "The most innovative element of this theory is the notion of a prima facie duty."
   Ross's five duties are fidelity, reparation, gratitude, beneficence and
   non-maleficence (§4.1), and "Ross’s major innovation involves characterising these as prima facie duties rather than absolute or exceptionless duties."
   They are not the final verdict (§4.1): "Prima facie duties do not state our actual obligation or duty proper – the thing we ultimately ought do – in a particular situation (RG 20)."
   Skelton quotes Ross's own use of the word: "He writes: ‘We think the principle ‘do evil to no one’ more pressing than the principle ‘do good to every one’, except when the evil is very substantially outweighed by the good’ (FE 75)."
   Dancy (SEP, §5) names Ross's theory "The classic example" of
   contributory principles and writes: "Particularists applaud Ross’s insistence that there can be many features of the situation each of which makes some difference to how one should act; they merely want to say that the matter is not regular in the way that Ross, as a generalist, supposes."

5. **Universal prescriptions (R. M. Hare).** Price (SEP, §4): "The two features of prescriptivity and universalizability remained the twin pillars of Hare’s theory ever afterwards."
   On the principles behind particular judgments (§4): "Hence, the speaker can rationally assent to a particular “ought”-statement only if it is derivable from some universal principle that he will accept if he gives impartial and positive weight to all preferences whose satisfaction would be affected by its observance."
   At Hare's "intuitive" level (§7) agents use "simple principles (often articulating emotional responses) whose acceptance can be justified at the critical level."
   McKeever and Ridge (§1) report that Dancy objected to an idea "which he took to be implicit in Hare’s universalizability principle, that if a consideration is a reason in one context then it is a reason with the same valence in any possible context in which it occurs."
   They add: "(This reading of Hare is open to objection. See McNaughton and Rawlings (2000) for discussion.)"

6. **The four principles of biomedical ethics.** Flynn (SEP, §4): "Beauchamp and Childress’ four principles are: the principle of autonomy (the value of self-direction regarding one’s life and choices), the principle of beneficence (the value of enhancing the welfare of others), the principle of nonmaleficence (the value of avoiding imposing harm on others), and the principle of justice (the value of according each person her due) (Beauchamp & Childress 2019a; Arras 2017: 5)."
   Their source (§4.2): "Since the third edition of PBE, the source of the four principles has been not high theory but the common morality."
   Flynn (§1) calls such approaches "mid-level theories, such as moral principlism and casuistry".
   Critics and replies, side by side (§4.3): "Such critics claim that without a clear prioritizing of principles, Beauchamp and Childress’ principles-centered approach lacks rigor and leaves too much room for intuitive judgments in cases where principles conflict."
   The reply: "Beauchamp and Childress respond, first, by rejecting the very idea that they are offering a philosophical theory, rather than a framework or practical guide. But perhaps more importantly, they doubt that any priority ranking of their principles would stand the test of time (Arras 2017: 6–9)."
   Flynn cites the critics as Gert, Culver and Clouser (2006). An earlier
   critique is K. D. Clouser and B. Gert, "A Critique of Principlism",
   *Journal of Medicine and Philosophy* 15(2): 219–236, 1990
   ([doi:10.1093/jmp/15.2.219](https://doi.org/10.1093/jmp/15.2.219));
   its bibliographic data are verified by DOI, its text was not read, and
   nothing is reported here about its content. A later critic,
   McMillan (2018), as Flynn reports him (§4.3): "McMillan claims that principle-centered methods cannot lead to the formulation of what he calls “reasoned convictions about moral problems”, and writes that the four principles approach hinders bringing moral reason to bear upon practical questions."

7. **Principles in [reflective equilibrium](../methods/reflective-equilibrium.md).** Knight (SEP, preamble): "In short, the method of reflective equilibrium is the mutual adjustment of principles and judgments in the light of relevant argument and theory."
   His distinctions (§1): "A first distinction is, then, that while principles necessarily generalize to more than one case (List & Valentini 2016; Slavny et al. 2021), judgments may be either general or particular. A second distinction is that judgments express an agent’s moral outlook while principles are candidate representations of that outlook (cf. Brun 2014)."
   On the standard view (§2.4): "Rawls and Daniels understand an equilibrium as between judgments and principles, so if there are no principles, there can be no equilibrium."
   Knight (§2.4) asks, as his own question: "It is not clear why the formal category of principles should be considered untouchable when everything else, including the entire content of all principles and the background theories by which they are assessed, is available for revision."
   Flynn (§4.1) reports that Beauchamp and Childress first gave the
   impression of a top-down order, in which "theory justified principles, that principles justified moral rules, and that rules justified moral judgments in particular cases."
   and later moved toward reflective equilibrium.

## Collisions

**Moral principle vs logical principle.** Horn (SEP "Contradiction", rev.
2024, preamble; [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/contradiction/))
uses the word for the first principles of logic: "This entry outlines the role of the Law of Non-Contradiction (LNC), or Principle of Non-Contradiction (PNC), as the foremost among the first (indemonstrable) principles of Aristotelian philosophy and its heirs"
Aristotle's phrase, as Horn quotes it (§1): "LNC—“the firmest of all principles”—is defined as follows: For the same thing to hold good and not to hold good simultanously of the same thing and in the same respect is impossible"
Dancy's definition of particularism (sense 3) is stated of "moral
principles"; the logical sense is the one used on [validity](validity.md).

**Moral principle vs law of nature.** Carroll (SEP "Laws of Nature", rev.
2020, preamble; [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/laws-of-nature/)):
"Science includes many principles at least once thought to be laws of nature: Newton’s law of gravitation, his three laws of motion, the ideal gas laws, Mendel’s laws, the laws of supply and demand, and so on."
A scientific principle states a regularity ("Einstein’s principle that no signals travel faster than light is also a true generalization", Carroll §1); a moral principle in the senses above states what is right, what counts as a reason, or what to do.

**Absolute vs prima facie.** The same word covers both conceptions. On
Dancy's absolute conception (sense 1) a principle is "a universal claim to the effect that all actions of a certain type are overall wrong (or right)";
Skelton (§4.1) describes Ross's duties as "prima facie duties rather than absolute or exceptionless duties" (sense 4); Flynn (§4.3) reports that Beauchamp and Childress "emphasize that their framework’s principles are all only prima facie binding." (sense 6). Pages name which conception a cited author holds.

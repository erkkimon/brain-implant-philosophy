# Justice

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Justice** is used here as a pointer term: what is due to each, in
whichever sense a cited source gives it. Miller (SEP 2021, §1) offers the
common core: "The most plausible candidate for a core definition comes from the Institutes of Justinian, a codification of Roman Law from the sixth century AD, where justice is defined as ‘the constant and perpetual will to render to each his due’."
On every page the sense is named (Platonic, Aristotelian distributive or
corrective, Humean, Rawlsian, entitlement, retributive, restorative,
global).

Why this definition: what is "due" is the question the sources dispute.
Miller (§2.1) reports the ambiguity: "This exposes an ambiguity in what it means to ‘render each his due’. What is ‘due’ might be what a person can reasonably expect to have given existing law, policy, or social practice, or it might be what the person should get under a regime of ideal justice: this could mean what the person deserves, or needs, or is entitled to on grounds of equality, depending on which ideal principle is being invoked."
His own conclusion (§7, his assessment) is that "no comprehensive theory of justice is available to us".
Adopting Rawls's, Nozick's, a desert-based or a utilitarian account as
the implant's own would take a side in that debate and in the debates
that depend on it ([egalitarianism](../positions/egalitarianism.md), the
[justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md),
[rights](rights.md)). The implant reports each sense with its holders and
critics ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Listed roughly by date of the source, not ranked.

1. **Justice in the Republic: conventional or of the soul.** In
   [Plato](../thinkers/plato.md)'s *Republic*, Brown (SEP 2017, §1.1)
   reports Thrasymachus' sense: "On Thrasymachus’ view (see especially 343c–344c), justice is conventionally established by the strong, in order that the weak will serve the interests of the strong."
   Glaucon's Gyges story puts the case for others (II 360c, Shorey): "And yet this is a great proof, one might argue, that no one is just of his own will but only from constraint, in the belief that justice is not his personal good, inasmuch as every man, when he supposes himself to have the power to do wrong, does wrong."
   Socrates' account, in the city (IV 433a, Shorey): "And again that to do one's own business and not to be a busybody is justice,"
   and in the soul, as Brown (§2.2) states it: "Finally, a person is just just in case all three parts of her soul are functioning as they should (441d12–e2; cf. 443c9–e2)."
   A doubt Brown reports (§2.2): "We might doubt that an answer concerning psychological justice is relevant to the question concerning practical justice (Sachs 1963)."
2. **Aristotle: universal and particular justice.** [Aristotle](../thinkers/aristotle.md),
   *Nicomachean Ethics* V (Ross tr.): "The just, then, is the lawful and the fair, the unjust the unlawful and the unfair." (V.1)
   Justice as lawfulness: "This form of justice, then, is complete virtue, but not absolutely, but in relation to our neighbour." (V.1)
   Particular justice divides (V.2): "Of particular justice and that which is just in the corresponding sense, (A) one kind is that which is manifested in distributions of honour or money or the other things that fall to be divided among those who have a share in the constitution (for in these it is possible for one man to have a share either unequal or equal to that of another), and (B) one is that which plays a rectifying part in transactions between man and man."
   Distributive: "This, then, is what the just is-the proportional; the unjust is what violates the proportion." (V.3)
   Rectificatory: "But the justice in transactions between man and man is a sort of equality indeed, and the injustice a sort of inequality; not according to that kind of proportion, however, but according to arithmetical proportion." (V.4)
   He also divides political justice into natural and legal (V.7) and
   calls equity "a correction of law where it is defective owing to its universality" (V.10).
3. **The Roman-law formula.** Justinian, *Institutes* 1.1 pr. (Moyle tr.,
   1913): "Justice is the set and constant purpose which gives to every man his due."
   The Latin: "Iustitia est constans et perpetua voluntas ius suum cuique tribuens."
   Miller's "constant and perpetual will" and Moyle's "set and constant
   purpose" both render *constans et perpetua voluntas*. Miller (§1) draws four aspects from
   it — individual claims, enforceable obligation, impartiality, agency:
   "Here there is a contrast with other virtues: we demand justice, but we beg for charity or forgiveness." (§1.2)
   "Justice is the opposite of arbitrariness." (§1.3)
   "So we cannot, except metaphorically, describe as unjust states of affairs that no agent has contributed to bringing about – unless we think that there is a Divine Being who has ordered the universe in such a way that every outcome is a manifestation of His will." (§1.4)
4. **Hume: an artificial virtue with circumstances.** [Hume](../thinkers/hume.md),
   *Treatise* 3.2.2: "Here then is a proposition, which, I think, may be regarded as certain, that it is only from the selfishness and confined generosity of men, along with the scanty provision nature has made for his wants, that justice derives its origin."
   And: "and may easily conclude, that if men were supplied with every thing in the same abundance, or if every one had the same affection and tender regard for every one as for himself; justice and injustice would be equally unknown among mankind."
   Miller (§1.1) quotes the *Enquiry* version: "Hume pointed out that in a hypothetical state of abundance where ‘every individual finds himself fully provided with whatever his most voracious appetites can want’, ‘the cautious, jealous virtue of justice would never once have been dreamed of’ (Hume, An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals, pp. 183–4)."
   Miller (§2.1) places Hume, with Hayek, at the conservative end, where
   justice is adherence to existing rules of property; Hayek's view, as he
   reports it: "For Hayek, to speak of ‘social justice’ as an ideal standard of distribution was as meaningless as to speak of a ‘moral stone’ (Hayek 1976, p. 78)"
5. **Utilitarian senses.** Miller (§4.1) quotes Bentham: "Bentham, in contrast, was more cavalier: ‘justice, in the only sense in which it has a meaning, is an imaginary personage, feigned for the convenience of discourse, whose dictates are the dictates of utility, applied to certain particular cases’ (The Principles of Morals and Legislation, pp. 125–6)."
   On Mill: "To explain the distinctiveness of justice, Mill suggests that it designates moral requirements that, because of their very great importance to human well-being, people have a right to have discharged, and are therefore matters of perfect obligation."
6. **Rawls: justice of the basic structure ("justice as fairness").**
   Miller (preamble): "Classically, justice was counted as one of the four cardinal virtues (and sometimes as the most important of the four); in modern times John Rawls famously described it as ‘the first virtue of social institutions’ (Rawls 1971, p.3; Rawls, 1999, p.3)."
   [Rawls](../thinkers/rawls.md)'s principles, as Wenar (SEP, §4.3) quotes
   them from *Justice as Fairness*: "First Principle: Each person has the same indefeasible claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties, which scheme is compatible with the same scheme of liberties for all;"
   and, of inequalities: "They are to be to the greatest benefit of the least-advantaged members of society (the difference principle) (JF, 42–43)."
7. **Distributive justice (modern sense).** Lamont & Favor (SEP 2017,
   preamble): "Arguments about which frameworks and/or resulting distributions are morally preferable constitute the topic of distributive justice."
   Miller (§2.2): "Distributive justice, on the other hand, is multilateral: it assumes a distributing agent, and a number of persons who have claims on what is being distributed. Justice here requires that the resources available to the distributor be shared according to some relevant criterion, such as equality, desert, or need."
   The egalitarian answers are on [egalitarianism](../positions/egalitarianism.md).
8. **Nozick: justice in holdings (entitlement).** [Nozick](../thinkers/nozick.md),
   *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), p. 151: "The complete principle of distributive justice would say simply that a distribution is just if everyone is entitled to the holdings they possess under the distribution."
   Lamont & Favor (§7): "For libertarians, just outcomes are those arrived at by the separate just actions of individuals; a particular distributive pattern is not required for justice."
   Miller (§2.3) classes the view as historical rather than end-state or
   patterned: "In Nozick’s particular case, a distribution of resources is said to be just if everyone within its scope is entitled to what they now own, having acquired it by legitimate means – such as voluntary contract or gift – from someone who was also entitled to have it, leading back eventually to a just act of acquisition – such as labouring on a plot of land – that gave the first owner his valid title."
9. **Corrective justice.** Miller (§2.2), tracing it to Aristotle's
   rectificatory justice: "Corrective justice, then, essentially concerns a bilateral relationship between a wrongdoer and his victim, and demands that the fault be cancelled by restoring the victim to the position she would have been in had the wrongful behaviour not occurred; it may also require that the wrongdoer not benefit from his faulty behaviour."
10. **Retributive justice.** Walen (SEP 2020, §3.2): "The positive desert claim holds that wrongdoers morally deserve punishment for their wrongful acts (see section 4.6 for a discussion of the deontic and consequentialist dimensions of this)."
    He reports (preamble) that its notions "remain contested and problematic"
    and (§3.1): "Retributivism has also often been conflated with revenge or the desire for vengeance."
    The debate is on [justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md).
11. **Restorative justice**, as reported by Duff & Hoskins (SEP 2021, §7): "Most prominently, many abolitionists look to ‘restorative justice’ as an alternative to punishment."
    "Advocates of restorative justice often contrast it with ‘retributive’ justice; they argue that we should look for restoration rather than retribution or punishment, and seek to repair harms caused rather than to inflict punitive suffering for wrongs done. But one might regard this as a false dichotomy (see Allais 2011; Duff 2011a)."
    The implant has no primary source of the restorative-justice movement
    on file; this sense is reported only through that entry.
12. **Global (international) justice.** Blake (SEP 2020, preamble): "International distributive justice has, in the past several decades, become a prominent topic within political philosophy."
    Miller (§3.2) ties it to whether justice is relational: "In particular it bears on the question whether there is such a thing as global distributive justice, or, in contrast, whether distributive principles only apply to people who are related together as members of the same society or citizens of the same state."
    Positions as Blake reports them: "Left Institutionalists agree on the following conclusion: that Rawls’s own limitation of his two principles of justice to the circumstances of a domestic society was a morally illegitimate constraint on his theory,"
    the nationalists, "committed only to the relatively modest conclusion that nationality has some moral relevance—and that the principles of distributive justice that are right within the nation may not be right at the global level (D. Miller 2007)."
    and Pogge (§4.2): "Pogge’s recent work (see, especially, 2002) has argued that the institutions we have built in the world are, in fact, directly violating the rights of the poor."

## Collisions

- **Justice vs legality.** Aristotle's first sense makes the just "the
  lawful" (V.1), yet he also calls equity "a correction of law where it is defective owing to its universality" (V.10),
  and divides political justice into natural and legal (V.7). Miller
  (§2.1) reports the two faces: "Philosophers writing on justice have observed that it has two different faces, one conservative of existing norms and practices, the other demanding reform of these norms and practices (see Sidgwick 1874/1907, Raphael 2001)."
  Miller (§1.3) also links justice to the rule of law: "This explains why justice is exemplified in the rule of law, where laws are understood as general rules impartially applied over time."
  Structural note (this implant): "unjust" in the conservative sense (a
  breach of existing rules) and in the ideal sense (a rule that should be
  changed) can apply to one act in opposite ways; pages name which sense.
- **Justice vs fairness.** Aristotle's particular justice is "the fair"
  (V.1). Miller (§2.3) separates a fair procedure from a claim of justice: "A coin toss is a fair way of deciding who starts a game, but neither the Blues nor the Reds have a claim of justice to bat first or kick off."
  Wenar names Rawls's theory: "Rawls’s most discussed work is his theory of a just liberal society, called justice as fairness."
  Structural note (this implant): the name of a theory is not a claim that
  the two words are synonyms.
- **Justice vs charity and humanity.** Miller (§1.2) contrasts what is
  demanded with what is begged; Hume (as Miller reports, §3.1) placed
  animals under "the laws of humanity" rather than justice.
- **Corrective vs retributive vs restorative.** Corrective justice
  restores the victim (Miller §2.2); retributive justice gives the
  wrongdoer what is deserved (Walen §3.2); restorative justice, per its
  advocates as Duff & Hoskins report them, seeks "to repair harms caused".
  Structural note (this implant): the three answer different questions
  (who is restored, who is punished, what is repaired) and are kept apart.
- **Retribution vs revenge.** Walen (§3.1) reports the conflation and
  Nozick's (1981) distinctions between them.
- **Social justice.** Hayek's denial and the ideal-justice theories use
  the same phrase (Miller §2.1); pages attribute it.

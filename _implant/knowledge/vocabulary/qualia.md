# Qualia

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Qualia** are the qualitative properties of phenomenal experiences — the
properties that make one experience feel different from another in ways not
captured by functional role, causal relation, or representational content.
The term names what is at stake in the debate between qualia realists
(Nagel, Jackson, Chalmers, Block) and qualia deniers (Dennett, Frankish,
Harman). The implant uses "qualia" to refer to whatever each side says it
does, without adopting either side's ontology as its own.

Why this definition: qualia realism and qualia eliminativism are substantive
positions with named proponents and named critics. Defining qualia as
"existing qualitative properties" would beg the question against
eliminativists; defining them as "illusory introspective appearances" would
beg the question against realists. The implant reports both definitions and
lets the reader decide. Pages in [positions](../positions/index.md) state
their stance explicitly.

## Descriptive definitions

- **Lewis 1929 (coinage):** C. I. Lewis introduced "quale" (plural "qualia")
  in *Mind and the World Order* (p. 59) to name the given element in
  experience, distinct from interpretation. Lewis's sense is epistemic, not
  ontological.
- **Nagel 1974:** the qualitative character of experience — "what it is
  like" — that physical description leaves out ("What Is It Like to Be a
  Bat?", p. 436). Nagel does not use the word "qualia" but defines the
  concept.
- **Jackson 1982:** qualia are the properties of experience that Mary does
  not know before leaving her black-and-white room ("Epiphenomenal Qualia",
  *Philosophical Quarterly* 32(127), pp. 127–128). See
  [knowledge argument](../arguments/knowledge-argument.md).
- **Dennett 1988:** qualia as traditionally conceived (ineffable, intrinsic,
  private, directly apprehensible) do not exist; the intuitions that seem
  to support them are cognitive illusions ("Quining Qualia", in *Consciousness
  in Contemporary Science*, ed. Marcel & Bisiach, pp. 42–77).
- **Block 1995:** distinguishes phenomenal properties (qualia) from access
  properties; argues that P-consciousness has qualitative character that
  A-consciousness lacks ("On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness",
  *BBS* 18(2), p. 230).
- **Chalmers 1996:** qualia are the properties that constitute the hard
  problem; they are irreducible to functional or physical properties (*The
  Conscious Mind*, pp. xii–xiii).
- **Frankish 2016:** qualia as non-physical properties do not exist; the
  appearance of qualia is an introspective illusion ("Illusionism as a
  Theory of Consciousness", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 23(11–12),
  pp. 11–39). See [illusionism](../positions/illusionism.md).
- **Tye 2003:** qualia are representational properties; the qualitative
  character of experience is exhausted by its intentional content
  (*Consciousness, Color, and Content*). Representationalism dissolves the
  explanatory gap without eliminating qualia.

## Collisions

- **Qualia vs. phenomenal consciousness.** Qualia are the properties of
  phenomenal experiences; phenomenal consciousness is the state of having
  such experiences. The terms are closely related but distinct: one can
  affirm phenomenal consciousness while denying qualia (representationalism)
  or affirm qualia while denying they are non-physical (a posteriori
  physicalism). See [consciousness](consciousness.md).
- **Qualia vs. sense data.** Sense data are mental objects posited by early
  20th-century empiricists (Russell, Moore, Ayer); qualia are properties of
  experiences. The conflation is common but misleading: sense-data theories
  are largely abandoned; qualia debates continue.
- **Qualia vs. raw feels.** "Raw feel" is sometimes used as a synonym for
  quale, but some authors distinguish raw feels as pre-conceptual sensory
  inputs from qualia as fully formed experiential properties. The implant
  records this distinction when cited.

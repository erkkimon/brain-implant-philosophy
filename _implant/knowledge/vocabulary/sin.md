# Sin

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Sin** is used here as a pointer term: an act, disposition or state that
a named tradition or author counts as a wrong against a divine or cosmic
order, in whichever sense the cited source gives it; every page names
the tradition or author whose sense it uses.

Why this definition: the sources disagree on what the word applies to,
not only on which acts fall under it. Aquinas (ST I-II q.71 a.6 ad 5)
separates two viewpoints: "The theologian considers sin chiefly as an offense against God; and the moral philosopher, as something contrary to reason."
T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids (ERE 1921, p. 533), for the Buddhist
believer in karma: "To him no personal deity ever made the moral law."
Tillich (1957, p. 46) separates "sins" in the plural, "deviations from
moral laws", from "sin" as a state. Fixing any one tradition's sense as
the implant's own would take a side between traditions and in the
debates that depend on it (on [good and evil](good-and-evil.md) and on
[virtue and vice](virtue-and-vice.md)). The implant reports each sense with
its holders and critics, every tradition on the same footing
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Traditions first, then philosophers, roughly by date; not ranked.

1. **Hebrew Bible and rabbinic usage: three grades.** Jacobs & Eisenstein
   (JE 1906) define sin "Under the Jewish theocracy" as wilful breach of
   the commands "as proclaimed by Moses and interpreted by the Rabbis", so
   that "it thus includes crimes against God and crimes against society or an individual member thereof".
   The lightest kind, *ḥeṭ*, is "an infraction of a command committed in ignorance of the existence or meaning of that command";
   *'awon* is "a breach of a minor commandment committed with a full knowledge of the existence and nature of that commandment";
   the gravest, *pesha'* or *mered*, is "a presumptuous and rebellious act against God".
   They cite Ps. 106:6 and the high priest's Yom Kippur confession in that
   order (Yoma 36b). On human nature they report: "Man is responsible for sin because he is endowed with free will"
   and, on whether Adam's sin is inherited, that Jewish theologians are
   divided, "the majority, however, do not hold Adam responsible for the sins of mankind".
2. **Augustine: will and original sin.** On [Augustine](../thinkers/augustine.md),
   Tornau (SEP 2024, §7.4) reports: "Augustine therefore defines sin as “the will to keep or pursue something unjustly” (De duabus animabus 15)."
   and states the doctrine: "Original sin transformed our initial ability not to sin into an inability not to sin; grace can restore ability not to sin in this life and will transform it into inability to sin in the next".
3. **Aquinas: a bad human act; original sin as privation.**
   [Aquinas](../thinkers/aquinas.md) takes up Augustine's definition
   (Contra Faust. xxii, 27): "Sin is a word, deed, or desire, contrary to the eternal law."
   (ST I-II q.71 a.6 obj. 1); for him "sin is nothing else than a bad human act"
   measured by two rules, human reason and the eternal law (a.6 corpus).
   Sin relates to vice "as act to habit" (q.71 a.2 obj. 4). On original
   sin: "According to the Catholic Faith we are bound to hold that the first sin of the first man is transmitted to his descendants, by way of origin."
   (q.81 a.1); "Hence original sin is concupiscence, materially, but privation of original justice, formally." (q.82 a.3).
4. **Catholic catechetical teaching: mortal and venial sin.** The
   *Catechism* (§1849): "Sin is an offense against reason, truth, and right conscience; it is failure in genuine love for God and neighbor caused by a perverse attachment to certain goods."
   "Mortal sin destroys charity in the heart of man by a grave violation of God's law" (§1855);
   its conditions: "Mortal sin is sin whose object is grave matter and which is also committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent."
   (§1857). Venial: "One commits venial sin when, in a less serious matter, he does not observe the standard prescribed by the moral law, or when he disobeys the moral law in a grave matter, but without full knowledge or without complete consent."
   (§1862). Aquinas: "But if mortal be taken metaphorically, as applied to sin, it is opposed to that which is venial."
   (ST I-II q.88 a.1). The *Catechism*: "Sin is a personal act." (§1868).
5. **Qur'anic and Islamic usage.** Edward Sell (ERE 1921, p. 567): "There are various words used in the Qur’an to express the ideas of fault, crime, sin."
   "The word dhanb expresses all forms of unbelief and wrong actions proceeding from such unbelief."
   whose commission makes one a *khaṭī*, a sinner; "The word ithm seems to imply a wrong attitude to others and so injustice."
   On inheritance: "Man does not inherit a sinful nature, but simply a weak one."
   Hughes (1895, pp. 594–595) reports a division into great (*kabīrah*)
   and little (*ṣaghīrah*) sins, and that "Muhammadan writers are not agreed as to the exact number of kabtrah sins"
   (OCR spelling; Hughes's 1895 terminology). Sell was a Church
   Missionary Society secretary (ERE vol. 11 author list); his own
   evaluative remarks on the Qur'an are in the raw file, not used here.
6. **Vedic and Hindu usage.** A. Berriedale Keith (ERE 1921, p. 560)
   reports two Vedic conceptions: sin as offence against the god Varuṇa —
   "Varuna is the god to whom the sinner addresses himself" (Rigveda VII.86) —
   and "the more primitive conception of sin as a pollution which can be removed by physical means"
   (Keith's wording). For the epics and early Dharmaśāstras: "the principle is accepted that each deed brings its recompense, sometimes on earth, but more normally in a tuture birth"
   (p. 561; "tuture" is the scan's).
7. **Buddhist usage: pāpa and karma.** The Rhys Davids (ERE 1921, p. 533):
   "No one holding the doctrine of karma, in any one of its various forms, could accept tbe doctrine of sin."
   and "What the European calls ‘ sin ’ he would call ‘ folly,’ a result of ignorance."
   ("tbe" and the spacing are the scan's). Breaches of the cosmic moral
   law are called "evil, wrong, bad, demeritorious, corrupt" (the scan's
   Pāli terms begin "papa, michchhd, akusala"). Goodman & Schultz (SEP 2024, §4): "The Pāli Canon contains the claim that Saints have “abandoned goodness (puñña) and vile actions (pāpa).”"
   and "“Goodness” and “vile actions” refer to actions which have karmic effects in the future, projecting a future existence that includes happiness or suffering, respectively."
8. **Kierkegaard: anxiety, sin-consciousness, despair.** On
   [Kierkegaard](../thinkers/kierkegaard.md)'s *The Concept of Anxiety*
   (1844), Lippitt & Evans (SEP 2023, §2): "On the surface this book is an inquiry into the nature of original or “hereditary” sin, but in reality it probes the nature of human choice in general, particularly the origins of evil."
   and "While Haufniensis claims that evil or sin cannot be scientifically explained because it has its origins in freedom, he nonetheless believes that anxiety helps us to understand how sin is possible."
   On *The Sickness unto Death* (1849) (§3.3.3): "In The Sickness Unto Death, Anti-Climacus claims that the despair of the forgiveness of sins is itself a sin (“offense”,) which can only be transcended in faith."
9. **Kant: radical evil.** [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md), *Religion* Book I
   (Semple's title: "Of Indwelling Sin"), on the propensity to evil: "we shall moreover call it a RADICAL EVIL, inborn in the nature of man, and yet nevertheless entailed by him upon him"
   (Semple 1838, p. 36; the line continues "self."), and "This evil is RADICAL, for it corrupts man's maxims in their last ground."
   (p. 42). Calder (SEP 2022, §2.2) glosses: "By this he means that all human beings have a propensity to subordinate the moral law to self-interest and that this propensity is radical, or rooted, in human nature in the sense that it is inextirpable."
10. **Tillich: estrangement.** [Tillich](../thinkers/tillich.md) (1957,
    p. 44): "The state of existence is the state of estrangement. Man is estranged from the ground of his being, from other beings, and from himself."
    He keeps the older word: "Man’s predicament is estrangement, but his estrangement is sin." (pp. 46–47);
    "Sin is estrangement; grace is reconciliation." (p. 57).
11. **Nietzsche: a genealogy of the consciousness of sin.**
    [Nietzsche](../thinkers/nietzsche.md) asks (GM II §4): "But how is it that that other melancholy object, the consciousness of sin, the whole "bad conscience," came into the world?"
    He derives "ought" from what "originates from the very material idea of "owe"" (§4),
    and proposes (§16): "I regard the bad conscience as the serious illness which man was bound to contract under the stress of the most radical change which he has ever experienced--that change, when he found himself finally imprisoned within the pale of society and of peace."
    "Original sin" appears in his account of guilt turned back on the
    first ancestor, "burdened with a curse ("Adam," "original sin," "determination of the will")" (§21).

## Collisions

- **Sin, crime and moral wrong.** The three overlap differently by
  source. JE (1906) reports that the rabbinic category "includes crimes
  against God and crimes against society", and: "The Talmudic word "'aberah" carries the idea of trespass, transgression, and includes both sin and crime."
  Aquinas (q.71 a.6 ad 5, above) gives the offence against God to the
  theologian and the "contrary to reason" to the moral philosopher. Sell reports Qur'anic words that "express the ideas of fault,
  crime, sin" together. Tillich (p. 46) contrasts "sins" as "deviations
  from moral laws" with sin as a state: "This has little to do with “sin” as the state of estrangement from that to which one belongs".
  The Rhys Davids report that a Buddhist would call "folly" what a
  European calls sin. A page that uses "sin" for "moral wrong" or
  "crime" names which source's overlap it relies on.
- **Original sin: inherited guilt, inherited weakness, or neither.** Aquinas
  (q.81 a.1) holds the first sin "transmitted to his descendants"; JE
  reports most Jewish theologians not holding Adam responsible; Sell
  reports a weak, not sinful, inherited nature; the Rhys Davids report
  karma in place of the doctrine; Kant's radical evil is "inborn" and yet
  self-incurred; Bernstein's objection, as Calder (§2.2) reports it: "Richard Bernstein argues that Kant cannot coherently hold both of these theses since we could not be responsible for a propensity that is in us originally and that we cannot be rid of (Bernstein 2002, 11–35)."
- **Grades of sin.** Mortal/venial (*Catechism*, Aquinas), *ḥeṭ*/*'awon*/*pesha'*
  (JE) and *kabīrah*/*ṣaghīrah* (Hughes) each grade sins, on different
  criteria (knowledge and consent; ignorance, knowledge and rebellion;
  listed great sins). Tillich (p. 57) reports the Protestant counter-view
  that there is only "the Sin", a qualitative category. A page citing a
  grade names its scheme.

## Related terms

- [Good and evil](good-and-evil.md) — the privation theory, as Calder
  (SEP 2022, §2.1) states it: "For instance, the evil of disease consists in a privation of health, and the evil of sin consist in a privation of virtue."
  Sin there is one kind of evil, beside the evil of disease.
- [Virtue and vice](virtue-and-vice.md) — Aquinas: sin is to vice "as act
  to habit" (q.71 a.2); the *Catechism* (§1866) ties the capital sins to
  vices.
- [Moral agency](moral-agency.md) — the *Catechism* (§1857) requires
  "full knowledge and deliberate consent" for mortal sin; JE grades
  *ḥeṭ* and *'awon* by ignorance and knowledge; Lippitt & Evans report
  Haufniensis placing the origin of sin in freedom.

# Fallacy

## Normative definition (this implant)

A *fallacy* is a pattern of reasoning that some named authority has
classified as defective, at a stated location. The implant never classifies
a pattern as a *fallacy* in its own voice; it reports classifications, with
the classifier, the date, and the receipt. Whether a given pattern is
actually defective is a substantive philosophical question on which
authorities disagree; the implant presents the disagreement.

Why this definition: the word "*fallacy*" carries an evaluative verdict. G4
([manifest](../../vision/manifest.md)) admits only two kinds of sentence —
logic/mathematics verifiable alone, or reports that someone said something
at a location. "This is a fallacy" in the implant's voice would be neither;
"Aristotle classifies X as a fallacy (*Sophistical Refutations* 167b)" is
kind 2. Every page in [persuasion](../persuasion/index.md) follows this
rule: classifications are attributed, never asserted.

## Descriptive definitions

- **Aristotle:** the founding taxonomy distinguishes *fallacies* *in dictione*
  (equivocation, amphiboly, composition, division, accent, figure of speech)
  from *fallacies* *extra dictionem* (accident, secundum quid, ignoratio
  elenchi, begging the question, false cause, many questions). Source:
  *Sophistical Refutations*, passim; standard edition Bekker 164a–184b.
- **SEP (Fall 2024 archive):** "a mistake in reasoning" but notes that
  "there is no generally accepted precise definition" and that "the very
  notion of a *fallacy* is controversial" (Hansen, "Fallacies", SEP Fall
  2024, §1). The entry lists over 200 named *fallacies* and records ongoing
  disputes about whether each is genuinely defective.
- **Informal logic / pragma-dialectics:** Van Eemeren and Grootendorst
  define fallacies as "violations of the rules for critical discussion"
  (*A Systematic Theory of Argumentation*, 2004, p. 135). This makes
  fallaciousness relative to a normative model of dialogue rather than to
  formal validity.
- **Bayesian / probabilistic approaches:** Some patterns traditionally
  classified as fallacies (appeal to authority, argument from ignorance)
  are reanalysed as weak but not zero-strength evidence under Bayesian
  updating (Bovens & Hartmann, *Bayesian Epistemology*, 2003; Hahn &
  Oaksford, "The Rationality of Informal Argumentation", 2007). These
  reanalyses are reported on individual persuasion pages.
- **Ordinary usage:** often any bad argument or false claim. Excluded; when
  a source uses "fallacy" this way, the implant reports the ordinary sense
  explicitly.

## Collisions

- *Fallacy* vs. invalidity. An invalid argument is one whose form does not
  guarantee truth-preservation ([validity](validity.md)). Not all invalid
  arguments are classified as *fallacies* (some are simply weak inductive
  arguments), and not all *fallacies* are invalid (begging the question is
  valid but circular). The implant distinguishes them: validity is checked
  mechanically by `logic.py`; fallaciousness
  is reported as an attributed classification.
- *Fallacy* vs. persuasion technique. A persuasion technique may be
  classified as a *fallacy* by some authorities and as legitimate rhetoric by
  others. The implant's persuasion pages present both sides:
  [straw-man](../persuasion/straw-man.md) records Talisse & Aikin's (2006)
  classification alongside cases where selection-form straw men are
  dialectically appropriate (Aikin & Casey 2011).
- *Fallacy* vs. cognitive bias. A cognitive bias is a systematic deviation
  in judgement documented by empirical psychology; a *fallacy* is a pattern
  of reasoning classified as defective. They overlap (confirmation bias
  produces cherry-picking, classified as the *fallacy* of suppressed evidence)
  but are distinct categories. Bias pages in [biases](../biases/index.md)
  cite replication records; *fallacy* classifications cite classifiers.

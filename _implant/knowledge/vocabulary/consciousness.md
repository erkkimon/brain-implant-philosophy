# Consciousness (phenomenal / access)

## Normative definition (this implant)

When this implant uses "consciousness" without qualification, it means
**phenomenal consciousness**: the property of a mental state such that there
is something it is like to be in that state. When a page concerns information
processing, reportability, or cognitive availability regardless of subjective
experience, it says **access consciousness** explicitly.

Why this split: Block (1995) introduced the P/A distinction to show that
many scientific studies of "consciousness" address access without touching
phenomenal character. Chalmers (1995) independently distinguished the "hard
problem" (why experience exists) from the "easy problems" (information
processing, reportability, integration). Conflating P and A makes every
neural correlate study look like progress on the hard problem when it may
address only access. The implant's [consciousness hub](../problems/consciousness.md)
and [AI consciousness hub](../problems/ai-consciousness.md) use this
distinction throughout; pages that do not specify which sense they mean are
flagged as ambiguous.

Fixed 2026-09-25 ([journal](../../journals/2026-09-25.md)). The normative
choice follows Block and Chalmers; other splits exist and are recorded below.

## Descriptive definitions

### Phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness)

- **Block 1995:** "A state is P-conscious if it has a qualitative character,
  if there is something it is like to be in that state" ("On a Confusion
  about a Function of Consciousness", *BBS* 18(2), p. 230).
- **Nagel 1974:** "an organism has conscious mental states if and only if
  there is something that it is like to be that organism" ("What Is It Like
  to Be a Bat?", *Philosophical Review* 83(4), p. 436). Nagel does not use
  the term "phenomenal consciousness" but defines the concept Block later
  named.
- **Chalmers 1995:** "the hard problem" is explaining why and how physical
  processes give rise to subjective experience (*The Conscious Mind*, 1996,
  pp. xi–xii). The hard problem targets P-consciousness specifically.

### Access consciousness (A-consciousness)

- **Block 1995:** "A state is A-conscious if it is poised to be used as a
  premise in reasoning, for rational control of action, and for verbal
  report" ("On a Confusion", p. 231).
- **Dehaene & Naccache 2001:** global workspace theory models access as
  broadcast to multiple cognitive systems; they explicitly distinguish this
  from phenomenal awareness ("Towards a Cognitive Neuroscience of
  Consciousness", *Cognition* 79, pp. 3–4).

### Other splits in the literature

- **Creature vs. state consciousness.** Rosenthal distinguishes
  consciousness of a creature (being awake rather than asleep/comatose) from
  consciousness of a mental state (having a higher-order representation of
  that state) ("Consciousness and Metacognition", 1993). The implant uses
  "consciousness" for state consciousness unless specified otherwise.
- **Transitive vs. intransitive.** Being conscious *of* something vs. being
  conscious simpliciter. Brentano's distinction; discussed in SEP
  "Consciousness" (Fall 2024, §1.1).
- **Monitoring vs. phenomenal.** Lycan's "inner sense" theory treats
  consciousness as higher-order perception rather than intrinsic quality
  (*Consciousness and Experience*, 1996). This is a theory of P-consciousness,
  not a separate sense.
- **Sentience vs. sapience.** Sentience is the capacity for phenomenal
  experience; sapience is the capacity for thought, reason, or reflection.
  See [sentience](sentience.md) for the distinction.

## Collisions

- **Phenomenal vs. access.** The primary collision. Studies of neural
  correlates, blindsight, subliminal priming, and global workspace often
  measure access; claims about their bearing on phenomenal consciousness
  require explicit bridging arguments. The implant records such bridges
  when made and flags their absence.
- **Consciousness vs. awareness.** Some authors use "awareness" for access
  and "consciousness" for phenomenal character (e.g., Koch, *The Quest for
  Consciousness*, 2004); others reverse the terms. The implant follows
  Block's convention and notes departures when citing specific authors.
- **Consciousness vs. self-consciousness.** Self-consciousness is awareness
  of oneself as a subject. It presupposes consciousness but is not identical
  to it. Kant's transcendental unity of apperception and Sartre's
  pre-reflective cogito are theories of self-consciousness, not of
  consciousness simpliciter.

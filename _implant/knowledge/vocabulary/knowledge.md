# Knowledge

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Knowledge** is the epistemic state that this implant's pages aim to help a
reader form or revise. The implant does not adopt any particular analysis of
knowledge as its own; it reports the analyses that have been proposed, with
their proponents, their receipts, and their critics. When a page says
"knowledge", it means *whatever the reader decides knowledge is*, given the
arguments and evidence presented.

Why this definition: every proposed analysis of knowledge — justified true
belief, reliabilism, virtue epistemology, knowledge-first, contextualism,
pragmatic encroachment — is a substantive philosophical position with named
proponents and named critics. Adopting one as the implant's normative sense
would be taking a side on the Gettier problem and its descendants, which
violates G3 ([manifest](../../vision/manifest.md)). The term is kept as a
pointer to the debate, not as a concept whose analysis is decided here.

## Descriptive definitions

- **Justified true belief (JTB):** knowledge is true belief that is
  justified. The standard attribution is Plato's *Theaetetus* (201c–d),
  though scholars dispute whether Plato endorsed it (see Fine, "Knowledge
  and Belief in Republic V–VII", in *Epistemology*, ed. Sosa & Steup,
  2005). Gettier (1963) presented counterexamples showing that JTB is
  insufficient; the counterexamples are recorded under
  [the Gettier problem](../problems/index.md) (todo: page not yet
  written).
- **Reliabilism:** knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable cognitive
  process. Goldman, "Discrimination and Perceptual Knowledge" (1976);
  *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986).
- **Virtue epistemology:** knowledge is true belief arising from intellectual
  virtue. Sosa, "The Raft and the Pyramid" (1980); Zagzebski, *Virtues of
  the Mind* (1996).
- **Knowledge-first:** knowledge is primitive and unanalysable; belief and
  justification are explained in terms of knowledge, not the reverse.
  Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000).
- **Contextualism:** the truth conditions of knowledge attributions vary with
  the conversational context. Lewis, "Elusive Knowledge" (1996); DeRose,
  *The Case for Contextualism* (2009).
- **Pragmatic encroachment:** practical stakes affect whether a true belief
  counts as knowledge. Stanley, *Knowledge and Practical Interests* (2005);
  Hawthorne, *Knowledge and Lotteries* (2004).
- **Ordinary usage:** varies widely; often closer to "being sure" or
  "having good evidence" than to any philosophical analysis. The implant
  reports ordinary usage when citing empirical studies of folk
  epistemology but does not use it as the normative sense.

## Collisions

- **Knowledge vs. belief.** On every analysis except knowledge-first, belief
  is a component of knowledge but not sufficient for it. The implant
  distinguishes them: belief pages report what someone holds; knowledge
  pages report what has been argued to meet whatever standard the reader
  applies.
- **Knowledge vs. information.** Information is data or content; knowledge
  is an epistemic state of a subject. Floridi distinguishes them sharply
  ("Outline of a Theory of Strongly Semantic Information", 2004). The
  implant uses "information" for data and "knowledge" for the epistemic
  target.
- **Knowledge vs. understanding.** Understanding is sometimes held to be a
  distinct epistemic state involving grasp of explanatory relations
  (Kvanvig, *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding*,
  2003). The implant records this distinction when cited but does not
  maintain separate pages for understanding unless a substantive debate
  warrants one.

## Related terms

- [Argument](argument.md) — the unit offered as reasons for accepting a
  claim; arguments aim at knowledge but may produce only belief.
- [Evidence](../../conventions/evidence-before-authority.md) — what weighs for
  or against a claim; the implant grades evidence independently of who
  presents it.
- [Validity](validity.md) — a property of arguments, not of knowledge; a
  valid argument with false premises does not produce knowledge on any
  standard analysis.
- [Reflective equilibrium](../methods/reflective-equilibrium.md) — a method
  of mutual adjustment between principles and judgements, used by many
  epistemologists to justify analyses of knowledge.
- *Analytic* / *a priori* knowledge. The [Vienna Circle](../schools/vienna-circle.md)
  denied "that any principle or claim was synthetic a priori" and held
  logic and mathematics "to be analytic" (Uebel, SEP "Vienna Circle", Fall
  2024, preamble and §2.3).

# Norm (philosophy)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Norm** is used here as a pointer term: a rule or standard that calls for,
permits or forbids some action, attitude or belief, in whichever sense a
cited source gives it. The sources give that common core in their own
words. McNamara & Van De Putte (SEP 2021, §6.1) distinguish a norm, which
can be created "by granting permission by the very use of the sentence",
from the normative proposition that reports it. Copp & Morton (SEP 2022,
preamble) mark the normative off from the rest by example: "In sum, the first is a normative claim, whereas the second is not."
— the first being "I shouldn’t drink another Dr. Pepper", the second "I’ve
had two already". On every page the sense is named (social, legal, moral,
epistemic, deontic-logical, statistical).

Why this definition: what makes a norm exist, bind or have authority is
the question the sources dispute. Copp & Morton ask: "Are any normative claims actually true, or is there properly speaking nothing I should or shouldn’t do?"
They report normative formalism, the view that "There is nothing to normativity beyond the fact that certain rules or norms “call for” certain actions or decisions in certain situations in the way that the rules of chess and considerations of strategy in chess call for castling early."
and write (their framing, §3.1): "The controversial issue, however, is whether there is such a thing as the “robust” or “authoritative” normativity that formalism rejects."
Bicchieri, Muldoon & Sontuoso (SEP 2023, preamble) report that "some of the key differences between moral, social, and legal norms—as well as differences between norms and conventions—have been blurred."
Adopting a social-scientific, a legal-positivist, a Kantian or a formalist
account as the implant's own would take a side in those debates; a
pointer sense leaves each to its holders
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)). The
statistical sense (*the norm* as what is usual) is listed under
Collisions, following the line Bicchieri, Muldoon & Sontuoso draw between
social and descriptive norms.

Listed in the order of the brief, not ranked.

1. **Social norms.** Bicchieri, Muldoon & Sontuoso (preamble): "Social norms, the informal rules that govern behavior in groups and societies, have been extensively studied in the social sciences."
   They report a view of norms as a grammar (§1): "It has been argued that social norms ought to be understood as a kind of grammar of social interactions. Like a grammar, a system of norms specifies what is acceptable and what is not in a society or group."
   Two families of accounts, as they report them (§1): "Some authors identify norms with observable, recurrent patterns of behavior. Others only focus on normative beliefs and expectations."
   Bicchieri's own account (§1): "In particular, according to Bicchieri’s (2006) account, preferences for conformity to social norms are conditional on “empirical expectations” (i.e., first-order beliefs that a certain behavior will be followed) as well as “normative expectations” (i.e., second-order beliefs that a certain behavior ought to be followed)."
   (Bicchieri 2006, [doi:10.1017/CBO9780511616037](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511616037),
   not read.) Against a purely behavioural account the authors argue (§1): "If we were to adopt a purely behavioral account of norms there would be no way to distinguish shared rules of fairness from, say, the collective morning habit of tooth brushing."
   Their summary (§1, their assessment): "In a nutshell, norms refer to actions over which people have control, and are supported by shared expectations about what should or should not be done in different types of social situations. However, norms cannot be identified just with observable behavior, nor can they merely be equated with normative beliefs."
   They record an open question (§8): "Explaining how normative expectations come to exist remains an open question."
2. **Legal norms.** Kelsen, as Marmor (SEP 2021, §1) reports him: "Kelsen’s answer to this question is surprisingly simple: an act or an event gains its legal-normative meaning by another legal norm that confers this normative meaning on it."
   The chain of validity ends in the **basic norm** (German *Grundnorm*;
   the two SEP entries use only the English term): "The normative content of this presupposition is what Kelsen has called the basic norm. The basic norm is the content of the presupposition of the legal validity of the (first, historical) constitution of the relevant legal system (GT, 110–111)."
   Green & Adams (SEP 2019, §1): "To “presuppose” this basic norm is not to endorse it as good or just—presupposition is a cognitive stance only—but it is, Kelsen thinks, the necessary precondition for a non-reductivist account of law as a normative system."
   Critics side by side: Green & Adams (§1, their objection): "There are many difficulties with this, not least of which is the fact that if we are going to accept the basic norm as the solution it is not clear what we thought was the problem in the first place."
   Marmor (§1) on Kelsen's two postulates of systemic unity: "Whether these two postulates are actually true is a contentious issue. Joseph Raz argued that they are both inaccurate, at best."
   Hart's alternative, as Green & Adams report it (§1): "The ultimate criterion of validity in a legal system is neither a legal norm nor a presupposed norm, but a social rule that exists only because it is actually practiced, that is, used to guide conduct."
   Kelsen's *General Theory of Law and State* (1945) and *Pure Theory of
   Law* (1960) are cited as Marmor cites them; not read.
3. **Moral norms.** Bicchieri, Muldoon & Sontuoso (§2) report Parsons's
   socialisation theory as possibly fitting "a particular conception of moral norms (in the sense of internalized, unconditional imperatives)".
   Copp & Morton (§4.5) report the Kantian approach: "One approach, which is suggested by Kant, seeks to explain normativity in terms of norms that one could rationally will to serve as requirements incumbent on all agents (1785 [1981])."
   and (§4.8) Copp's pluralist teleology, on the "problem of sociality": "Societies do better at coping with this generic problem when their members share a moral code that calls for them to cooperate with one another, to avoid force and fraud, and, generally, and with qualifications, not to interfere with one another."
   Whether there are moral normative facts at all is disputed: "By far the most prominent concern with the existence of such facts, going back at least to J.L. Mackie, is the concern that they would be intolerably queer (Mackie 1977: 33)."
   (§2; see [Mackie](../thinkers/mackie.md)).
4. **Epistemic norms.** Chignell (SEP 2018, preamble): "The central question in the debate is whether there are norms of some sort governing our habits of belief-formation, belief-maintenance, and belief-relinquishment."
   and on their source: "Are they instrumental norms grounded in contingent ends that we set for ourselves? Are they categorical norms grounded in ends set for us by the very nature of our intellectual or moral capacities?"
   One historical example he reports (§1): "(Clifford's Principle) “It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence.”"
   A norm of assertion, as Pagin & Marsili (SEP 2021, §5.1.4) state
   Williamson's: "(KNA) One must: assert p only if one knows p."
   — with critics who appeal to "a justification norm" (see
   [Williamson](../thinkers/williamson.md) and
   [Moore's paradox](../problems/moores-paradox.md)).
5. **The norm of deontic logic.** McNamara & Van De Putte (§1): "Work in twentieth century symbolic modal logic provided the explicit impetus for von Wright (1951a, 1951b), the central early figure in the emergence of deontic logic as a full-fledged branch of symbolic logic in the twentieth century."
   (von Wright 1951, [doi:10.1093/mind/LX.237.1](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LX.237.1), not read.)
   Obligation and permission are interdefined there (§2): "These definitions imply that something is permissible iff (if and only if) its negation is not obligatory, impermissible iff its negation is obligatory,"
   On Jørgensen's dilemma (§6.1): "A widespread distinction that was put forward in an attempt to overcome the dilemma is that between a norm and a normative proposition."
   "It is often maintained that the two uses are mutually exclusive, and only the latter use allows for truth or falsity."
   Its sources (note 78): "Von Wright 1963; Hedenius 1963 [1941]; Alchourrón & Bulygin 1971, 1981; Makinson 1999. Von Wright 1963 attributes the distinction to Ingemar Hedenius."
   — von Wright 1963 is *Norm and Action: A Logical Enquiry* (as the entry
   cites it; not read). A further
   route treats norms as primitive (§6.1): "One may, e.g., assume a primitive notion of “norms” (sometimes also called “imperatives”), which generate “obligations” in a purely syntactic fashion."

## Collisions

- **Norm vs normal (statistical).** "The norm" also names what is usual.
  Murphy (SEP 2020, §4.3) reports Boorse's use in medicine: "Boorse (1977, 1997) insists that the notion of normality in biomedical concepts is statistical — how things usually are in a reference class, but this view faces the problem of specifying the reference classes in an informative way."
  and the objection he reports from Ereshefsky: "As Ereshefsky (2009) puts it, Boorse assumes that statistical normality coincides with the kind of normality that medicine cares about, but this looks wrong."
  Bicchieri, Muldoon & Sontuoso keep "descriptive norms" apart from social
  norms (preamble): "Paying attention to the role played by expectations in supporting social norms has helped differentiate between social norms, conventions, and descriptive norms: an important distinction often overlooked in the social science accounts, but crucial when we need to diagnose the nature of a pattern of behavior in order to intervene on it."
  Structural note (this implant): *x is the norm* as frequency and *x is
  the norm* as requirement share a sentence form; pages name the sense.
- **Norm vs fact (is and ought).** [Hume](../thinkers/hume.md) (T 3.1.1.27)
  remarks the move from "is, and is not" to "an ought, or an ought not",
  which "expresses some new relation or affirmation". Cohon (SEP 2018, §5)
  reports the reading she calls dominant: "According to the dominant twentieth-century interpretation, Hume says here that no ought-judgment may be correctly inferred from a set of premises expressed only in terms of ‘is,’ and the vulgar systems of morality commit this logical fallacy."
  and that "A number of present-day philosophers, including R. M. Hare, endorse this putative thesis of logic, calling it “Hume’s Law.”"
  ([Hare](../thinkers/hare.md)); other readings are on the Hume page.
  Kelsen's basic norm rests on that premise as both SEP entries report:
  Marmor (§1): "More precisely, any alternative would violate David Hume’s injunction against deriving an “ought” from an “is”."
  Green & Adams (§1): "Nor can it be a social fact, for Kelsen maintains that the reason for the validity of a norm must always be another norm—no ought from is."
- **Norm vs normative proposition.** A sentence that creates a permission
  and one that reports it look alike (McNamara & Van De Putte §6.1, the
  parking example; see above). Structural note (this implant): pages that
  run norms through `logic.py` treat the
  reported proposition, which is what has a truth value on the view the
  entry reports.
- **Social norm vs convention.** Bicchieri, Muldoon & Sontuoso (§5): "This is the reason why David Lewis models conventions as equilibria of coordination games."
  "The point is that conforming to social norms, as opposed to conventions, is almost never in the immediate interest of the individual. Often there is a discontinuity between the individual’s self-interest and the interests of the community that supports the social norm."
- **Social vs moral vs legal norms.** The same authors report that the
  social-science literature blurred them (preamble, quoted above); a legal
  norm in Kelsen's sense is valid by its chain of authorisation, not by
  being practised or endorsed (Green & Adams, quoted above). Whether a
  legal norm that fails a moral standard still binds is the subject of
  [rule according to higher law](../positions/rule-of-higher-law.md),
  which reports Kelsen among the legal positivists (Green & Adams §§1–2).
- **Formal vs robust normativity.** Copp & Morton (§3.1): "Formalism has been defended by Evan Tiffany (2007; see also Hubin 2001), and it seems to be implicit in places where it is not explicitly defended."
  They judge (their assessment): "Yet formalism is a radical view."
  and report: "H.L.A. Hart arguably has a similar view about the normativity of law (1994: 257; see Copp 2019)."

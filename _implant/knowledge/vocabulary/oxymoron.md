# Oxymoron

## Normative definition (this implant)

An **oxymoron** is a phrase that places two ordinarily opposed terms
together on purpose, so that the apparent clash makes a point — Silva
Rhetoricae's "Placing two ordinarily opposing terms adjacent to one
another" ([Burton, *Silva Rhetoricae*](http://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/O/oxymoron.htm);
excerpt: `raw/oxymoron-and-dilemma-definitions-silva-rhetoricae-wiktionary-etymonline.md`).
It is a figure of speech, classified under
[rhetoric](persuasion-rhetoric-dialectic.md), and makes no claim that a
contradiction is true.

Why this one: the implant needs one word for the deliberate figure and a
different one for the logical relation, so that a page never reports a
rhetorical device as if it were a claim of the form *P and not-P*. The
logical relation is written "contradiction" (the conjunction of a
proposition and its negation, which [classical logic](../methods/classical-logic.md)
counts as false in every row; checkable with `logic.py`). A phrase used
loosely as "an oxymoron" is reported in its source's own word, with the
sense stated. Fixed 2026-09-26 ([journal](../../journals/2026-09-26.md)).

## Descriptive definitions

- **Rhetorical figure.** "a figure conjoining words or terms apparently
  contradictory so as to give point to the statement or expression"
  (Online Etymology Dictionary, [oxymoron](https://www.etymonline.com/word/oxymoron),
  dating the English word to the 1650s). Silva Rhetoricae calls it "A
  compressed paradox" and gives Milton's "darkness visible" (*Paradise
  Lost* 1.62–64) and *festina lente* ("make haste slowly").
- **Wiktionary sense 1:** "A figure of speech in which two words or phrases
  with opposing meanings are used together intentionally for effect"
  ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/oxymoron)), with examples
  "deafening silence", "open secret", "tough love", "tragicomedy", and
  Shakespeare's "Parting is such sweet sorrow".
- **Loose sense: "contradiction in terms".** Wiktionary lists it as sense 2,
  labelled "(loosely, sometimes, proscribed)", and its usage note reports
  that this use "is considered incorrect by some speakers and writers";
  Etymonline: "Now often used loosely to mean "contradiction in terms."".
  In this sense a speaker calls a phrase "an oxymoron" to assert that its
  two terms cannot both apply — a claim about the world, not a figure.
- **Etymology.** From Greek *oxys* "sharp" and *mōros* "dull, foolish"
  (Silva Rhetoricae; Etymonline: "pointedly foolish"). Wiktionary, citing
  the OED, reports that "The compound form ὀξύμωρον is not found in the
  extant Ancient Greek sources."

## Collisions

- **Oxymoron vs. contradiction.** The figure asserts nothing contradictory;
  its terms are read so that both can apply ("darkness visible").
  The loose sense *is* an accusation of contradiction. When a source calls
  something "an oxymoron", this implant records which sense the source
  uses and, for the loose sense, records it as that source's claim
  ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md)).
- **Oxymoron vs. paradox.** Silva Rhetoricae files oxymoron as "A
  compressed paradox", in the rhetorical sense of paradox. In
  [logic and philosophy](paradox.md) a paradox is an argument or claim
  that yields an unacceptable conclusion; an oxymoron is a two-word figure
  and has no premises. Pages about the sorites or the liar never call them
  oxymorons.

## Related terms

- [Paradox](paradox.md) — an argument or statement, not a figure; the
  rhetorical sense overlaps at "compressed paradox".
- [Dilemma](dilemma.md) — a choice or argument form with two horns; no
  overlap except that both words name two-part structures.
- [Persuasion, rhetoric, dialectic, argumentation](persuasion-rhetoric-dialectic.md)
  — the practice oxymoron belongs to as a figure.

# Individuation

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Individuation** is never used here unqualified. Each occurrence carries
one of four senses, each with its owner:

1. **Metaphysical individuation** — the question of what makes a thing the
   individual it is, distinct from others of the same kind, and what
   answers it (the *principle of individuation*, *principium
   individuationis*). Cross describes the haecceity of Scotus as "a
   non-qualitative property responsible for a substance’s individuation
   and identity" ([SEP Fall 2024 "Medieval Theories of Haecceity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/medieval-haecceity/),
   preamble; excerpt: `raw/sep-medieval-haecceity-fall-2024-individuation.md`).
2. **Schopenhauer's *principium individuationis*** — space and time (with,
   per Wicks, the forms of the understanding) as what divides the world of
   representation into individuals.
3. **Jungian individuation** — a psychological process of a person's
   differentiation from collective psychology, which Jung defined in
   *Psychological Types* (1921; CW 6).
4. **Simondon's individuation** — an operation, prior to any individual,
   in which a pre-individual, metastable state resolves into an individual
   and its milieu.

Why this split: Jung himself uses both the Latin *principium
individuationis* and his own term (see Collisions), so an unqualified
"individuation" can join a claim about matter to one about psychotherapy. Senses 1, 2 and 4 are about
what an individual *is*; sense 3 is about how a person *develops*. Pages
that depend on the split: [Jung](../thinkers/jung.md),
[*Psychological Types*](../works/psychological-types.md),
[archetype and collective unconscious](archetype-and-collective-unconscious.md),
and [the Ship of Theseus](../problems/ship-of-theseus.md). The split is a
choice of this implant, recorded with the batch that admitted Jung
([journal 2026-10-01](../../journals/2026-10-01.md)); it is not a claim
that any of the four uses is wrong
([Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md),
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md)).

## Descriptive definitions

- **Scholastic: what individuates a substance.** Cross reports the
  medieval discussion as starting partly from theology: the 1277 Paris
  condemnation concerned a view "held by Christian Aristotelians in the thirteenth century – pre-eminently, Thomas Aquinas – and originated in the claim that intra-specific individuation is in some sense through different lumps of matter",
  and "angels are good cases for testing intuitions related to the identity of indiscernibles, and thus the question of individuation"
  (preamble). The answers Cross and others report:
  - *Matter* (Aquinas). Pasnau: "The principle of individuation, for forms, is matter (King 2000; Klima 2000)",
    and "Substantial forms both unify a substance at a given time and individuate a substance over time"
    ([SEP Fall 2024 "Thomas Aquinas"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/aquinas/),
    §4; excerpt: `raw/sep-aquinas-fall-2024-individuation-by-matter.md`).
    Pasnau's "King 2000" is Peter King, "The Problem of Individuation in
    the Middle Ages", *Theoria* 66: 159–84,
    [doi:10.1111/j.1755-2567.2000.tb01161.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1755-2567.2000.tb01161.x)
    (held bibliographically, not read).
  - *Haecceity* (Duns Scotus). A "“thisness” (a haecceitas, from the Latin haec, meaning “this”) as opposed to a “whatness”"
    (Cross, preamble). "Scotus rejects the view that matter could be responsible for individuation, on the grounds that we require too an explanation for the individuation of (chunks of) matter"
    (§1, citing *Ordinatio* II, d. 3, p. 1, qq. 5–6, n. 187). Cross quotes
    Fonseca: "According to Fonseca, the principle of individuation is a certain positive difference, primarily incommunicable, which, when added to the species, in itself constitutes the individual … which difference others call haecceities."
    (§4).
  - *Primitive singularity* (Auriol, Ockham). Cross quotes Nielsen's
    report of Peter Auriol: "Maintaining that an object is singular or individual because it results from or is endowed with a proper principle of individuation which is itself singular, amounts to nothing more than duplicating what should be explained."
    and writes that "Ockham claims that a substance is singular through itself" (§5).
  - *Suárez.* Shields and Schwartz list "Individual unity and individuation (DM V)"
    among the *Disputationes metaphysicae* and describe "a pervasive commitment to nominalism, according to which only individuals exist"
    ([SEP Fall 2024 "Francisco Suárez"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/suarez/),
    §§2, 2.1; excerpt: `raw/sep-suarez-fall-2024-dm-v-individuation.md`).
- **Early modern: individuation and identity over time.** Hobbes writes of
  "a great controversy among philosophers about the beginning of individuation, namely, in what sense it may be conceived that a body is at one time the same, at another time not the same it was formerly."
  (*De Corpore* II.11.7, Molesworth vol. I p. 135; excerpt:
  `raw/hobbes-1655-de-corpore-2-11-7-ship-of-theseus-molesworth.md`).
- **Schopenhauer.** Wicks: "In The World as Will and Representation, Schopenhauer often refers to an aspect of the principle of sufficient reason as the “principle of individuation” (principium individuationis), linking the idea of individuation explicitly with space and time, but also implicitly with rationality, necessity, systematicity and determinism."
  and "Before the human being comes onto the scene with its principle of sufficient reason (or principle of individuation) there are no individuals."
  ([SEP Fall 2024 "Arthur Schopenhauer"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/schopenhauer/),
  §§3–4; excerpt: `raw/sep-schopenhauer-fall-2024-principium-individuationis.md`).
  Nietzsche quotes Schopenhauer's sailor who "sits quietly supported by and trusting in his _principium individuationis_"
  and calls Apollo "the glorious divine image of the _principium individuationis,_"
  (*The Birth of Tragedy* §1, trans. Haussmann,
  [Gutenberg #51356](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/51356); excerpt:
  `raw/nietzsche-1872-birth-of-tragedy-1-principium-individuationis-haussmann.md`).
- **Jung: a psychological process.** In the 1923 English translation of
  *Psychological Types*, Definition 29: "In general, it is the process of forming and specializing the individual nature ; in particular, it is the development of the psychological individual as a differentiated being from the general, collective psychology."
  and "Individuation, therefore, is a process of differentiation , having for its goal the development of the individual personality."
  (trans. Baynes, pp. 561–563, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/psychological_types);
  excerpt: `raw/jung-1923-psychological-types-individuation-baynes.md`).
  Sharp gives the Collected Works locators: CW 6, pars. 757–758 and 761
  for this definition, in the Hull translation ("the development of the psychological individual as a being distinct from the general, collective psychology. [ Ibid., par. 757.]"),
  and CW 9i, par. 278: "The goal of the individuation process is the synthesis of the self."
  (Daryl Sharp, *Jung Lexicon*, 1991,
  [author's online text](http://www.psychceu.com/jung/sharplexicon.html),
  "Individuation"; excerpt: `raw/sharp-1991-jung-lexicon-individuation.md`).
  In 1939 Jung glossed it as "the psychological process that makes of a human being an “individual”—a unique, indivisible unit or “whole man.”"
  (*The Integration of the Personality*, trans. Dell, p. 3,
  [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/integrationofper00jung);
  excerpt: `raw/jung-1939-integration-of-personality-meaning-of-individuation.md`).
  CW 6 and CW 9i were not read; their paragraph numbers are Sharp's.
- **Simondon: individuation before the individual.** Simondon says
  substantialism and hylomorphism "both presuppose the existence of a principle of individuation that is anterior to the individuation itself"
  and proposes "to know the individual through the individuation, rather than the individuation through the individual."
  He writes: "Pre-individual being is being in which there is no phase; the being in which individuation occurs is that in which a resolution appears through the division of being into phases."
  ("The Position of the Problem of Ontogenesis", introduction to
  *L’individuation psychique et collective*, trans. Flanders, *Parrhesia*
  7, 2009, pp. 4–6,
  [PDF](https://www.parrhesiajournal.org/parrhesia07/parrhesia07_simondon1.pdf);
  excerpt: `raw/simondon-1964-individuation-psychique-position-of-the-problem-flanders.md`).

## Collisions

- **Jung uses both the old phrase and his new term.** In *Psychological
  Types* he writes that "the ego owes its existence to the principium individuationis, i.e. to individual differentiation"
  (p. 78) and, summarising Nietzsche, that the Dionysian "represents horror at the annihilation of the principle of individuation"
  (p. 173). Structural note (this implant): in those sentences the Latin
  phrase is glossed by Jung as "individual differentiation", which is the
  vocabulary of his own Definition 29; no read source states that Jung took
  his term from Schopenhauer, so this page reports the two uses side by
  side and makes no claim of derivation.
- **Process vs. principle.** In the scholastic sense, what is sought is a
  *principle* (matter, haecceity) that makes something an individual; in
  Jung's sense, individuation is a *process* a person goes through. Sharp
  reports, "In Jung's view, no one is ever completely individuated."
  A scholastic substance, on any of the reported answers, is individuated
  from the start. Simondon sits between: he keeps the metaphysical subject
  matter but treats individuation as an operation, and argues that "the search for the principle of individuation must be reversed"
  (p. 5).
- **Individuation vs. identity over time.** Pasnau reports that forms
  "individuate a substance over time", and Hobbes frames "the beginning of
  individuation" as the question whether a body is the same at different
  times; those two sources tie sense 1 to [the Ship of Theseus](../problems/ship-of-theseus.md).
  Structural note: Jungian individuation, as defined above, is a process
  within a person, not a criterion of sameness over time.
- **Individuation vs. individualism.** Jung separates them in Definition
  29: "An actual conflict with the collective norm takes place only when an individual way is raised to a norm, which, moreover, is the fundamental aim of extreme individualism."
  (p. 563); Sharp glosses: "Individuation differs from individualism in that the former deviates from collective norms but retains respect for them, while the latter eschews them entirely."
- **Assessments are owned.** Whether Jung's individuation is an empirical
  finding is itself a claim with an owner: Jung wrote, "If the process of individuation is an empirical fact, rather than a theory, one must expect the problem to have its history."
  (1939, p. 28). This page records that claim and grades no sense
  ([How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md)).

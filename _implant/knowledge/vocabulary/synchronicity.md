# Synchronicity

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Synchronicity** names, on this implant's pages, the concept C. G. Jung
introduced in "Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle" (German
1952, in *Naturerklärung und Psyche*, together with Wolfgang Pauli's essay
on Kepler; English 1955, trans. R. F. C. Hull; later in *Collected Works*
vol. 8). Jung states the sense he intends: "a coincidence in time of two
or more causally unrelated events which have the same or a similar
meaning, in contrast to “synchronism,” which simply means the simultaneous
occurrence of two events" (Jung 1955, p. 36; excerpt:
`raw/jung-1952-synchronicity-acausal-connecting-principle.md`;
[archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/x-the-interpretation-of-nature-and-the-psyche)).

The term is used here as **the name of Jung's proposal**, not as a
description of any event. To call an event "synchronistic" in this
implant's own voice would assert that it falls under Jung's acausal
principle, which is the disputed claim; pages therefore say that Jung
classes an event as synchronistic, or that the Pauli-Jung conjecture calls
such correlations synchronistic, with the owner named
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md)).

Why this one: three senses travel under the one word — Jung's acausal
principle, the dual-aspect reading developed from the Jung–Pauli
collaboration, and an ordinary sense in which the word names a chance
coincidence. Keeping Jung's sense as the anchor and the others as
reported senses lets a page say which claim is in play: the existence of
a connecting principle other than causality (Jung), a non-causal
mind–matter correlation grounded in a neutral level (Pauli-Jung, as
Atmanspacher reports it), or the bare occurrence of a striking
coincidence (ordinary usage), which nobody disputes. Fixed 2026-10-01
with this page, under [Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md);
see the [vocabulary index](index.md).

## Descriptive definitions

- **Jung's acausal connecting principle (1952).** Jung argues that
  meaningful coincidence cannot be explained "as a phenomenon of energy",
  and concludes: "Therefore it cannot be a question of cause and effect,
  but of a falling together in time, a kind of simultaneity. Because of
  this quality of simultaneity, I have picked on the term “synchronicity”
  to designate a hypothetical factor equal in rank to causality as a
  principle of explanation" (Jung 1955, pp. 26–27). His working
  definition: "Synchronicity therefore means the simultaneous occurrence of a certain psychic state with one or more external events which appear as meaningful parallels to the momentary subjective state—and, in certain cases, vice versa" (p. 36).
  He states the inference to acausality: "Hence the interconnection of
  meaningfully coincident factors must necessarily be thought of as
  acausal" (p. 41). Jung records an earlier formulation from his essay
  "The Spirit of Psychology": "I defined synchronicity as a psychically
  conditioned relativity of space and time" (p. 27).
- **Jung's paradigm case.** Jung reports that "A young woman I was treating had, at a critical moment, a dream in which she was given a golden scarab", and that while she told it an insect flew against the window: "the nearest analogy to a golden scarab that one finds in our latitudes, a scarabaeid beetle, the common rose-chafer" (pp. 30–31).
- **The Jung–Pauli quaternio.** In the book's conclusion Jung reports that
  "Pauli suggested replacing the opposition of space and time in the
  classical schema by (conservation of) energy and the space-time
  continuum" (p. 136); the resulting schema sets "Constant Connection
  through Effect (Causality)" opposite "Inconstant Connection through
  Contingency, Equivalence, or “Meaning” (Synchronicity)" (p. 136).
- **Synchronistic correlations in the Pauli-Jung conjecture (dual-aspect
  monism).** Harald Atmanspacher, in the SEP entry "Quantum Approaches to
  Consciousness" ([Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/qt-consciousness/);
  excerpt: `raw/sep-qt-consciousness-fall-2024-pauli-jung-conjecture.md`),
  reports Jung and Pauli (1955) as proposing "Jung’s conception of a
  psychophysically neutral, archetypal order" (§2). He writes that "The Pauli-Jung conjecture is clearly related to scenario (B) of Sec. 2, combining an epistemically dualistic with an ontically monistic approach" and that "Correlations between the mental and the physical are conceived as non-causal with respect to one another" (§5.2).
  Within that frame: "In the Pauli-Jung conjecture, particularly striking ones among these correlations are called synchronistic" (§5.3),
  and "An essential criterion required for synchronistic correlations is
  that they are meaningful for those who experience them" (§5.3). The
  entry reports a further "“causal” relationship, in the sense of formal
  rather than efficient causation" between the neutral level and the
  mental and material domains (§5.2).
- **Statisticians' "coincidence".** Persi Diaconis and Frederick Mosteller
  define: "A coincidence is a surprising concurrence of events, perceived
  as meaningfully related, with no apparent causal connection", and add
  "Our definition is close to that given by Jung" (Diaconis & Mosteller
  1989, §1, [doi:10.1080/01621459.1989.10478847](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1989.10478847);
  excerpt: `raw/diaconis-mosteller-1989-coincidences-and-synchronicity.md`).
  Structural note: their wording puts meaning in perception ("perceived")
  and causation as "apparent"; Jung's says "causally unrelated".
- **Ordinary usage.** The Cambridge Dictionary marks the word "formal" and
  defines it as "the happening by chance of two or more related or similar
  events at the same time" ([Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/synchronicity);
  excerpt: `raw/cambridge-dictionary-synchronicity.md`). Diaconis and
  Mosteller report that "Jung's images have captured the popular
  imagination, and synchronicity has become a standard synonym for
  coincidence" (1989, §1).

### Reported for and against

Structural note (manifest [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)): the statements
below are paired by what they address; each is its owner's, and none is
ranked.

- **On whether chance suffices.** For acausality: Jung writes that "If we don’t want to risk the hypothesis of acausality, then the only alternative is to explain synchronistic phenomena as mere chance, which brings us into conflict with Rhine’s ESP discoveries and other well-attested facts reported in the literature of parapsychology" (pp. 41–42). Against: Diaconis and Mosteller hold that, once coincidences with apparent causes are set aside, "four principles account for large numbers of remaining coincidences: hidden cause; psychology, including memory and perception; multiplicity of endpoints [...]; and the law of truly large numbers", and that "These sources account for much of the force of synchronicity" (1989, abstract).
- **On frequency.** Diaconis and Mosteller summarise Jung: "Jung argued that meaningful coincidences occur far more frequently than chance allows" (§1). Their reply is the law of truly large numbers: "With a large enough sample, any outrageous thing is likely to happen" (§7); they also cite "memory failure, selective attention, and the heuristic shortcuts we take in dealing with perceptions" (§2), a theme this implant files under [confirmation bias](../biases/confirmation-bias.md) and [availability](../biases/availability.md) (structural note: the link is this implant's; the authors do not use the term "confirmation bias").
- **On where the meaning lies.** In the dual-aspect reading, Atmanspacher (SEP, §5.2) writes that "dual-aspect monism according to scenario (B) is the only philosophical position that explains such correlations in a most natural way, rather than just positing them" — his assessment. Robert Todd Carroll, in *The Skeptic's Dictionary*, holds that "The coincidences are predictable but we are the ones who give them meaning" and that "The concept of synchronicity is but an expression of apophenia" ([skepdic.com](https://www.skepdic.com/jung.html); excerpt: `raw/carroll-skepdic-synchronicity.md`).
- **On evidence.** Atmanspacher (SEP, §6) writes that "there is now a huge body of empirically documented (ordinary and exceptional) mind-matter correlations in clinical psychology, psychophysiology, and psychophysics that supports the Pauli-Jung conjecture in particular", and reports Atmanspacher and Fach (2013) classifying "more than 2000 cases of so-called “exceptional experiences”" (§5.3). Carroll heads his entry with an epigraph he attributes to Steven Strogatz, saying that no evidence has been found for Jung's idea (not traced further here). The same SEP entry also says of the Pauli-Jung, Eddington-Wheeler and Bohm-Hiley lineages: "They are based on speculations that obviously exceed the scope of contemporary quantum theory" (§5).
- **On interpretation.** Carroll asks: "What guide could one possibly use to determine the correctness of an interpretation?" (skepdic). Atmanspacher writes of meaning that "this entails difficult problems concerning a clear-cut definition and operationalization" (SEP, §5.3).

Disclosure the reader needs: the SEP entry's author is also an author of
the Pauli-Jung studies he cites (Atmanspacher & Fach 2013, Atmanspacher &
Rickles 2022, per the entry's bibliography). Diaconis and Mosteller, for
their part, write that "we argue (perhaps along with Jung) that
coincidences occur in the minds of observers" (Concluding Remarks).

## Collisions

- **Jung's sense vs. ordinary "meaningful coincidence".** Jung's term
  denies a causal connection and posits a connecting principle; the
  dictionary sense says "by chance". An ordinary remark that something was pure synchronicity commits to the
  second sense only. On
  pages here, the bare word means Jung's proposal; an ordinary use quoted
  from a source keeps its source's sense and is marked as such.
- **Synchronicity vs. synchronism.** Jung himself separates them:
  "synchronism" "simply means the simultaneous occurrence of two events"
  (p. 36). Simultaneity alone is not his criterion; meaning is.
- **"Acausal" vs. causation debates.** Jung defines cause as an energy
  transfer ("“effect” cannot be understood as anything except a phenomenon
  of energy", p. 26) and
  argues from there to acausality. The SEP entry, by contrast, reports
  the Pauli-Jung correlations as non-causal between mind and matter but
  with a "formal rather than efficient" causation from the neutral level
  (§5.2). Structural note: in the SEP reading "acausal" thus excludes efficient
  causation between the two events, which is narrower than a denial of any
  cause. Pages on causation or on [consciousness](../problems/consciousness.md)
  that cite the Pauli-Jung conjecture should say which sense they report.
- **Synchronicity vs. "synchronic" correlation.** The same SEP entry uses
  "synchronic" (non-temporal, as opposed to diachronic) for correlations in
  other quantum proposals (§2). The words share a root, not a meaning.
- **Coincidence (statistics) vs. synchronicity.** Diaconis and Mosteller's
  "coincidence" is close to Jung's description of the experience but makes
  no claim about its explanation; the dispute is over the explanation.

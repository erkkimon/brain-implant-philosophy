# Good and evil

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Good** and **evil** are used here as a pointer pair: the positive and
the negative pole of evaluation, in whichever sense a cited source gives
each word. On every page the sense is named — for *good*: simpliciter,
good for, attributive, Moore's indefinable property; for *evil*: broad or
narrow, natural or moral, privation, Kantian, Arendtian, or the
"good/evil" pair Nietzsche sets against "good/bad".

Why this definition: what each word means is itself the subject of the
debates the implant reports. Schroeder (SEP "Value Theory", rev. 2021,
§1) separates four constructions of *good* and reports theories that
reduce some of them to others; Calder (SEP "The Concept of Evil", rev.
2022, preamble) writes that "there are at least two concepts of evil: a broad concept and a narrow concept."
Whether the narrow concept should be used at all is disputed: "Evil-skeptics believe we should abandon the concept of evil." (§1).
Adopting Moore's, Foot's, the privation, Kant's or Arendt's account as
the implant's own, or adopting or dropping the word *evil*, would take a
side in those debates and in those that depend on them, such as the
[problem of evil](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md). The implant
reports each sense with its holders and critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).
Religious senses are reported as their holders' and their scholars'.

### Good

1. **The four constructions.** Schroeder (§1) lists "Pleasure is good.",
   "It is good that you came.", "It is good for him to talk to her." and
   "That is a good knife.", and notes of such sentences: "The word “value” doesn’t appear anywhere on this list; it is full, however, of “good”, “better”, and “best”, and correspondingly of “bad”, “worse”, and “worst”."
   He calls sentences of the first kind value claims.
2. **Good simpliciter.** Schroeder (§1): "Sentences like 2 make claims about what I’ll (again stipulatively) call goodness simpliciter; this is the kind of goodness appealed to by traditional utilitarianism."
   He reports (§1.1): "Claims about good simpliciter are those which have garnered the most attention in moral philosophy."
3. **Good for.** Schroeder (§1) reads *good for* sentences with a person
   as subject as claims about welfare or well-being. He reports Foot's
   reduction (§1.1.1): "For example, Philippa Foot [1985] gives an important but compressed argument that apparent talk about what is good simpliciter can be made sense of as elliptical talk about what is good for some unmentioned person"
   and the contrasting case: "G.E. Moore [1903], in contrast, struggled to make sense of good-for claims."
4. **Attributive good.** Schroeder (§1): "And sentences like 4 are what, following Geach [1956], I’ll call attributive uses of “good”, because “good” functions as a predicate modifier, rather than as a predicate in its own right."
   The source is P. T. Geach, "Good and Evil", *Analysis* 17(2), 1956,
   pp. 33–42 ([doi:10.1093/analys/17.2.33](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/17.2.33));
   its text was not read for this page, and its content is given only as
   Schroeder reports it. On whether good simpliciter is a case of
   attributive good, Schroeder (§1.1.2) reports: "Geach and Foot both argue that it does not, on the ground that states of affairs are too thin of a kind to support attributive good claims"
   [Foot](../thinkers/foot.md)'s later "natural goodness" of a thing as a
   member of its kind is on her page.
5. **Moore: good as indefinable.** [G. E. Moore](../thinkers/g-e-moore.md),
   *Principia Ethica* (1903), ch. I §6: "If I am asked ‘What is good?’ my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter."
   His test (§13): "whatever definition be offered, it may be always asked, with significance, of the complex so defined, whether it is itself good."
   Hurka (SEP, §1) reports the property's status: "This meant the property is “non-natural,” or distinct from any of the natural properties studied by science, though he also thought it distinct from metaphysical properties."
   and an objection: "Another objection, made from the 1970s on and very influential, says that while the open-question argument may show that the concept “good” is distinct from any non-moral concept, it cannot support a similar conclusion about the property of goodness."
6. **Good and better.** Schroeder (§1.2.3) reports a reason given for not
   reducing *good* to *better*: "But it has been held by many moral philosophers that an inventory of what is better than what would still leave something interesting and important out: what is good."
   He adds that the context-dependence of *good* may explain it.

### Evil

7. **Broad evil: natural and moral.** Calder (preamble): "The broad concept picks out any bad state of affairs, wrongful action, or character flaw."
   "Natural evils are bad states of affairs which do not result from the intentions or negligence of moral agents."
   "By contrast, moral evils do result from the intentions or negligence of moral agents."
   "Evil in the broad sense, which includes all natural and moral evils, tends to be the sort of evil referenced in theological contexts, such as in discussions of the problem of evil."
8. **Narrow evil.** Calder (preamble): "In contrast to the broad concept of evil, the narrow concept of evil picks out only the most morally despicable sorts of actions, characters, events, etc."
   "Since the narrow concept of evil involves moral condemnation, it is appropriately ascribed only to moral agents and their actions."
9. **Evil-skeptics and evil-revivalists.** Calder (§1): "By contrast, evil-revivalists believe that the concept of evil has a place in our moral and political thinking and discourse."
    The skeptic's scope: "Evil-skeptics believe the concept of evil is particularly problematic and should be abandoned while other moral concepts, such as right, wrong, good, and bad, are worth keeping."
    Calder reports three skeptical reasons and the replies, side by side:
    - *Supernatural commitment* (§1.1): "Some evil-skeptics believe that the concept of evil necessarily makes reference to supernatural spirits, dark forces, or creatures."
      (citing Clendinnen 1999; Cole 2006, 2019). Reply: "There is a secular moral concept of evil which is distinct from fictional or religious conceptions, and it is this secular conception of evil that is meant most often when the term ‘evil’ is used in moral and political contexts (see Garrard 2002, 325; Card 2010, 10–17)."
    - *No explanatory power* (§1.2), Clendinnen's view as Calder states it: "To say that a person, or an action, is evil is just to say that that person, or action, defies explanation or is incomprehensible (see Clendinnen 1999, 81; Baron-Cohen 2011, 5-7; see also, Pocock 1985)."
      Reply: "Another common response is to argue that evil is no less explanatorily useful than other moral concepts such as good, bad, right, and wrong (Garrard 2002, 322–326; Russell 2009, 268–269)."
    - *Danger of misuse* (§1.3), with Card's reply: "Claudia Card argues that “If the likelihood of the ideological abuse of a concept were sufficient reason to abandon the concept, we should probably abandon all normative concepts, certainly ‘right’ and ‘wrong.’” (Card 2010, 15)"
    For keeping the word, Calder (§1.4) quotes Haybron: "As Daniel Haybron puts it “Prefix your adjectives [such as ‘wrong’ or ‘bad’] with as many ‘very’s as you like; you still fall short. Only ‘evil’, it seems, will do” (Haybron 2002b, 260)."
10. **Evil as privation (Neoplatonist, Augustine).** Calder (§2.1) states
    the Neoplatonist theory: "For instance, the evil of disease consists in a privation of health, and the evil of sin consist in a privation of virtue."
    [Augustine](../thinkers/augustine.md), *Enchiridion* 11 (Shaw tr.): "For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?"
    and *Confessions* VII.12.18 (Pilkington tr.): "That evil, then, which I sought whence it was, is not any substance; for were it a substance, it would be good."
    Tornau (SEP, §7.5) reports the source: "After having encountered the books of the Platonists, Augustine rejected the existence of an evil substance and endorsed the Neoplatonic view (argued, e.g., in Plotinus, Enneads I.8) that evil is in fact unsubstantial and a privation or corruption of goodness."
    Against it, Calder (§2.1): "Pain is a distinct phenomenological experience which is positively bad and not merely not good."
    The contrasting dualist sense, as Calder reports Manichaeism: "The Manichaean solution to the problem of evil is that God is neither all-powerful nor the sole creator of the world."
11. **Kant: evil as a will not fully good.** Calder (§2.2): "Instead, Kant equates evil with having a will that is not fully good."
    "There are three grades of evil which can be seen as increasingly more evil stages of corruption in the will."
    (frailty, impurity, perversity; *Religion*, Bk I, as Calder reports).
12. **Arendt: radical and banal evil.** Calder (§2.3): "For Arendt, radical evil involves making human beings as human beings superfluous."
    On *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963): "In Eichmann in Jerusalem, she argues that “desk murderers” such as Eichmann were not motivated by demonic or monstrous motives."
    Reception, as Calder reports it: "Arendt’s reflections on Eichmann and her concept of the banality of evil have been both influential and controversial"
    and "Others have contested Arendt’s suggestion that ordinary people can be regular sources of evil (see Card 2010; Calder 2003 and 2009)."
13. **Nietzsche: "good and bad" against "good and evil".**
    [Nietzsche](../thinkers/nietzsche.md), *Genealogy of Morals* (1887;
    Samuel tr.), First Essay. The aristocratic pair (I §2): "the judgment "good" did not originate among those to whom goodness was shown. Much rather has it been the good themselves, that is, the aristocratic, the powerful, the high-stationed, the high-minded, who have felt that they themselves were good, and that their actions were good, that is to say of the first order, in contradistinction to all the low, the low-minded, the vulgar, and the plebeian."
    The pair of resentment (I §10): "he has conceived "the evil enemy," the "evil one," and indeed that is the root idea from which he now evolves as a contrasting and corresponding figure a "good one," himself--his very self!"
    The contrast (I §11): "these two words "bad" and "evil," how great a difference do they mark, in spite of the fact that they have an identical contrary in the idea "good.""
    And (I §17): "what I exactly mean by that dangerous motto which is inscribed on the body of my last book: Beyond Good and Evil--at any rate that is not the same as "Beyond Good and Bad.""
    Calder (§1.3.1) reads him as an evil-skeptic: "He contends that the powerless and weak created the concept of evil to take revenge against their oppressors."
    Card's reply, as Calder reports it: "Instead, she argues that judgments of evil often indicate a healthy recognition that one has been treated unjustly."
14. **Good and evil in human nature (Mencius and Gaozi).** In
    [Mencius](../thinkers/mencius.md) 6A2 (Legge tr.), Gaozi: "Man's nature is indifferent to good and evil, just as the water is indifferent to the east and west."
    Mencius: "The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards."

## Collisions

- ***Bad* vs *evil*.** Nietzsche (GM I §11) gives *bad* and *evil* one
  contrary, *good*, and says the two senses of *good* differ. Calder
  (§1.4) reports the view that *evil* says what *bad* cannot, quoting
  Haybron. Structural note (this implant): *the opposite of good* is
  ambiguous between these; pages name which pair they mean.
- **Broad vs narrow *evil*.** A toothache is evil in Calder's broad sense
  and not in the narrow one (preamble). The problem of evil uses the
  broad sense, as Calder reports (preamble); the evil-skeptic debate
  concerns the narrow. Structural note (this implant): a claim about
  *evil* carried from one debate to the other changes its subject.
- **Evil vs very wrong.** Calder (§3.1): "One controversial answer to this question is that nothing more is required: an evil action is just a very wrongful action (Russell 2007 and 2014)."
  Against it: "This position is resisted by most evil-revivalists who claim instead that evil is qualitatively, rather than merely quantitatively, distinct from mere wrongdoing (See, e.g., Steiner 2002; Garrard 1998 and 2002; Calder 2013 and 2019)."
- **Kant's *evil* vs the narrow *evil*.** Calder (§2.2): "However, most theorists acknowledge that Kant’s theory is disappointing as a theory of evil in the narrow sense since it does not pick out only the morally worst sorts of actions and characters."
- **Good simpliciter vs attributive good.** Schroeder's sentences 2 and
  4 (§1) use different constructions; Geach and Foot, as Schroeder
  reports them, deny that good simpliciter is a case of attributive good.
  Structural note (this implant): an argument moving from *a good X* to
  *good* without qualification changes construction.

# Virtue and vice

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Virtue** is used here as a pointer term: a trait of a person counted
as an excellence (Greek *aretê*), in whichever sense a cited source
gives it. **Vice** is used for a trait counted as its opposite or
corruption, in the same source's sense. Hursthouse and Pettigrove (SEP
2022, §1.1) state the virtue-ethical core: "A virtue is an excellent trait of character."
Homiak (SEP 2019, §1) sets out the field between the two: "If someone lacks virtue, she may have any of several moral vices, or she may be characterized by a condition somewhere in between virtue and vice, such as continence or incontinence."
On every page the sense is named (Aristotelian, Platonic, Thomist,
Confucian, Humean, MacIntyrean, theological, epistemic).

Why this definition: what makes a trait a virtue, and which traits are
virtues, is what the sources dispute. Aristotle locates it in a mean,
Aquinas adds virtues infused by God, Hume's test is usefulness or
agreeableness and moves "monkish virtues" into the catalogue of vices,
MacIntyre ties virtue to practices, and situationist critics question
whether robust traits of this kind are what people have (all reported
below). Adopting any one account as the implant's own would take a side
in these debates and in the dispute between virtue ethics and its rivals
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)). The
pointer sense keeps "virtue" (a trait) apart from "virtue ethics" (a
theory that makes such traits foundational) and from "sin" (a
theological category of act or state).

## Descriptive definitions

Sources: Hursthouse & Pettigrove, "Virtue Ethics", SEP Fall 2024 archive
(rev. 2022-10-11; [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/ethics-virtue/));
Homiak, "Moral Character" (rev. 2019-04-15; [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-character/));
Doris, Stich, Phillips & Walmsley, "Moral Psychology: Empirical
Approaches" (rev. 2020-01-06; [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-psych-emp/));
Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics" ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/aristotle-ethics/)).
Primary texts are cited at each sense; excerpts in `raw/`. Listed roughly by date, not ranked.

1. **Virtue as a mean between two vices (Aristotle).**
   [Aristotle](../thinkers/aristotle.md), *Nicomachean Ethics* II.6
   (1106b–1107a, definition ending 1107a1–2 per Kraut; Ross tr.): "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."
   Vice is the excess or the defect: "Now it is a mean between two vices, that which depends on excess and that which depends on defect; and again it is a mean because the vices respectively fall short of or exceed what is right in both passions and actions, while virtue both finds and chooses that which is intermediate."
   and "Hence in respect of its substance and the definition which states its essence virtue is a mean, with regard to what is best and right an extreme."
   Some states admit no mean: "But not every action nor every passion admits of a mean; for some have names that already imply badness, e.g. spite, shamelessness, envy, and in the case of actions adultery, theft, murder;"
   Moral virtue is acquired: "Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit." (II.1).
   Kraut (SEP, §5.1) glosses the virtue as a disposition: "Aristotle describes ethical virtue as a “hexis” (“state” “condition” “disposition”)—a tendency or disposition, induced by our habits, to have appropriate feelings (1105b25–6)."
   and reports an objection to the mean as a guide to deliberation (§5.1).
2. **The four cardinal virtues (Plato, Republic IV; Aquinas).**
   [Plato](../thinkers/plato.md), *Republic* IV (c. 427e; Jowett tr.),
   Socrates on the well-ordered city: "And being perfect, is therefore wise and valiant and temperate and just."
   Frede & Lee (SEP 2023, §3.2): "The catalogue of what in later tradition has been dubbed ‘the four cardinal Platonic virtues’ – wisdom, courage, moderation, and justice – is first presented without comment."
   and, their observation: "There is no proof offered that there are exactly four virtues in a state, nor that they are items that can be lifted up, singly, for inspection, like objects from a basket."
   Finnis (SEP 2021, §4.4) on [Aquinas](../thinkers/aquinas.md): "Aquinas accepts the Platonic-Aristotelian thesis that there are four virtues which are cardinal, that is on which the moral life and all other virtues hinge or depend: prudentia, justice, courage, and temperantia."
   Justice as one of these virtues is treated on [justice](justice.md).
3. **Theological virtues (Aquinas, ST I-II q.62).** Aquinas (English
   Dominican tr.) names three, on scriptural authority: "On the contrary, The Apostle says (1 Cor. 13:13): "Now there remain faith, hope, charity, these three.""
   He gives three reasons for the name: "Such like principles are called "theological virtues": first, because their object is God, inasmuch as they direct us aright to God: secondly, because they are infused in us by God alone: thirdly, because these virtues are not made known to us, save by Divine revelation, contained in Holy Writ."
   and separates them from the acquired kinds (a.2): "Wherefore the theological virtues are specifically distinct from the moral and intellectual virtues."
4. **Confucian virtues: ren and de.** Csikszentmihalyi (SEP 2024, §4),
   on [Confucius](../thinkers/confucius.md): "Five behaviors of the gentleman most central to the Analects are benevolence (ren 仁), righteousness (yi 義), ritual propriety (li 禮), wisdom (zhi 智), and trustworthiness (xin 信)."
   On de: "Another term sometimes translated as “virtue” (de 德), is usually used to describe the authority of a ruler that grows out of goodness or favor to others, and is a key term in many of the social and political works discussed in the following section."
   Legge renders ren in *Analects* 12.1 as "perfect virtue": "To subdue one's self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue."
   Whether "virtue" is the right frame is itself disputed, as Wong reports: "A common way to understand Confucian ethics is that it is a virtue ethic. For some scholars this will be an obvious, uncontroversial truth. For others, it is a misconstrual that imposes contentious Western assumptions on Confucianism about what it is to be a person and what an ethics should be about."
5. **Virtue as a quality useful or agreeable (Hume).**
   [Hume](../thinkers/hume.md), second *Enquiry* (1751), App. 1.10: "It defines virtue to be whatever mental action or quality gives to a spectator the pleasing sentiment of approbation; and vice the contrary."
   M 9.3: "And as every quality which is useful or agreeable to ourselves or others is, in common life, allowed to be a part of personal merit; so no other will ever be received, where men judge of things by their natural, unprejudiced reason, without the delusive glosses of superstition and false religion."
   By that test he reclassifies a list of religious practices: "Celibacy, fasting, penance, mortification, self-denial, humility, silence, solitude, and the whole train of monkish virtues; for what reason are they everywhere rejected by men of sense, but because they serve to no manner of purpose; neither advance a man's fortune in the world, nor render him a more valuable member of society; neither qualify him for the entertainment of company, nor increase his power of self-enjoyment?"
   "We justly, therefore, transfer them to the opposite column, and place them in the catalogue of vices;"
   Structural note (this implant): the page records Hume's classification beside the others without choosing.
6. **Virtue in the modern revival (virtue ethics).** Hursthouse &
   Pettigrove (§1): "It was heralded by Anscombe’s famous article “Modern Moral Philosophy” (Anscombe 1958) which crystallized an increasing dissatisfaction with the forms of deontology and utilitarianism then prevailing."
   (on [Anscombe](../thinkers/anscombe.md), whose own role is
   qualified on her page: "Anscombe herself did not advocate this development").
   Their fuller characterisation (§1.1): "It is a disposition, well entrenched in its possessor—something that, as we say, goes all the way down, unlike a habit such as being a tea-drinker—to notice, expect, value, feel, desire, choose, act, and react in certain characteristic ways."
   and "Possessing a virtue is a matter of degree." Forms they report:
   eudaimonist (§2.1), "A virtue is a trait that contributes to or is a constituent of eudaimonia and we ought to develop virtues, the eudaimonist claims, precisely because they contribute to eudaimonia."
   and agent-based forms, which define vice from the exemplar (§2.2, Zagzebski 2004).
7. **Virtue relative to practices (MacIntyre).**
   [MacIntyre](../thinkers/macintyre.md), *After Virtue* (1981), as Lutz
   (IEP, §4.a.ii) quotes it: "“A virtue is an acquired human quality the possession and exercise of which tends to enable us to achieve those goods which are internal to practices and the lack of which effectively prevents us from achieving any such goods” (AV, p. 191)."
   "MacIntyre therefore adds a second level to his definition of virtue."
   Hursthouse & Pettigrove (§3) cite him for the relativity objection: "Is it not the case that different cultures embody different virtues, (MacIntyre 1985) and hence that the v-rules will pick out actions as right or wrong only relative to a particular culture?"
8. **Vice: the capital vices or "seven deadly sins".** Aquinas (ST I-II
   q.84 a.4) asks whether the list is fitting; the first objection names it: "Objection 1: It would seem that we ought not to reckon seven capital vices, viz. vainglory, envy, anger, sloth, covetousness, gluttony, lust."
   He upholds it on Gregory's authority: "On the contrary, stands the authority of Gregory who enumerates them in this way (Moral. xxxi, 17)."
   His sense of "capital": "As stated above (Article [3]), the capital vices are those which give rise to others, especially by way of final cause."
   and "Wherefore pride, like a universal vice, is not counted along with the others, but is reckoned as the "queen of them all," as Gregory states (Moral. xxxi, 27)."
   O'Neil (*Catholic Encyclopedia*, "Sin", 1913) reports the list's history: "Earlier writers had distinguished eight capital sins: so St. Cyprian (De mort., iv); Cassian (De instit. cænob., v, coll. 5, de octo principalibus vitiis); Columbanus ("Instr. de octo vitiis princip." in "Bibl. max. vet. patr.", XII, 23); Alcuin (De virtut. et vitiis, xxvii sqq.)."
   "The number seven, however, had been given by St. Gregory the Great (Lib. mor. in Job. XXXI, xvii), and it was retained by the foremost theologians of the Middle Ages."
   and the criterion: "It is not then the gravity of the vice in itself that makes it capital but rather the fact that it gives rise to many other sins."
   A secular philosophical treatment: Gabriele Taylor, *Deadly Vices*
   (OUP 2006; DOI 10.1093/0198235801.001.0001, verified; book not read).
   Her publisher's abstract: "It is shown that vices are harmful to their possessor, and negative support is given for some central claims of an Aristotelean-type virtue-theory."
   Hurka (NDPR 2007.04.05) reports her frame: "Taylor is guided throughout by the neo-Aristotelian view, first promoted by Elizabeth Anscombe, that the virtues are traits a person needs to flourish, and the vices traits harmful to flourishing."
   and objects: "If the key feature of vice, the one that makes the vices vicious, is its frustrating its possessor's desires, then our primary response to vice as vice should surely be pity."
   The theological category is treated on [sin](sin.md).

## Collisions

- **Do the traits exist as described? (the situationist challenge).**
  Hursthouse & Pettigrove (§3): "The most recent objection (h) to virtue ethics claims that work in “situationist” social psychology shows that there are no such things as character traits and thereby no such things as virtues for virtue ethics to be about (Doris 1998; Harman 1999)."
  The two papers: John M. Doris, "Persons, Situations, and Virtue
  Ethics", *Noûs* 32 (1998): 504–530, DOI 10.1111/0029-4624.00136;
  Gilbert Harman, "Moral Philosophy Meets Social Psychology: Virtue
  Ethics and the Fundamental Attribution Error", *Proceedings of the
  Aristotelian Society* 99 (1999): 315–332, DOI 10.1111/1467-9264.00062
  (both DOI-verified; neither paper read). Side by side:
  - *The findings, as Doris et al. report them (SEP, §4).* "The Phone Booth Study (Isen & Levin (1972: 387): people who had just found a dime in a payphone’s coin return were 22 times more likely than those who did not find a dime to help a woman who had dropped some papers (88% v. 4%)."
    "The Good Samaritan Study (Darley & Batson 1973: 105): unhurried passersby were 6 times more likely than hurried passersby to help an unfortunate who appeared to be in significant distress (63% v. 10%)."
    The skeptics' inference: "Exploiting this observation, “character skeptics” contend that if moral conduct varies so sharply, often for the worse, with minor perturbations in circumstance, ostensibly good character provides very limited assurance of good conduct."
    Homiak (§5.1): "Doris and others argue that traits are not robust in this sense."
  - *Caveats the same authors report.* On the evidence: "This contention is potentially buttressed by recent prominent replication failures in social psychology."
    On what the skeptics claim: "It is too often said, particularly in reference to Doris (1998, 2002) and Harman (1999, 2000), that character skepticism comes to the view that character traits “do not exist” (e.g., Flanagan 2009: 55)."
    "Yet in his most recent contribution, Harman (2009: 241) says, “I do not think that social psychology demonstrates there are no character traits”."
    (Doris, a party to the debate, is the entry's first author.)
  - *Replies.* Hursthouse & Pettigrove (§3): "In reply, some virtue ethicists have argued that the social psychologists’ studies are irrelevant to the multi-track disposition (see above) that a virtue is supposed to be (Sreenivasan 2002; Kamtekar 2004)."
    Doris et al. (§4): "These virtue ethicists maintain that the behavioral variation provoking character skepticism evinces not unreliability, but rationally appropriate sensitivity to differing situations (Adams 2006; Kamtekar 2004)."
    Homiak (§5.2): "The objectors say that the situationists rely on an understanding of character traits as isolated and often non-reflective dispositions to behave in stereotypical ways."
    "In particular, the objectors say, the situationists ignore the role of practical deliberation (or, in the case of virtuous character, practical wisdom)."
    Empirically grounded alternatives (Hursthouse & Pettigrove, §3): "Others have worked to develop alternative, empirically grounded conceptions of character traits (Snow 2010; Miller 2013 and 2014; however see Upton 2016 for objections to Miller)."
    Adams's middle road: "On his conception, character traits may be “frail and fragmentary” but still virtues, and not uncommon."
    and the authors' own assessment of it: "But giving up the idea that practical wisdom is the heart of all the virtues, as Adams has to do, is a substantial sacrifice, as Russell (2009) and Kamtekar (2010) argue."
  - *State of the debate, as each source puts it.* Hursthouse & Pettigrove (§3): "Even though the “situationist challenge” has left traditional virtue ethicists unmoved, it has generated a healthy engagement with empirical psychological literature, which has also been fuelled by the growing literature on Foot’s Natural Goodness and, quite independently, an upsurge of interest in character education (see below)."
- **Virtue vs virtue ethics vs virtue theory.** Hursthouse & Pettigrove (§1): "(One consequence of this has been that it is now necessary to distinguish “virtue ethics” (the third approach) from “virtue theory”, a term which includes accounts of virtue within the other approaches.)"
  Structural note (this implant): using the word "virtue" commits a page
  to no theory; a claim that virtues are foundational ("Rather, virtues and vices will be foundational for virtue ethical theories and other normative notions will be grounded in them.") is a claim of virtue ethics, attributed as such.
- **Moral vs intellectual (epistemic) virtues and vices.** Aristotle
  already divides the kinds (II.1, Ross): "Virtue, then, being of two kinds, intellectual and moral, intellectual virtue in the main owes both its birth and its growth to teaching (for which reason it requires experience and time), while moral virtue comes about as a result of habit, whence also its name (ethike) is one that is formed by a slight variation from the word ethos (habit)."
  In contemporary epistemology the pair names a separate research
  programme; Turri, Alfano & Greco (SEP "Virtue Epistemology", 2021,
  preamble): "Second, they view intellectual agents and communities as the primary focus of epistemic evaluation, with a focus on the intellectual virtues and vices embodied in and expressed by these agents and communities."
  and (§1): "Perhaps epistemology should examine intellectual vices and other defects to tell cautionary tales of what not to do and how not to be (Alfano 2015, Battaly 2014, Cassam 2016)."
  This page covers the moral sense; the epistemic sense is a pointer
  only ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/epistemology-virtue/)).
- **Vice vs sin.** The capital-vices list belongs to Christian moral
  theology (Aquinas; O'Neil), where "sin" is the governing category;
  Taylor's book treats the same seven as vices within a neo-Aristotelian
  framework, as Hurka reports. Structural note (this implant): calling a
  trait a vice does not assert the theological claim that it is a sin,
  and conversely; see [sin](sin.md).
- **"Virtue" as a translation.** Homiak (§1): "His phrase for excellences of character – êthikai aretai – we usually translate as “moral virtue(s)” or “moral excellence(s).”"
  *Ren* and *de* are also rendered "virtue" (sense 4). Structural note
  (this implant): a shared English word does not show that the concepts
  coincide; pages name the original term where a source does.

# Sentience

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Sentience** is the capacity for phenomenal experience — the capacity to
have states that feel like something. A sentient entity can suffer, enjoy,
or otherwise have subjective experience. Sentience is distinguished from
sapience (the capacity for reason or reflection), from access consciousness
(information availability without subjective character), and from
intelligence (problem-solving capacity regardless of experience).

Why this definition: sentience is the term at stake in debates about animal
ethics, AI moral status, and the scope of moral concern. Birch (2024) uses
it as the threshold for moral patiency in [*The Edge of Sentience*](../works/the-edge-of-sentience.md); Butlin et
al. (2023) use it as the target of their AI consciousness indicators;
Singer's utilitarianism grounds moral consideration in sentience (*Animal
Liberation*, 1975). The implant keeps sentience narrow (phenomenal capacity)
so that claims about AI sentience are not conflated with claims about AI
intelligence or sophistication.

## Descriptive definitions

- **Bentham 1789:** "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they
  talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals
  and Legislation*, ch. XVII, n. 1). Bentham does not use "sentience" but
  defines the concept by its moral relevance.
- **Singer 1975:** sentience is the capacity to suffer and/or experience
  enjoyment; it is the boundary of moral consideration (*Animal Liberation*,
  ch. 1).
- **Birch 2024:** sentience is the capacity for valenced experience
  (positive or negative phenomenal states); it is the ground of moral
  patiency for animals and potentially for AI (*The Edge of Sentience*,
  p. 3).
- **Butlin et al. 2023:** sentience is used interchangeably with phenomenal
  consciousness in the context of AI indicators; the indicators are designed
  to detect evidence of conscious experience, not intelligence or
  sophistication ("Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence", arXiv
  2308.08708, p. 2).
- **Ordinary usage:** often "capable of feeling" or "aware". The implant
  uses the narrower philosophical sense and records ordinary usage when
  cited.

## Collisions

- **Sentience vs. sapience.** Sapience is the capacity for reason,
  reflection, or higher cognition. An entity can be sentient without being
  sapient (most non-human animals on standard accounts) and sapient without
  being sentient (a high-capability AI on functionalist accounts that deny
  phenomenal character). The implant distinguishes them throughout.
- **Sentience vs. consciousness.** Consciousness may refer to access
  consciousness (information availability) without phenomenal character.
  Sentience requires phenomenal character. See
  [consciousness (phenomenal / access)](consciousness.md).
- **Sentience vs. intelligence.** Intelligence is problem-solving capacity
  independent of subjective experience. A system can be highly intelligent
  without being sentient (standard LLMs on most accounts) and sentient
  without being intelligent (simple organisms on standard accounts).
- **Sentience vs. nociception.** Nociception is the neural detection of
  noxious stimuli; sentience is the subjective experience of pain. The two
  can come apart (nociception without sentience in reflex arcs; sentience
  without nociception in phantom limb pain). Animal ethics debates turn on
  whether nociception evidences sentience; the implant reports the evidence
  without adjudicating.

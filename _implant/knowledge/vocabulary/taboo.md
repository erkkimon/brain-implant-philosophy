# Taboo

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Taboo** is used here as a pointer term: a prohibition that a group
treats as binding, in whichever sense a cited source gives it. The common
core is the one Cook recorded at Tonga in 1777 — the word, he wrote,
"in general, signifies that a thing is forbidden"
(`raw/cook-1777-tonga-taboo-voyage-pacific-and-etymonline.md`). On every
page the sense is named (Polynesian, anthropological, psychoanalytic,
moral-psychological).

Why this definition: what makes a prohibition a taboo rather than a moral,
religious, legal or magical rule is what the sources dispute. Frazer (EB9)
calls taboo "a system of religious prohibitions" and later (1922) "negative
magic"; Thomas (EB 1911) stresses its "non-moral character"; Freud (1913)
says taboo restrictions "are different from religious or moral prohibitions";
Radcliffe-Brown (1939) judges the anthropological use of the word
"undesirable and inconvenient" and replaces it with "ritual avoidance".
Adopting one of these as the implant's own would take a side in the debate
on the origin of moral rules and on whether moral judgment rests on
reasons, which the psychological studies below dispute. The implant
reports each sense with its holders and critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

1. **The Polynesian word as Cook reported it (1777).** At Tongataboo the
   chiefs would not eat at Cook's table (Vol. V, Book II, Chap. VII):
   "On expressing my surprize at this, they were all taboo, as they said; which word has a very comprehensive meaning; but, in general, signifies that a thing is forbidden."
   Of a later ceremony (Chap. IX): "We seldom got any other answer to our inquiries, but taboo; a word which I have before observed, is applied to many other things."
   Etymonline dates the English adjective to "1777 (in Cook's "A Voyage to the Pacific Ocean")"
   and reports that "The noun ("prohibitory restraining injunction") and verb ("to put under taboo") are English innovations first recorded in Cook's account [OED, 2nd ed., 1989]."
   It calls the Tongan *ta* + *bu* derivation possible "folk etymology",
   reporting a reconstructed "Proto-Polynesian *tapu, from Proto-Oceanic *tabu "sacred, forbidden"".
   Radcliffe-Brown (1939, p. 133): "In the languages of Polynesia the word means simply 'to forbid', 'forbidden', and can be applied to any sort of prohibition."
2. **A system of religious prohibitions (Frazer, EB9).** "TABOO (also written Tabu and Tapu) is the name given to a system of religious prohibitions which attained its fullest development in Polynesia (from Hawaii to New Zealand; see vol. xix. p. 426), but of which under different names traces may be discovered in most parts of the world."
   He adds: "Although it was employed for civil as well as religious purposes, the taboo was essentially a religious observance."
3. **Negative magic (Frazer, *Golden Bough*, 1922, ch. III §1).**
   "Thus taboo is so far a negative application of practical magic. Positive magic or sorcery says, "Do this in order that so and so may happen." Negative magic or taboo says, "Do not do this, lest so and so should happen.""
4. **A prohibition with religious or magical sanction, non-moral (Thomas, EB 1911).**
   "TABOO (also written tapu and tabu), the Polynesian name given to prohibitions enforced by religious or magical sanctions. As a verb it means to “prohibit,” as an adjective “prohibited, sacred, dangerous, unclean.”"
   Of an older definition he says: "This definition does not cover the whole connotation of taboo as it is employed at the present day, but it indicates clearly the non-moral character of the idea."
5. **An unreasoned prohibition, distinct from moral and religious ones (Freud 1913, ch. II).**
   Brill's translation: "The taboo restrictions are different from religious or moral prohibitions. They are not traced to a commandment of a god but really they themselves impose their own prohibitions"
   Freud reports Wundt: "Wundt(26) nennt das Tabu den ältesten ungeschriebenen Gesetzeskodex der Menschheit."
   (Brill: "calls taboo the oldest unwritten code of law of humanity".)
   He names "the two basic laws of totemism: namely not to kill the totem animal, and to avoid sexual intercourse with totem companions of the other sex."
   as "The oldest and most important taboo prohibitions" and explains taboo
   through the ambivalence he reports in obsessional neurosis (raw context).
6. **Ritual avoidance (Radcliffe-Brown 1939).** He objects (p. 134):
   "The use of the word taboo in anthropology for customs all over the world which resemble in essentials the example given from Polynesia seems to me undesirable and inconvenient."
   and proposes "to refer to the customs we are considering as 'ritual avoidances' or 'ritual prohibitions' and to define them by reference to two fundamental concepts for which I have been in the habit of using the terms 'ritual status' and 'ritual value'."
   His hypothesis (p. 151): "The primary basis of ritual, so the formulation would run, is the attribution of ritual value to objects and occasions which are either themselves objects of important common interests linking together the persons of a community or are symbolically representative of such objects."
   He classes Polynesian, English (spilling salt), Roman Catholic, Hebrew
   and Kikuyu rules together "for scientific purposes" (raw context).
7. **Pollution and classification (Douglas 1966, as Campkin 2013 quotes her).**
   Douglas's book was not read here (lending-restricted scans); Campkin
   quotes "the old definition of dirt as matter out of place" (Douglas
   p. 36) and her later preface: "Taboo is a spontaneous coding practice which sets up a vocabulary of spatial limits and physical and verbal signals to hedge around vulnerable relations".
   Katherine Shonfield's summary, as Campkin quotes it: "Douglas’ view is that pollution taboos – rules in society that guard against the unclassifiable, the impure and the hybrid – have a physical expression."
   Campkin reports that "In her understanding of taboo, Douglas drew on the work of the anthropologist Franz Steiner, who had been her teacher at the University of Oxford; Franz Steiner, Taboo, New York: Philosophical Library, 1956."

- **Taboo vs morality: source, part, or distinct.** Frazer (EB9) conjectures:
  "For we shall scarcely err in believing that even in advanced societies the moral sentiments, in so far as they are merely sentiments and are not based on an induction from experience, derive much of their force from an original system of taboo."
  Thomas (1911, §10) makes taboo "one source of the categorical imperative, the distinguishing feature of which is that it is non-rational and instinctive."
  Freud (1913, preface) says of taboo that "according to its psychological nature, it is still nothing else than Kant’s ‘Categorical Imperative’, which tends to act compulsively and rejects all conscious motivations."
  These are those authors' comparisons; Kant's own concept is on
  [categorical imperative](../positions/categorical-imperative.md) and
  [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md). Against assimilation, Thomas and Freud also
  separate taboo from moral prohibition (Descriptive 4–5).
- **Taboo vs religion vs magic.** Frazer (EB9) calls taboo "essentially a religious observance"; the same Frazer (1922) calls it "negative magic".
  Radcliffe-Brown (1939, p. 137) reports that Frazer's Maori evidence
  "would seem to make the Polynesian taboo a matter of religion, not of magic"
  and, finding no agreement on the magic/religion line, proposes to avoid
  both terms (raw context). He groups a Catholic rule with the rest:
  "If he fails to observe the rule he sins, and must proceed, as in any other sin, to confess and obtain absolution."
  and reads Leviticus as one where "the change in ritual status through touching an unclean carcase is described by the terms 'sin', 'unclean' and 'guilty'."
- **The word in Polynesia vs the word in anthropology.** Radcliffe-Brown
  keeps "taboo" for the anthropologists' sense and "tabu" for the
  Polynesian one, because the Polynesian word "has a much wider meaning",
  equivalent to "forbidden" (p. 134, raw context); Frazer (EB9) extends the
  term to "most parts of the world". Structural note (G4): the same English
  string names both a local Polynesian word and a cross-cultural category.
- **Harmless-taboo studies and moral judgment (Haidt, Koller & Dias 1993).**
  The abstract asks: "Are disgusting or disrespectful actions judged to be moral violations, even when they are harmless? Stories about victimless yet offensive actions (such as cleaning one's toilet with a flag) were presented to Brazilian and U.S. adults and children of high and low socioeconomic status (N = 360)."
  It reports that elite-university students treated the stories as convention or preference, while for the moralizing participants "moral judgments were better predicted by affective reactions than by appraisals of harmfulness."
  Machery & Stich (SEP, §7) note the phrase is Haidt's: "what he characterized as “harmless taboo violations”, (Haidt 2012: 19)"
  and report that "the effect of SES was much larger than the effect of location."
  Side by side, Machery & Stich (§9) report that Royzman, Landy & Goodwin
  (2014) "conclude that we should not assume that the sort of socioeconomic differences that Haidt found in his moral/conventional task studies are due to fundamentally divergent values."
  Machery & Stich's own assessment of that conclusion: "Much more work is needed before accepting this conclusion, and careful skepticism is warranted".
- **Moral dumbfounding (Haidt 2001) and its critics.** Haidt's abstract:
  "The author gives 4 reasons for considering the hypothesis that moral reasoning does not cause moral judgment; rather, moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached."
  Richardson (SEP, §1.2) reports: "When asked to give reasons for our moral intuitions, we are often “dumbfounded,” finding nothing to say in their defense (Haidt 2001)."
  Royzman, Kim & Leeman (2015) describe the claim as one in which subjects "tend to “stubbornly” maintain their disapproval of the act without supporting reasons"
  and report that "with subjects’ beliefs about harm and standards of normative evaluation properly factored in, a more rigorous assessment procedure yielded a dumbfounding estimate of about 0."
  Machery & Stich (§9) also report that when Royzman et al. (2009) asked
  about harm in a disgust vignette, "71% of their participants judged that other guests at the diner table were negatively affected".
- **Taboo trade-offs (Tetlock et al. 2000; Tetlock 2003).** "Experiments 1 and 2 revealed that people responded to taboo trade-offs that monetized sacred values with moral outrage and cleansing."
  Tetlock (2003) states the clash with economics: "They treat the mere thought of trading off sacred values against secular ones (such as money) as transparently outrageous - in effect, taboo. Economists insist, however, that in a world of scarce resources, taboo trade-offs are unavoidable."
  and reports that "although people do respond with moral outrage to taboo trade-offs, they often acquiesce when secular violations of sacred values are rhetorically reframed as routine or tragic trade-offs."
  Whether values have a common measure is on the sibling page
  [commensurability](commensurability.md). No published critique of
  Tetlock's model was read for this page; that gap is recorded, not filled.
- **Left out: Steiner, *Taboo* (1956)** — its scan, like Douglas's book,
  is lending-restricted on the Internet Archive and was not read.

# Consent

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Consent** is used here as a pointer term: a party's agreement to, or
permission for, what another does or proposes, in whichever sense a cited
source gives it. Whisnant (SEP 2021, §2.1) states a general account:
"To consent to something is to reverse a prima facie supposition about what may and may not be done."
"Consent thus alters the structure of rights and obligations between two or more parties."
On every page the sense is named (normative power, informed, political,
sexual or legal), and so is the form in dispute (express, tacit, presumed,
hypothetical).

Why this definition: what consent is (a mental state, an act, or both),
what makes it valid, and what it can ground are the questions the sources
dispute. Eyal (SEP 2019, §3): "English admits of both psychological and behavioral senses of consent."
Whisnant (§2.1): "Consent in general may be understood as either attitudinal or performative (Kazan 1998)."
Dagger and Lefkowitz (SEP 2021, §4.1) report a standing dispute over
whether enough people have consented to their government to ground a
general obligation to obey the law. Adopting an attitudinal, performative
or hybrid analysis, or a view of tacit consent, as the implant's own would
take a side in those debates and in the ones that depend on them
([euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md), political obligation, rape law).
The implant reports each sense with its holders and critics
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

Listed in the order of the brief, not ranked.

1. **Consent as a normative power ("moral magic").** Hurd, "The Moral
   Magic of Consent", *Legal Theory* 2(2) (1996): 121–146 (abstract read;
   the article was not): "When we make promises, we generate obligations for ourselves, and when we give consent, we create rights for others."
   "Since the rights and obligations that are affected by means of promising and consenting largely define the boundaries of permissible action, our exercise of these seemingly magical powers can significantly affect the lives and liberties of others."
   Eyal (§8) reports the assumption and its limit: "Many contemporary philosophers assume that consent has moral “magic” that turns what would have been a wrongful action into a right one, in an objective, moral sense (Hurd 1996)."
   "But some of the aforementioned justifications for informed consent better support other assumptions."
   What consent is, on this question, Eyal (§3) reports in two senses: "Psychologically, consent can designate “a state of mind of acquiescence” (Westen 2003, 5), and “an act of will—a subjective mental state…” (Hurd 1996, 121)."
   "But the medical requirement of informed consent seems to include at least some behavioral expression of consent (Kleinig 2010, 9–10), ranging from a patient’s signature, to her not protesting when a nurse approaches with a syringe (so-called implicit or tacit consent)."
   He notes hybrid views (Miller and Wertheimer 2010; Alexander 2014).
2. **Informed consent in medicine and research.** Nuremberg Code (dated
   1947 by the Belmont Report, note 1), principle 1: "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential. This means that the person involved should have legal capacity to give consent; should be so situated as to be able to exercise free power of choice, without the intervention of any element of force, fraud, deceit, duress, over-reaching, or other ulterior form of constraint or coercion; and should have sufficient knowledge and comprehension of the elements of the subject matter involved as to enable him to make an understanding and enlightened decision."
   Principle 9: "During the course of the experiment the human subject should be at liberty to bring the experiment to an end if he has reached the physical or mental state where continuation of the experiment seems to him to be impossible."
   Belmont Report (1979), C.1: "Respect for persons requires that subjects, to the degree that they are capable, be given the opportunity to choose what shall or shall not happen to them."
   "Nonetheless, there is widespread agreement that the consent process can be analyzed as containing three elements: information, comprehension and voluntariness."
   "An agreement to participate in research constitutes a valid consent only if voluntarily given."
   Eyal (§1): "Informed consent is shorthand for informed, voluntary, and decisionally-capacitated consent."
   "Consent is considered fully informed when a capacitated (or “competent”) patient or research participant to whom full disclosures have been made and who understands fully all that has been disclosed consents voluntarily to treatment or participation on this basis."
   "In its most important role in bioethics, informed consent is a legitimacy requirement for certain actions."
   Its rationales as Eyal reports them: protection (§2.1), where expositions "typically echo" Mill — "bioethicists’ expositions of this instrumental rationale typically echo utilitarian philosopher John Stuart Mill, for whom “Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign” (Mill 1990, 135; see also ch. 3)."
   — and autonomy (§2.2; Faden and Beauchamp 1986, Beauchamp and Childress,
   as on the [autonomy](autonomy.md) page). Variants he reports (§3):
   presumed consent — "They say that presumed consent obtains, even if actual and express consent does not."
   — and its critics: "Some authors oppose the terminology of presumed consent as a contrived “myth” or “fiction” of actual consent (Harmon 1990; Brownsword 2004, 232–3; Beauchamp and Childress 2008, 107; Dworkin 1988, 117)."
   On the status of the requirement (§8): "Bioethicists and human rights lawyers tend to assume that informed consent is a natural right that generates a correlative objective moral duty and, downstream, legal and institutional duties."
3. **Consent of the governed.** Locke, *Second Treatise* (1690), §95: "MEN being, as has been said, by nature, all free, equal, and independent, no one can be put out of this estate, and subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent."
   §119: "There is a common distinction of an express and a tacit consent, which will concern our present case."
   "And to this I say, that every man, that hath any possessions, or enjoyment, of any part of the dominions of any government, doth thereby give his tacit consent, and is as far forth obliged to obedience to the laws of that government, during such enjoyment, as any one under it;"
   Dagger & Lefkowitz (§1.3): "Locke does agree with Hobbes, of course, in deriving obligations to obey the law from the consent of the governed."
   Their assessment of §119 (theirs): "As for Locke, he seems to stretch the notion of tacit consent too far when he states, in §119 of the Second Treatise, that the “very being of anyone within the territories” expresses a person’s willing submission to the rule of its government."
   [Hume](../thinkers/hume.md), "Of the Original Contract" — "In “Of the Original Contract,” published in 1752, Hume takes particular exception to the appeal to tacit consent." (Dagger & Lefkowitz §1.3). Hume states the view he opposes: "The other party, by founding government altogether on the consent of the people, suppose that there is a kind of original contract by which the subjects have reserved the power of resisting their sovereign whenever they find themselves aggrieved by that authority with which they have, for certain purposes, voluntarily entrusted him."
   and objects: "Can we seriously say that a poor peasant or artisan has a free choice to leave his own country, when he knows no foreign language or manners, and lives from day to day by the same small wages which he acquires?"
   "We may as well assert that a man, by remaining in a vessel, freely consents to the dominion of the master, though he was carried on board while asleep, and must leap into the ocean and perish the moment he leaves her."
   His alternative ground: "If the reason is asked of that obedience which we are bound to pay to government, I readily answer, because society could not otherwise subsist."
   The present debate, as Dagger & Lefkowitz report it (§4.1): "Most people who believe that they have an obligation to obey the law probably think that this putative obligation is grounded in their consent."
   The critics (Hume; Simmons 1979): "The critics’ claim is not that consent cannot be a source of obligations, for they typically believe that it can."
   "Their claim, instead, is that too few people have either expressly or tacitly given the kind of actual consent that can ground a general obligation to obey the law, and hypothetical consent cannot supply the defect, for reasons already noted."
   The adherents: "Nevertheless, consent theory still has its adherents among political philosophers."
   Beran (1987) proposes formal procedures for express consent; others widen
   the notion: "Thus John Plamenatz (1968, “Postscript”) and Peter Steinberger (2004, p. 218) have maintained that voting or otherwise participating in elections should count as consent;"
4. **Consent in sexual ethics and law.** Whisnant (§2.1), on the
   attitudinal/performative pair: "Attitudinal accounts see consent as a mental state of affirmation or willingness, while performative accounts see it as a certain kind of action or utterance (for instance, saying “yes” or nodding)."
   The feminist criticism she reports: "Women’s sexual consent has in many instances been understood quite expansively, as simply the absence of refusal or resistance; feminists have criticized this approach on the grounds that, among its other untoward implications, it regards even unconscious women as consenting (MacKinnon 1989b, 340; Archard 1998, 85)."
   She reports that feminists have often preferred performative accounts,
   and states their limit: "One limitation of a purely performative account of consent is that it does not take into account the context in which the relevant behavior or utterance occurs."
   Eyal lists Wertheimer, *Consent to Sexual Relations* (2003), in his
   bibliography; it was not read here.
5. **Legal consent: capacity and voluntariness, as reported.** The
   Nuremberg Code requires "legal capacity to give consent" (principle 1,
   quoted above). The Belmont Report (C.1) treats some classes as unable to
   consent for themselves: "Each class of subjects that one might consider as incompetent (e.g., infants and young children, mentally disabled patients, the terminally ill and the comatose) should be considered on its own terms."
   and for them requires "seeking the permission of other parties". Eyal
   (§5.1): "Voluntary consent is usually thought incompatible with coercion, which philosophers define, roughly, as a threat to make someone seriously worse off than she is or should be, unless she consents (Wertheimer 1987)."
   Dagger & Lefkowitz (§4.1) report a limit on what consent can do: "For example, consent to be another’s slave generates no obligation even if it genuinely expresses a person’s will."
   Statutory ages of consent: no source read for this page states them
   (none recorded).

## Collisions

- **Mental state vs act.** Hurd's "act of will—a subjective mental state"
  (as Eyal quotes her, 1996, 121) and the performative accounts Whisnant
  reports (§2.1) name different things by one word. Structural note (this
  implant): a claim that someone consented in the attitudinal sense is not
  thereby a claim that they consented in the performative sense, or the
  reverse; pages say which is meant.
- **Express, tacit, presumed, hypothetical.** Locke (§119) distinguishes
  express from tacit consent; Eyal (§3) reports presumed consent and its
  critics; Dagger & Lefkowitz (§1.3) report the objection that a
  hypothetical contract "is no contract at all" (Dworkin 1977, as they
  quote him). Structural note (this implant): these are four claims, not
  one; a page reporting that consent was given names which.
- **Consent vs informed consent.** Informed consent in Eyal's sense adds
  disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness and capacity to consent (§1);
  the Belmont Report lists three elements (C.1). Eyal asks how it relates
  to "consent in sexual ethics, business ethics, and political philosophy"
  (preamble) and treats it in §9, "Consent in other domains".
- **Tacit consent in medicine and in politics.** Eyal uses "tacit" for a
  patient's not protesting at a syringe (§3); Locke uses it for residence
  in a territory (§119). Hume's vessel objection is aimed at the second.

## Related terms

- [Autonomy](autonomy.md) — the Belmont Report derives informed consent
  from respect for persons, whose first conviction is that "individuals should be treated as autonomous agents"
  (Part B.1); Eyal (§2.2) reports autonomy as the rationale that rose in
  the 1970s.
- [Rights](rights.md) — Hurd's abstract says consent creates rights for
  others; Whisnant (§2.1) says it "alters the structure of rights and
  obligations".
- [Harm principle](../positions/harm-principle.md) — Eyal (§2.1) reports
  that the protection rationale for informed consent echoes the sentence
  of Mill's that closes the harm-principle paragraph.
- [Euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md) — Eyal (§2.2) cites Velleman's
  argument that closing the option of voluntary euthanasia may increase
  autonomy: "For instance, closing off patients’ options for voluntary euthanasia was said to increase their autonomy by freeing them, as well as similar patients, from family pressure to use that option (Velleman 1992); surely it may do so even when accomplished through forced injection of an antidote or other violations of informed consent."

# Ideal (ethics)

## Normative definition (this implant)

**Ideal** is used here as a pointer term: a standard of perfection or of
high excellence, for a state of affairs, a person, a life or a society,
held up for aspiration or comparison, in whichever sense a cited source
gives it. On every page the sense is named (Kantian, Moorean,
axiological, personal, perfectionist, Rawlsian).
Heyd (SEP 2024, §1) gives one example of the use: "For instance, the state of affairs of a world with no war is a moral ideal and the individual Socrates is virtuous, whereas the practice of paying back debts is obligatory and acts of theft are prohibited."

Why this definition: the sources do not agree on what an ideal is or what
force it has. For Kant it is an idea of reason "in individuo" with
"practical power—as regulative principles"; for Moore (1903, §110) the
word is "ambiguous" among three senses; Heyd (§1) reports the view that
ideals "can only be commended and recommended but not strictly required",
while the anti-supererogationists he reports (§3.1) deny that morality
splits into an ideal level and a required level; in Hare's later
framework, as Price reports it (SEP 2019, §4), ideals "register" as
universal preferences. Whether an ideal binds, only invites, or is to be
weighed like a preference is the question these debates are about
(supererogation, the [demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md),
state neutrality). Adopting one of these accounts as the implant's own
would take a side in them; the implant reports each sense with its holder
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[Words are contracts](../../conventions/words-are-contracts.md)).

1. **Kant: the ideal of reason, the kingdom of ends, the ideal of holiness.**
   [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md), *Critique of Pure Reason*, "Of the Ideal in
   General" (Meiklejohn trans.; AA III 383): "But still further removed than the idea from objective reality is the Ideal, by which term I understand the idea, not in concreto, but in individuo—as an individual thing, determinable or determined by the idea alone."
   On its practical use (AA III 384): "Without rising to these speculative heights, we are bound to confess that human reason contains not only ideas, but ideals, which possess, not, like those of Plato, creative, but certainly practical power—as regulative principles, and form the basis of the perfectibility of certain actions."
   His example: "But the wise man of the Stoics is an ideal, that is to say, a human being existing only in thought and in complete conformity with the idea of wisdom."
   and "As the idea provides a rule, so the ideal serves as an archetype for the perfect and complete determination of the copy."
   In the *Groundwork* (1785, Abbott trans.), of the kingdom of ends:
   "It is certainly only an ideal." (4:433). In the *Critique of Practical
   Reason* (1788, Abbott trans.; AA V 32): "This holiness of will is, however, a practical idea, which must necessarily serve as a type to which finite rational beings can only approximate indefinitely, and which the pure moral law, which is itself on this account called holy, constantly and rightly holds before their eyes."
   and, of the love commandment (AA V 83): "That law of all laws, therefore, like all the moral precepts of the Gospel, exhibits the moral disposition in all its perfection, in which, viewed as an ideal of holiness, it is not attainable by any creature, but yet is the pattern which we should strive to approach, and in an uninterrupted but infinite progress become like to."
   Heyd (§3.3) reads Kant's two levels this way: "Even Kant, who suggests the ideal of the Kingdom of Ends in which members of the moral community exercise their free will (Wille) by the necessity of their nature, believes that imperfect moral creatures like us have a free choice (Willkür) between good and evil."
2. **Moore: three senses of "ideal".** [Moore](../thinkers/g-e-moore.md),
   *Principia Ethica* (1903), ch. VI "The Ideal", §110: "The title of this chapter is ambiguous. When we call a state of things ‘ideal’ we may mean three distinct things, which have only this in common: that we always do mean to assert, of the state of things in question, not only that it is good in itself, but that it is good in itself in a much higher degree than many other things."
   (1) "By this is meant the best state of things conceivable, the Summum Bonum or Absolute Good."
   (2) "But this conception may be quite clearly distinguished from a second, namely, (2) that of the best possible state of things in this world."
   — "It is in this sense that Utopias are said to be Ideals."
   (3) "But, thirdly, we may mean by calling a state of things ‘ideal’ merely (3) that it is good in itself in a high degree."
   Moore says the chapter is principally concerned with sense (3); his
   answer (§113) is quoted on his page. On Kant (ch. IV §67): "Such an assertion is made by Kant when he tells us that his ‘Kingdom of Ends’ is the ideal."
   Hurka (SEP 2024, §3) names Moore's normative view: "According to his ideal consequentialism, what is right is in large part what most promotes loving personal relationships and aesthetic appreciation for all persons everywhere."
3. **Ideals on the value side of morality (the supererogation literature).**
   Heyd (§1) describes two categories of moral judgment, "the axiological and the deontic."
   "The former refers to goodness, ideals and virtues; the latter to what ought to be done, to duties and obligations, to justice and rights."
   "Consequently, the deontic sphere of morality is often taken as describing the minimal conditions of morality, the basic requirements of social morality that secure a just society, while the axiological sphere aims at higher ideals which can only be commended and recommended but not strictly required."
   "Ideals of goodness and virtue, in their open-ended texture, cannot be similarly expected of everyone and their determination is not subjected to the strict condition of “‘ought’ implies ‘can’.”"
   Heyd (§3.3) places one view of supererogation here: "This view of supererogation locates it in the open-ended dimension of morality, that of ideals rather than principles, what Urmson calls “the higher flights of morality” and Bergson (1956) “the morality of aspiration.”"
   Religious ideals, as Heyd reports them (§1): "Forgiveness and love of one’s enemies are also religious ideals that originate in the New Testament and were sometimes given a supererogatory interpretation in later Church doctrine."
   and, for Jewish thought, the question "is there an independent system of moral norms and ideals which is not directly derived from the Halakhic, commandment-based, legally binding (and enforceable) law (Lichtenstein 1975 and Lebens 2023)."
   Pelikan and Sanders (Britannica, "Jesus", Ethics) report a reading of
   the perfection sayings and then their own: "The impossibility of being perfect during a full lifetime leads some modern interpreters to propose that Jesus intended these admonitions to be only an ideal, not a requirement. It is more likely, however, that Jesus the eschatological prophet regarded perfection as quite possible during the short period before the arrival of the Son of Man."
4. **Ideals as universal preferences (Hare).** [Hare](../thinkers/hare.md),
   as Price reports *Freedom and Reason* (1963) (§4): "In Freedom and Reason, Hare allows the argument to be evaded by the “fanatic” who is so committed to some impersonal ideal (say that debtors deserve a hard time) that he is willing to disregard his own personal interests"
   On the later Hare, Price (§4): "In their practical force, ideals are equivalent to universal preferences that differ from personal preferences in their content, but owe their moral weight to the prevalence and intensity of whatever preferences their realization would satisfy."
   "Moral ideals register within this framework simply as universal preferences; to allow one’s own ideals to override more intense or prevalent desires or ideals of others is a kind of egoism, and so excluded."
   [Mackie](../thinkers/mackie.md) (1977, §8, p. 37) cites "what Hare calls 'ideals' or, less kindly. 'fanaticism'."
5. **Personal ideals of life (Strawson).** P. F. Strawson, "Social Morality
   and Individual Ideal", *Philosophy* 36 (1961): 1–17, DOI
   [10.1017/S003181910005779X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S003181910005779X)
   (verified; only the opening paragraph, reproduced in the Crossref
   record, was read): "Men make for themselves pictures of ideal forms of life. Such pictures are various and may be in sharp opposition to each other; and one and the same individual may be captivated by different and sharply conflicting pictures at different times."
   His list of examples ends: "any of these ideas, and a great many others too, may form the core and substance of a personal ideal."
6. **Ideals of the good (perfectionism and neutrality).** Wall (SEP 2017,
   §3.1): "Many contemporary writers on politics reject perfectionism and hold that the state should be neutral among rival understandings of the good (Dworkin 1978; Ackerman 1980; Larmore 1987; Rawls 1993)."
   Their case as Wall reports it: "Defenders of state neutrality often defend the doctrine by appealing to the ideal of public reason."
   "Public reasons, they argue, must be shareable in a way that excludes appeal to controversial ideals of the good."
   A perfectionist reply he reports: "These perfectionists can claim further that the truth of value pluralism explains how fully reasonable people can adopt and pursue different ideals of the good."
   On perfectionism's own ideal (§1.2): "This looks like a monistic ideal, one that identifies a single form of life as best for all human beings."
   followed by Wall's assessment: "But, in fact, the ideal leaves many issues open."
7. **Ideal theory (Rawls).** Wenar (SEP 2021, §2.3) on
   [Rawls](../thinkers/rawls.md): "Ideal theory makes two types of idealizing assumptions about its subject matter."
   "First, ideal theory assumes that all actors (citizens or societies) are generally willing to comply with whatever principles are chosen."
   "Second, ideal theory assumes reasonably favorable social conditions, wherein citizens and societies are able to abide by principles of political cooperation."
   "Completing ideal theory first, Rawls says, yields a systematic understanding of how to reform our non-ideal world, and fixes a vision (mentioned above) of what is the best that can be hoped for."
   Wenar also calls one of Rawls's principles an ideal in the sense of a
   standard (§4.3): "The difference principle thus expresses a positive ideal, an ideal of social unity."
   Korsgaard (1986) draws on this distinction for Kant's ethics: "morality itself sometimes allows or even requires us to do something that from an ideal perspective is wrong" (p. 327), and "When dealing with evil circumstances we may depart from this ideal." (p. 346).

## Collisions

- **Ideal vs duty.** In sense 3 an ideal is commended, not required (Heyd
  §1); in Kant's sense 1 the holy will is a "type" that the moral law
  "holds before" finite beings. Anti-supererogationists, as Heyd reports
  them (§3.1), deny the split itself: "Normativity is one and cannot be split into two levels, that of the good (the desirable, the ideal, the recommended) and that of the required (the obligatory, the prescribed)."
  Structural note (this implant): a page that calls an act "ideal" does not
  thereby say whether it is obligatory; the sense is named.
- **Moore's "ideal" vs Kant's "Ideal".** Moore's senses (§110) are states of
  things good in a high degree; Kant's Ideal is an idea of reason "in
  individuo", such as the Stoic sage. Moore applies his word to Kant's
  kingdom of ends (§67); the two technical terms are not the same term.
- **Ideal theory vs moral ideals.** Wenar's "ideal theory" (sense 7) names
  idealizing assumptions (full compliance, favourable conditions), not a
  standard of excellence for a person. Korsgaard's "ideal perspective" and
  the "ideal worlds" of deontic logic (McConnell, SEP "Moral Dilemmas" §5,
  citing Holbo 2002: "A more complicated response is to grant that the crucial deontic principles hold, but only in ideal worlds.";
  see [moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)) are further,
  separate uses.
- **Ideal vs idealism.** "Idealists such as Bradley" (Hurka, SEP on Moore,
  §4) names a metaphysical school, not holders of moral ideals.
- **Ideal justice.** Miller (SEP "Justice", §2.1) contrasts what is due
  under existing rules with "what the person should get under a regime of ideal justice" — see
  [justice](justice.md).
- **Autonomy as an ideal or a right.** Feinberg's list, as Christman
  reports it on the [autonomy](autonomy.md) page, includes "a personal ideal"
  among four meanings of autonomy; Wall (§3.2) reports a view on which
  "Autonomy, on this view, is a sovereign right, not an ideal to be promoted (Feinberg 1989)."

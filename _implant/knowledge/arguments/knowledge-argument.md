# The knowledge argument (Mary's room)

An argument page of the [arguments](index.md) branch, bearing on the hub
[What is consciousness?](../problems/consciousness.md). Written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md)
and graded by [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md):
the reconstruction is the implant's, every verdict on a premise belongs
to whoever gave it.

## Earliest primary source

Frank Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia", *The Philosophical Quarterly* 32
(127), April 1982, pp. 127–136
([doi:10.2307/2960077](https://doi.org/10.2307/2960077); excerpt:
`raw/jackson-1982-epiphenomenal-qualia-mary.md`). The Mary passage is on
p. 130:

> "Mary is a brilliant scientist who is, for whatever reason, forced to
> investigate the world from a black and white room via a black and white
> television monitor. [...] What will happen when Mary is released from
> her black and white room or is given a colour television monitor? Will
> she learn anything or not? It seems just obvious that she will learn
> something about the world and our visual experience of it. But then it
> is inescapable that her previous knowledge was incomplete. But she had
> all the physical information. Ergo there is more to have than that, and
> Physicalism is false." (p. 130)

Two gaps between the popular story and the text, both in the excerpt:

- The paper's title thesis is not the Mary case but that "the view that
  qualia are epiphenomenal is a perfectly possible one" (p. 127, §IV);
  Mary is introduced as a variant of an earlier "Fred" case (a man who
  sees two reds), and Jackson calls both "essentially rhetorical devices"
  (excerpt's context note).
- Jackson later gave the argument up. The SEP entry "Qualia: The
  Knowledge Argument" (Fall 2024, Nida-Rümelin & O Conaill), §5, reports:
  "Jackson changed his mind. Jackson (1995) argues that knowledge about
  qualia is impossible if qualia are epiphenomenal and he concludes that
  something must be wrong with the knowledge argument. In Jackson (2003)
  and Jackson (2007) he argues that the argument goes wrong in
  presupposing a false view about sensory experience and that it can be
  answered by endorsing strong representationalism"
  ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/qualia-knowledge/),
  §5). Jackson 2003 is "Mind and Illusion", *Royal Institute of
  Philosophy Supplement* 53, pp. 251–271
  ([doi:10.1017/S1358246100008365](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1358246100008365));
  the excerpt holds it bibliographically only, so the retraction is
  documented through SEP's report, not Jackson's later text.

## Standard reconstruction

Reconstruction by this implant from the quoted text (manifest
[G4](../../vision/manifest.md)); Jackson's p. 130 paragraph supplies each
step in order.

- P1. Before release, Mary has "all the physical information there is to
  obtain about what goes on when we see ripe tomatoes" (p. 130).
- P2. On release "she will learn something about the world and our visual
  experience of it" (p. 130).
- P3. If she learns something, "her previous knowledge was incomplete"
  (p. 130).
- P4. If physicalism is true, then someone with all the physical
  information has complete knowledge (the suppressed premise the "Ergo"
  requires; marked P4* because the text does not state it).
- C. "There is more to have than that, and Physicalism is false" (p. 130).

Form: deductive; a reductio of physicalism. Checked with the implant's
[logic tool](../../skills/analyse-an-argument.md) as a propositional
schema (A = all physical information, L = learns, I = incomplete,
P = physicalism):

```
P1: A    P2: L    P3: L -> I    P4*: P -> (A -> ~I)    C: ~P
VALID
```

Validity is the tool's report; whether P2 and P4* are true is the map in
the next sections.

## Supports

- [Dualism](../positions/dualism.md) — the conclusion is "Physicalism is
  false" (p. 130); Jackson's own 1982 form was epiphenomenalist: qualia
  exist and are causally idle (§IV, p. 127).
- [Panpsychism](../positions/panpsychism.md) — by a different route.
  Strawson does not use Mary, but takes the reality of experience as a
  premise: one is "not a real physicalist, if you deny the existence of
  the phenomenon whose existence is more certain than the existence of
  anything else: experience" ([Strawson 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199267422.003.0003),
  p. 3; excerpt: `raw/strawson-2006-realistic-monism.md`), and concludes
  that "real physicalists must give up NE" — the thesis that physical
  stuff is "wholly and utterly non-experiential" (pp. 11, 25). A
  structural note the implant adds, not a verdict (manifest
  [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)): Strawson accepts what P2 asserts
  (experience is real and known) and rejects C, by redefining
  physicalism rather than denying the premise.

## Which premise is disputed, and by whom

| Premise | Who disputes it | On what grounds, cited |
|---|---|---|
| P2 (Mary learns something) | Dennett, as SEP reports him | The Multiple Drafts Model "denies the existence of qualia and thus rejects any attempt to distinguish conscious states from nonconscious states by their presence" (SEP "Consciousness", Fall 2024, §9.4, reporting Dennett 1991; excerpt: `raw/dennett-1991-consciousness-explained-via-sep.md`). The excerpt holds no sentence of Dennett's on Mary herself. |
| P2 | Frankish 2016 | Illusionism "claims that phenomenal consciousness is illusory; experiences do not really have qualitative, 'what-it's-like' properties, whether physical or non-physical" ([Frankish 2016](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/contentone/imp/jcs/2016/00000023/f0020011/art00002), §1.2, eprint p. 3; excerpt: `raw/frankish-2016-illusionism.md`). What introspection reports is a "quasi-phenomenal property … a non-phenomenal, physical property (perhaps a complex, gerrymandered one) that introspection typically misrepresents as phenomenal" (§1.2, p. 3). |
| P4* (physical information exhausts what there is to know) | Jackson after 1995, as SEP reports him | The argument "goes wrong in presupposing a false view about sensory experience and … can be answered by endorsing strong representationalism" (SEP "Qualia: The Knowledge Argument", Fall 2024, §5). |
| C, as a conclusion the premises license | Levine 1983, for his own version | "One cannot conclude from my version of the argument that materialism is false" ([Levine 1983](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0114.1983.tb00207.x), p. 354; excerpt: `raw/levine-1983-materialism-and-qualia-explanatory-gap.md`). |
| C | Nagel 1974, for the bat argument | The excerpt's context note records that Nagel writes "it would be a mistake to conclude that physicalism must be false" and that we do not currently understand how it could be true (`raw/nagel-1974-what-is-it-like-to-be-a-bat.md`; the page number of that sentence is not in the excerpts held). |

The prior physicalist commitment the conclusion contradicts, stated
before Jackson wrote: "Sensations are nothing over and above brain
processes" ([Smart 1959](https://doi.org/10.2307/2182164), p. 145;
excerpt: `raw/smart-1959-sensations-and-brain-processes.md`), motivated
by Occam's razor and the refusal to accept sensations as "nomological
danglers" (p. 142). The excerpts hold no reply by Smart to Jackson.

## Objections

- **The qualia-denial objection** (Dennett via SEP §9.4; Frankish 2016
  §1.2) — there is nothing of the kind Mary is said to learn; what needs
  explaining is "why experiences seem to have phenomenal properties",
  Frankish's "illusion problem" (introduction, eprint p. 1).
- **The epiphenomenalism objection** (Jackson 1995, as SEP §5 reports) —
  "knowledge about qualia is impossible if qualia are epiphenomenal"; the
  1982 paper's §IV defence of epiphenomenalism and its knowledge argument
  are, on this later view of Jackson's, in tension.
- **The representationalist objection** (Jackson 2003, 2007, as SEP §5
  reports) — the argument presupposes "a false view about sensory
  experience". The excerpts do not hold Jackson's own statement of it.
- **The epistemic-only objection** (Levine 1983, p. 354) — the intuition
  behind arguments of this family supports "a closely related
  epistemological thesis", an explanatory gap, and not the metaphysical
  one; see [Leibniz's mill](leibniz-mill.md) for that lineage.

## Replies

Replies by Jackson to these objections are not in the excerpts held; his
1982 §IV answers the epiphenomenalism objection in advance by "arguing
that the view that qualia are epiphenomenal is a perfectly possible one"
(p. 127). Against the qualia-denial line, the reply on record in the raw
files is Strawson's: experience is "the phenomenon whose existence is
more certain than the existence of anything else" (2006, p. 3). Frankish
himself reports that illusionism "is often dismissed out of hand as
failing to 'take consciousness seriously' (Chalmers, 1996)" (2016,
introduction) — his report of Chalmers, not a text the excerpts hold.

## Variants

- **Nagel's bat (1974)** — the relative Jackson's §III contrasts with his
  own. "Every subjective phenomenon is essentially connected with a
  single point of view, and it seems inevitable that an objective,
  physical theory will abandon that point of view"
  ([Nagel 1974](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914), p. 437); bat sonar "is
  not similar in its operation to any sense that we possess, and there is
  no reason to suppose that it is subjectively like anything we can
  experience or imagine" (p. 438). The gap with the popular story: the
  paper does not conclude that physicalism is false (excerpt's context
  note). See [Nagel](../thinkers/nagel.md).
- **The modal argument** — Jackson's §II contrast; the zombie and Kripke
  line, on its own page: [zombie argument](zombie-argument.md).
- **Levine's explanatory gap (1983)** — "My purpose in this paper is to
  transform Kripke's argument from a metaphysical one into an
  epistemological one" (p. 354); what C-fibre firing leaves unexplained
  "is why pain should feel the way it does" (p. 357). Levine calls his
  version "a weaker attack than Kripke's" (p. 354).
- **Chalmers's hard problem (1995)** — the same family in the form of a
  question: "Why doesn't all this information-processing go on 'in the
  dark', free of any inner feel?" ([Chalmers 1995](https://consc.net/papers/facing.html),
  §2; excerpt: `raw/chalmers-1995-facing-up-hard-problem.md`), with
  "an explanatory gap (a term due to Levine 1983) between the functions
  and experience" (§2). Chalmers's gene analogy states the contrast:
  asking how DNA "is a gene" after its function is explained is "a
  conceptual mistake", asking how information "is experienced" after its
  processing is explained is "not … a conceptual mistake" (§2). See
  [Chalmers](../thinkers/chalmers.md).
- **Survey data, reported as data:** of 998 respondents to the 2020
  PhilPapers Survey, 62.42 % accept or lean towards there being a hard
  problem, 29.76 % towards not; physicalism 51.93 % against
  non-physicalism 32.08 % (N = 1733)
  ([Bourget & Chalmers 2023](https://doi.org/10.3998/phimp.2109); excerpt:
  `raw/bourget-chalmers-2023-philpapers-survey-consciousness-and-amcs-2023-open-letter.md`).
  The survey has no question on Mary.

## Vocabulary

Terms the argument turns on, each open work in
[vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md): *qualia* (Jackson's "qualia freak",
p. 127), *physical information* (P1), *physicalism* (C), *epiphenomenal*
(§IV), *explanatory gap* (Levine 1983), *quasi-phenomenal* (Frankish
2016). Whether "information" in P1 and "knowledge" in P3 are the same
kind of thing is where the representationalist objection lives.

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and slavery:** "Born sometime in the 50s C.E. in Hierapolis, a Greek city of Asia Minor, Epictetus spent a portion of his life as the slave of Epaphroditus, an important administrator in the court of Nero." (Graver §1).
- **Teacher:** "The circumstances of Epictetus’s education are likewise unknown, except that he studied for a time under Musonius Rufus, a Roman senator and Stoic philosopher who taught intermittently at Rome." (§1).
- **Expulsion, Nicopolis, death:** "Eventually receiving his freedom, he began lecturing on his own account but was forced to leave the city, presumably by the edict of Domitian (in 89) banning philosophers from the Italian peninsula. He then established his own school at Nicopolis, an important communications hub and administrative center in Epirus, on the Adriatic coast of northwest Greece, and remained there teaching and lecturing until his death around 135." (§1).
  Durand (SEP "Stoicism", §1.1) gives a different year for the expulsion:
  "Epictetus, a former slave, was expelled from Rome by Domitian along with
  other philosophers in 93 CE" (excerpt:
  `raw/sep-stoicism-fall-2024-school-and-doctrines.md`). The two SEP
  entries differ (89 vs 93); this page does not choose.
- **The Discourses' date:** "The teaching represented in the Discourses is that of his later career, around the year 108 by Millar’s (1965) dating, at which time he walked with a limp attributed variously to arthritis or to physical abuse during his time of slavery." (§1).
- **Who wrote the Discourses down:** "According to their preface, the Discourses are not the writing of Epictetus but are ghostwritten by the essayist and historiographer Arrian of Nicomedia in an effort to convey the personal impact of his instruction." (§1). The dissenting view, as Graver reports it: "A few scholars, including especially Dobbin (1998), argue that Epictetus must have composed them himself, the role of Arrian being merely to preserve a mild fiction of orality." (§1).
- **School:** Graver: "The essentials of Epictetus’s thought derive from the early or foundational period of Stoicism, from the third-century writings of Zeno of Citium, Cleanthes, and Chrysippus." (§2). Durand lists him among the Roman-period Stoics (see [Stoicism](../schools/stoicism.md)).

## Problems addressed

- **What is in our power (the dichotomy of control).** No problem page
  yet. *Encheiridion* 1 (Long): "Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion ([Greek: hupolaepsis]), movement towards a thing ([Greek: hormae]), desire, aversion ([Greek: echchlisis]), turning from a thing; and in a word, whatever are our acts. Not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts."
  and "And the things in our power are by nature free, not subject to restraint or hindrance; but the things not in our power are weak, slavish, subject to restraint, in the power of others."
  *Discourses* 1.1 (Higginson): "As it was fit, then, this most excellent and superior faculty alone, a right use of the appearances of things, the gods have placed in our own power; but all other matters they have not placed in our power."
- **Volition (prohairesis) and freedom.** Graver (preamble): "Epictetus’s chief concerns are with integrity, self-management, and personal freedom, which he advocates by demanding of his students a thorough examination of two central ideas, the capacity he terms ‘volition’ (prohairesis) and the correct use of impressions (chrēsis tōn phantasiōn)"
  "Volition, Epictetus argues, is “by nature unimpeded” (1.17.21), and it is for this reason that freedom is for him an inalienable characteristic of the human being." (§4.3);
  "It is the volition that is the real person, the true self of the individual." (§4.3).
  *Discourses* 1.1 (Higginson): "You will fetter my leg, but not Zeus himself can get the better of my free will."
  On autonomy under a Zeus-governed order, Graver's reconstruction of his
  reply: "Epictetus would reply that autonomy is guaranteed not by the absence of antecedent causes but by the very nature of the reasoning faculty." (§4.7).
- **The good and the indifferent.** Graver: "What is ultimately worth having, the “good of humankind,” consists in “a certain disposition of one’s volition” (1.8.16)."
  and "“The materials of action are indifferent, but the use we make of them is not indifferent” (2.5.1)." (§4.4).
- **Emotion and judgement.** *Encheiridion* 5 (Long): "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things; for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death that it is terrible, is the terrible thing."
  Graver: "Grief, fear, envy, desire, and every form of anxiety, result from the incorrect supposition that happiness is to be found outside oneself (2.16, 3.13.10, etc.)."
  and the limit: "Epictetus’s conception of emotional adjustment is not that one should be “unfeeling like a statue” (3.2.4)." (§4.5).
  The method of testing impressions, *Discourses* 2.18 (Higginson): "But in the first place, be not hurried away by excitement; but say, Semblance, wait for me a little. Let me see what you are, and what you represent. Let me try you."
- **[Omnipotence paradox](../problems/omnipotence-paradox.md)** — what
  divine power cannot do. Graver (§4.8): "Zeus’s power is limited in that he cannot do what it is logically impossible to do. He could not cause a person to be born before his parents (1.12.28–29), and he could not have made volition execute any choices but its own (1.1.23, 1.17.27)."
  In *Discourses* 1.1 (Higginson) Zeus says: "O Epictetus, if it had been possible, I had made this little body and property of thine free, and not liable to hindrance. But now do not mistake; it is not thy own, but only a finer mixture of clay."
  Structural note (manifest [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)): the problem
  page's Aquinas position (omnipotence ranging over the absolutely
  possible only, ST I q.25 a.3) and Epictetus' Zeus, as Graver reports him, both
  restrict divine power to the logically possible; no read source draws
  this comparison.
- **[Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md)** — preconceptions.
  Graver (§4.4): "We are not wrong to believe that whatever is good is advantageous to us and worthy of unconditional pursuit, for this is just the “preconception” (prolēpsis) of good which all human beings possess (1.22)."
  The problem page reports, through Schwab's review of Fine (2014), that
  Stoic prolepsis is read as the school's response to the paradox, and
  Schwab's doubt about that reading
  (`raw/schwab-2014-review-of-fine-2014-possibility-of-inquiry.md`). No read
  source names Epictetus in that debate. *Discourses* 2.17 (Higginson):
  "What is the first business of one who studies philosophy? To part with self-conceit. For it is impossible for any one to begin to learn what he thinks that he already knows."

## Works

## In dialogue with

- **[Socrates](socrates.md) and [Plato](plato.md).** Graver: "The clearest instance of such influence concerns Plato, for Epictetus draws much inspiration from the Socrates depicted in Plato’s shorter dialogues." (§2). Socrates is the example in *Encheiridion* 5 (above). Of Epictetus' three modes of discourse, "As Long (2002) has noted, the three modes are associated respectively with Diogenes the Cynic, with Socrates, and with Zeno of Citium, the founder of the Stoic school (3.21.19; cf. 2.12.5)." (§5).
- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md).** Graver: "An argument has sometimes been made for Aristotelian influence, primarily because Epictetus’s favored term prohairesis (see section 4.3 below) is prominent in Nicomachean Ethics 3.1–5 as a quasi-technical term"
  Against it, Graver: "But neither Aristotle nor any author in the Aristotelian tradition is ever mentioned in the Discourses. It is better to make the provisional assumption that his interest in volition derives, like other main elements of his philosophy, from the early Stoa, though with greater emphasis." (§2).
- **[Epicurus](epicurus.md).** Graver: "Epicureanism he identifies with the pleasure principle and accordingly despises (3.7)." (§2).
- **Cynics.** Graver: "He is impressed with Cynicism, but sees it as a vocation to itinerant teaching and bare-bones living rather than as a body of doctrine (3.22)." (§2).
- **Marcus Aurelius** (no thinker page yet; [marcus.md](marcus.md) is Ruth
  Barcan Marcus). Graver: "The emperor Marcus Aurelius was never in fact his pupil, but was so deeply impressed with what he had read as to consider himself a follower of the freedman philosopher." (§6).
  Marcus, *Meditations* I.IV (Casaubon tr., Gutenberg numbering), crediting Rusticus: "whom also I must thank that ever I lighted upon Epictetus his _Hypomnemata_, or moral commentaries and common-factions: which also he gave me of his own."
  He quotes him: "What art thou, that better and divine part excepted, but as Epictetus said well, a wretched soul, appointed to carry a carcass up and down?" (IV.XXXIV)
  and "'Of the free will there is no thief or robber:' out of Epictetus" (XI.XXXI).
  Excerpt: `raw/marcus-aurelius-meditations-casaubon-epictetus-mentions.md`
  ([Gutenberg #2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2680)).
- **Later readers** — Origen, Simplicius, Pascal, [Descartes](descartes.md):
  see Reception.

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Graver's own assessment (preamble):** "Epictetus has had significant influence on the popular moralistic tradition, but he is more than a moralizer; his lucid resystematization and challenging application of Stoic ethics qualify him as an important philosopher in his own right."
- **Antiquity.** Graver: "In the early third century Origen remarks on the popularity of Epictetus with his own contemporaries, which he finds to rival that of Plato (Contra Celsum 6.2)."
  "More demonstrable is the homage paid to Epictetus by Simplicius, the sixth-century Aristotle commentator, who composed a long philosophical commentary on the Encheiridion combining Stoic elements with his own Neoplatonism." (§6).
- **Christian readers, for and against.** Graver: "The Encheiridion was translated into Latin by Poliziano in 1497 and during the subsequent two centuries became exceptionally popular in Europe."
  "Seventeenth-century intellectuals like Guillaume du Vair, Justus Lipsius, and Thomas Gataker generally found Epictetus’s Stoicism to be fully compatible with Christianity; see the discussion in Brooke (2006). Pascal reacted against this perception; he admired Epictetus as a moralist but regarded it as sheer arrogance to believe that the human psyche is part of the divine and can be perfected by one’s own efforts." (§6).
  The doctrine Pascal objected to, in Graver's report: "our minds are actually fragments of Zeus’s mind, “parts and offshoots of his own being” (1.14.6, 2.8.10–12)." (§4.2).
- **Descartes.** Graver: "Descartes adopted a recognizably Epictetan value system as part of his personal ethics." (§6).
- **Stockdale.** James Bond Stockdale, *Courage under Fire: Testing
  Epictetus's Doctrines in a Laboratory of Human Behavior* (Hoover
  Institution, 1993, ISBN 0-8179-3692-0;
  [PDF](https://hoover-s3-website.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/uploads/documents/StockdaleCourage.pdf);
  excerpt: `raw/stockdale-1993-courage-under-fire-epictetus.md`). The
  author note: "As the senior naval prisoner of war officer in Hanoi for eight years, he was tortured fifteen times, put in leg irons for two years, and put in solitary confinement for four years."
  Stockdale reports receiving the book at Stanford from Philip Rhinelander: "On my last session, he reached high in his wall of books and brought down a copy of The Enchiridion." (p. 1),
  and, on ejecting over North Vietnam: "Five years down there, at least. I’m leaving the world of technology and entering the world of Epictetus." (p. 7).
  His reading of *Encheiridion* 1: "a Stoic always kept separate files in his mind for (A) those things that are “up to him” and (B) those things that are “not up to him.”" (p. 7).
- **Cognitive therapies.** Cavanna, Purpura, Riva, Nacinovich & Seri,
  "The Western origins of mindfulness therapy in ancient Rome",
  *Neurological Sciences* 44 (2023): 1861–1869,
  [doi:10.1007/s10072-023-06651-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10072-023-06651-w)
  (excerpt: `raw/cavanna-2023-stoic-roots-of-cbt-ellis-beck-epictetus.md`;
  the books by Ellis and Beck are quoted through this paper, not read):
  "Both Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck (the founders of rational emotive behaviour therapy and cognitive behavioural therapy, respectively) explicitly acknowledged the role of Stoicism as the philosophical precursor of their treatment approaches"
  They quote Ellis (1962): "The truths of Stoicism were perhaps best set forth by Epictetus, who, in the first century AD wrote in the Enchiridion: ‘Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them’."
  and correct his dating: "Despite the chronological inaccuracy (Epictetus’ words were actually written down in the ‘Εγχειρίδιον’ — literally, ‘Handbook’ — by his disciple Arrian in the first half of the second century), Ellis’ statement clearly shows that he was fully aware of his debt to his illustrious predecessor."
  Ellis (2005, as quoted): "I am happy to say that in the 1950s I managed to bring Epictetus out of near-obscurity and make him famous all over again"
  The authors' assessment of that claim: "Such a bold statement might sound like an exaggeration; however, it was mainly through Ellis’ writings that Stoicism exerted its influence on Aaron Beck, the father of second-wave cognitive behavioural therapy."
  Beck et al. (1979, as quoted): "The philosophical origins of cognitive therapy can be traced back to the Stoic philosophers, particularly Zeno of Citium (fourth century BC), Chrysippus, Cicero, Seneca, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius."

## Vocabulary

- *Prohairesis*: "volition" in Graver: "In studying his usage it is helpful to remember that his favored term prohairesis refers more often to the capacity for choice than it does to particular acts of choosing. The word is variously translated; the rendering “volition” is adopted here as in Long 2002." (§4.3).
  Higginson's *Discourses* 1.1 renders the matching idea "free will". No
  vocabulary page covers it yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Externals*: Graver: "These things are all “externals”; that is, things external to the sphere of volition." (§4.3).
- *Prolēpsis* ("preconception"): see the Meno's paradox entry above.
- *Use of impressions* (*chrēsis tōn phantasiōn*); Long translates
  "appearances", Higginson "semblances".

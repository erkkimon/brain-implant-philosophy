## Facts a claim depends on

From Menzies (excerpts in `raw/mackie-biography-adb-menzies-2012-and-wikipedia-rev-1374503773.md`):

- **Birth:** "John Leslie Mackie (1917-1981), philosopher, was born on 25 August 1917 at Killara, Sydney".
- **Education:** "John was educated at Knox Grammar School and the University of Sydney (BA, 1938), graduating with first-class honours in Greek and Latin and the G. S. Caird scholarship in philosophy." "Having won the (William Charles) Wentworth travelling fellowship, he went to England to read literae humaniores at Oriel College, Oxford (BA, 1940; MA, 1944) and graduated with first-class honours."
- **War service:** "After serving in the Middle East (1942-43) and Italy (1943-45), he was mentioned in despatches and demobilised as a temporary captain in 1946."
- **Posts:** "That year Mackie returned to Sydney to become a lecturer in moral and political philosophy at the university." "He was appointed to the chair of philosophy and psychology at the University of Otago, New Zealand, in 1955, but went back to Sydney in 1959 to succeed his former teacher John Anderson as Challis professor of philosophy." "In 1963 he left for England to become the inaugural professor of philosophy at the University of York." "In 1967 he was elected a fellow and tutor in philosophy at University College, Oxford, and in 1978 the university promoted him to a personal readership. He was a Radcliffe philosophy fellow in 1971-73 and a fellow of the British Academy from 1974."
- **Teacher:** John Anderson (quoted above). Menzies: "Mackie was far from an uncritical disciple, distancing himself from the more polemical and purely programmatic aspects of Andersonianism."
- **Death:** "he died of cancer on 12 December 1981 at Oxford". *The Miracle of Theism* appeared in 1982; its Preface is signed "J.L.M." and dated September 1981.
- **Religious stance, in his words:** "While my own view is atheist, I have tried to give a full and fair hearing to the opposing case." (*Miracle*, Preface).

## Problems addressed

- **Whether there are objective values (metaethics).** No problem page yet. Mackie's thesis: "There are no objective values. This is a bald statement of the thesis of this chapter" (*Ethics* ch. 1 §1, p. 15). Because ordinary moral concepts claim objectivity, the denial is put forward as an "'error theory', a theory that although most people in making moral judgements implicitly claim, among other things, to be pointing to something objectively prescriptive, these claims are all false." (§7, p. 35). "It is this that makes the name 'moral scepticism' appropriate." (§7, p. 35). Joyce (§3.2): "John Mackie (who coined the term “error theory” in 1977) argues that when we participate in moral discourse we commit ourselves to the existence of objective values and objective prescriptions, but there are no such things."
  Mackie names his two arguments: "Traditionally it has been supported by arguments of two main kinds, which I shall call the argument from relativity and the argument from queerness" (§7, p. 35).
  - *Argument from relativity.* "The argument from relativity has as its prerniss the well-known variation in moral codes from one society to another and from one period to another" (§8, p. 36; OCR spelling of the scan). His conclusion: "the argument from relativity has some force simply because the actual variations in the moral codes are more readily explained by the hypothesis that they reflect ways of life than by the hypothesis that they express perceptions, most of them seriously inadequate and badly distorted, of objective values." (§8, p. 37).
  - *Argument from queerness.* "This has two parts, one metaphysical, the other epistemological. If there were objective values, then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe." "Correspondingly, if we were aware of them, it would have to be by some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else." (§9, p. 38). An objective good would be sought "just because the end has to-be-pursuedness somehow built into it." (§9, p. 40).
  Joyce's summary (§3.2): "Moral facts place demands upon us, but (Mackie asks) how could such demands exist objectively? This would seem to require rules of conduct somehow written into the fabric of the universe, and nothing in our understanding of the objective naturalistic world (Mackie goes on) suggests that anything of the kind exists." Joyce adds: "Mackie occasionally cashes out his “argument from queerness” in terms of practical reasons." Sayre-McCord (§3) reads the argument as one about motivation: "Some error theorists do argue that combining cognitivism with motivational internalism results in an untenable position (Mackie 1977)."
- **[The Epicurean trilemma and the logical problem of evil](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md).** "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955), as Beebe (§1) quotes it: "Evil is a problem, for the theist, in that a contradiction is involved in the fact of evil on the one hand and belief in the omnipotence and omniscience of God on the other." and (§8): "Here it can be shown, not that religious beliefs lack rational support, but that they are positively irrational, that several parts of the essential theological doctrine are inconsistent with one another." Against the free-will reply (p. 209, as Beebe §4 quotes): "there was open to him the obviously better possibility of making beings who would act freely but always go right." Tooley (SEP "The Problem of Evil", §1.3) places him among those who "had defended incompatibility versions of the argument from evil" (`raw/sep-evil-fall-2024-logical-evidential-and-responses.md`).
  In 1982 Mackie concedes the inconsistency claim for one theodicy: "Since this defence is formally possible, and its principle involves no real abandonment of our ordinary view of the opposition between good and evil, we can concede that the problem of evil does not, after all, show that the central doctrines of theism are logically inconsistent with one another. But whether this offers a real solution of the problem is another question." (*Miracle* ch. 9(a), p. 154). His conclusion on the free will defence is quoted under *In dialogue with*, Plantinga.
- **[The omnipotence paradox](../problems/omnipotence-paradox.md).** Pearce (IEP "Omnipotence", §1a) gives the general form after Mackie: "could an omnipotent being make something it could not control (Mackie 1955: 210)?" (`raw/iep-omnipotence-pearce-stone-paradox-voluntarism.md`). Wikipedia (rev. 1371942568) reports the 1955 answer: "J. L. Mackie tried to resolve the paradox by distinguishing between first-order omnipotence (unlimited power to act) and second-order omnipotence (unlimited power to determine what powers to act things shall have)." (`raw/wikipedia-omnipotence-paradox-rev-1371942568-history-and-stone.md`). In 1982 he revised it: "When I originally raised this question, I thought that it was a genuine paradox, that the question could not be answered satisfactorily either in the affirmative or in the negative." "However, this was a mistake. Clearly, a god might have both first- and second-order omnipotence, so long as he did not exercise his second-order power in such a way as to limit his first-order power." (*Miracle* ch. 9(c), p. 160).
- **[The Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md).** Murphy (SEP "Theological Voluntarism", §2.1): "John Mackie, an atheist, and George Mavrodes, a theist, have both drawn from this the same moral: if there is a God, then the normativity of morality can be understood in theistic terms; otherwise, the normativity of morality is unintelligible (Mavrodes 1986; Mackie 1977, p. 48)." (`raw/sep-voluntarism-theological-fall-2024-goodness-and-arbitrariness.md`).
- **[The paradox of nihilism](../problems/paradox-of-nihilism.md)** (self-refutation). Baghramian & Carter (SEP "Relativism", §4.3.1): "J.L. Mackie, for instance, has argued that alethic absolutism is a requisite of a coherent notion of truth and that a claim to the effect that “There are no absolute truths” is absolutely self-refuting (Mackie 1964: 200)." (`raw/sep-relativism-fall-2024-self-refutation.md`; Mackie 1964, "Self-Refutation—a Formal Analysis", doi:[10.2307/2955461](https://doi.org/10.2307/2955461)).
- **[Fitch's paradox of knowability](../problems/fitchs-paradox-of-knowability.md).** Brogaard & Salerno (SEP, §1): "Mackie (1980) and Routley (1981), among others at the time, point to difficulties with this general position but ultimately agree that Fitch’s result is a refutation of the claim that all truths are knowable" (`raw/sep-fitch-paradox-fall-2024-proof-history-and-responses.md`; Mackie 1980, "Truth and knowability", doi:[10.1093/analys/40.2.90](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/40.2.90)).
- **[The repugnant conclusion](../problems/repugnant-conclusion.md).** Arrhenius, Ryberg & Tännsjö (SEP, §2.8) list him among those who accept it on the ground that "we are deluded by our intuition (Sikora 1978, 2004; Mackie 1985; Tännsjö 1992, 1998, 2002; Hare 1993; Ryberg 1996a, 2004)." (`raw/sep-repugnant-conclusion-fall-2024-mere-addition-and-responses.md`).
- **[The raven paradox](../problems/raven-paradox.md).** Fitelson & Hawthorne (2010, n. 9) list "(Mackie 1963)" among Bayesian approaches (`raw/fitelson-hawthorne-2010-bayesian-ravens.md`).
- **[The paradox of the court](../problems/paradox-of-the-court.md).** Suber (1990, note 2): "Although Lenzen's source of the paradox is J.L. Mackie, Truth, Probability and Paradox, Oxford University Press, 1973, p. 296, Mackie uses "when", not "if and only if"." (`raw/suber-1990-paradox-of-self-amendment-protagoras-v-euathlus.md`).

## In dialogue with

- **[Plato](plato.md)** (earlier). In the queerness argument: "The Form of the Good is such that knowledge of it provides the knower with both a direction and an overriding motive" (*Ethics* ch. 1 §9, p. 40).
- **[G. E. Moore](g-e-moore.md)** (earlier). "These points were recognized by Moore when he spoke of non-natural qualities" (*Ethics* ch. 1 §9, p. 38). - **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (earlier). - **[David Hume](hume.md)** (earlier). The *Miracle* title echoes Hume (quoted under *Works*); Mackie wrote *Hume's Moral Theory* (1980, Menzies). Cohon (SEP "Hume's Moral Philosophy", §6) names Mackie for the dispositional reading of Hume: "which understands moral evaluations as factual judgments to the effect that the evaluated trait or action is so constituted as to cause feelings of approval or disapproval in a (suitably characterized) spectator (Mackie, in one of his proposals)." (`raw/sep-hume-moral-fall-2024-practical-reason-four-readings-of-evaluation.md`).
- **[Epicurus](epicurus.md)** (earlier). The trilemma attributed to him and Mackie's 1955 form are reported together on the [problem page](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md).
- **Alvin Plantinga** (later; no page). Tooley (SEP "The Problem of Evil", §1.3): "Plantinga, starting out from an examination of John L. Mackie’s essay “Evil and Omnipotence” (1955), in which Mackie had defended an incompatibility version of the argument from evil, focuses mainly on the question of whether the existence of God is compatible with the existence of evil". The two sides, side by side:
  - *Plantinga* (1974, p. 190, as Beebe §4 quotes): "The essential point of the Free Will Defense is that the creation of a world containing moral good is a cooperative venture; it requires the uncoerced concurrence of significantly free creatures."
  - *Mackie* (1955, p. 209, as Beebe §4 quotes): "If God has made men such that in their free choices they sometimes prefer what is good and sometimes what is evil, why could he not have made men such that they always freely choose the good?"
  - *Mackie's 1982 reply:* "Plantinga has not rescued the free will defence but made its weakness all too clear." "it is obvious that my original criticism of the free will defence holds good: had there been such a god, it would have been open to him to create beings such that they would always freely choose the good." (*Miracle* ch. 9(f), p. 174). "In short, all forms of the free will defence fail, and since this defence alone had any chance of success there is no plausible theodicy on offer." He adds: "We cannot, indeed, take the problem of evil as a conclusive disproof of traditional theism" (p. 176).
  - Wikipedia (rev. 1374503773): "Mackie's views on this logical problem of evil prompted Alvin Plantinga to respond with the "free-will defense", which Mackie later responded in his The Miracle of Theism."
- **George Mavrodes** (contemporary; no page). Shared conclusion on theism and moral normativity (Murphy, quoted under *Problems addressed*).
- **[R. M. Hare](hare.md)** (contemporary). In *Ethics* §8 Mackie cites "what Hare calls 'ideals' or, less kindly. 'fanaticism'." (p. 37; punctuation as in the scan). Both are listed among accepters of the repugnant conclusion (SEP, §2.8, above).
- **[Derek Parfit](parfit.md)** (contemporary). Thanked in the *Miracle* Preface among those who sent "corrections and suggestions".
- **Richard Joyce, Ian Hinckfuss, Jonas Olson** (later; no pages). Joyce (§3.2): "Defenders of moral error theory include Mackie 1977, Hinckfuss 1987, Joyce 2001, and Olson 2014."
- **David Lewis** (later; no page). Quoted by Joyce (§3.2): "“Strictly speaking, Mackie is right: genuine values would have to meet an impossible condition, so it is an error to think there are any. Loosely speaking, the name may go to a claimant that deserves it imperfectly … What to make of the situation is mainly a matter of temperament” (Lewis [1989] 2000: 93)."
- **Richard Dawkins and Mary Midgley** (contemporaries; no pages). ## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing (Menzies).** "The influence that Mackie exerted on British, American and Australian philosophy was largely through the books he wrote late in his career." "His fundamental theoretical position was empirical realism; his method was close analysis of argument, relying on the inherent rationality of common sense and eschewing unnecessary logical technicalities."
- **On the error theory, two lines of objection (Joyce §3.2).** "If we consider Mackie’s argument, for example, then one kind of opponent will agree that objective values and prescriptions would be too weird to countenance, but maintain that it doesn’t matter because moral discourse is not committed to the existence of such strange creatures. Another kind of opponent will agree with Mackie that morality is committed to the existence of objective values and prescriptions, but maintain that there is nothing particularly strange about them."
  Mackie anticipates the second line: "Indeed, the best move for the moral objectivist is not to evade this issue, but to look for companions in guilt." (*Ethics* ch. 1 §9, p. 39).
- **A moral realist's reply (Sayre-McCord §3).** "Yet if the motivational internalism one embraces for moral claims has a parallel for pain claims, then this argument must be wrong (assuming it is true of some experiences that they are painful)." "While there may well be reason to think there are no moral facts, this argument does not provide it." On disagreement (§1): "moral realists need also to respond to the error theorist's contention that the arguments and disagreements all rest on some false supposition to the effect that there are actually facts of the sort there would have to be for some of the claims to be true."
- **On the word "queer" (Joyce §3.2).** "Mackie famously calls such properties “queer” (in what seems now an increasingly anachronistic use of the term)." Joyce also writes that such an error theorist "is forced to fall back on vague metaphors in presenting their case: Moral properties have a “to-be-pursuedness” to them (Mackie 1977: 40)".
- **As a sceptic to be answered (Sinnott-Armstrong §3.4).** "Since smart and knowledgable people do take moral nihilism seriously and even argue for it (Mackie 1977, Joyce 2001), moral nihilism cannot be dismissed as readily as Descartes’s deceiving demon."
- **On the logical problem of evil (Beebe §8).** "Even Mackie admits that Plantinga solved the problem of evil, if that problem is understood as one of inconsistency." Beebe on the free will defence: "As an attempt to rebut the logical problem of evil, it is strikingly successful." Against Plantinga, on heaven (Beebe §10): "If W3 is possible, then the complaint lodged by Flew and Mackie above that God could (and therefore should) have created a world full of creatures who always did what is right is not answered." Mackie's own 1982 verdict on Plantinga is quoted under *In dialogue with*.

## Vocabulary

- *Objective values*, *objectively prescriptive*: what Mackie's thesis denies (*Ethics* ch. 1 §§1, 7); his terms, not an implant definition ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Error theory* and *moral scepticism*: Mackie's names for his second-order view (*Ethics* ch. 1 §7, p. 35). Sinnott-Armstrong (§1) files it under scepticism about moral reality, "the claim that no moral facts or properties exist", and calls Mackie's version "moral falsehood, as developed by Mackie (1977)". No vocabulary page yet.
- *Queerness*: the strangeness of objective values, metaphysical and epistemological (§9, p. 38); Joyce's remark on the word is under *Reception*.
- *Paradox of omnipotence*: for Mackie "can an omnipotent being make things which he cannot control?" (*Miracle* ch. 9(c), p. 160); see the implant's [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md) entry for its own sense of the word.

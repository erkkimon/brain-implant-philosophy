# Peter Singer

A [thinkers](index.md) hub that reports, not endorses
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md)).
Biographical facts and Singer's later statements of his own views are quoted
from the "About" page of his website, [petersinger.info/about](https://petersinger.info/about)
(an author's self-description; excerpts in `raw/singer-website-about-faq-life-views.md`).
The 1972 paper is quoted from a reprint copy (`raw/singer-1972-famine-affluence-and-morality.md`),
"All Animals Are Equal" (1974) from its repository PDF (`raw/singer-1974-all-animals-are-equal.md`).
*Animal Liberation* and *Practical Ethics* were not read here; what they say is given only as
Singer's own website or the encyclopedia authors named below report it.

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and education:** "I was born in Melbourne, Australia, in 1946 and educated at the University of Melbourne and the University of Oxford." (website, About Me).
- **Family history** (his own report, 1991): "he and others in the audience were taken aback when I told them that I was the child of Austrian-Jewish refugees, and that three of my grandparents had died in Nazi concentration camps." (["On Being Silenced in Germany"](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1991/08/15/on-being-silenced-in-germany/), *New York Review of Books*, 15 August 1991; `raw/singer-1991-on-being-silenced-in-germany.md`).
- **Oxford and animals:** "In 1970, when I was a graduate student at Oxford, a chance lunch with Richard Keshen, a Canadian graduate student and a vegetarian, introduced me to concerns about factory farming." "My wife, Renata, and I stopped eating meat in 1971 because we recognized that there is no ethical justification for treating animals as if their interests didn’t count." (website).
- **Princeton:** "For 25 years, I was the Ira W. DeCamp Professor of Bioethics at the University Center for Human Values at Princeton University." "I retired from Princeton in 2024 and returned to live in Melbourne," (website, About Me).
- **Organisations:** "I am the founder, and currently Chair of the Board, of The Life You Can Save, an organization based on my book of the same name." (website).
- **Labels he reports others giving him:** "Journalists have bestowed on me the tag of “world’s most influential living philosopher.”" and "I am also known for my controversial critique of the sanctity of life ethics in bioethics." (website; the tag is attributed by Singer to journalists, the word "controversial" is his).

## Problems addressed

- **[Famine, affluence and morality](../problems/famine-affluence-and-morality.md)** (Singer 1972). Opening: "As I write this, in November 1971, people are dying in East Bengal from lack of food, shelter, and medical care." The principle: "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it." (p. 231 per Hurka and Timmerman). The case: "if I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning in it, I ought to wade in and pull the child out. This will mean getting my clothes muddy, but this is insignificant, while the death of the child would presumably be a very bad thing."
  On distance: "It makes no moral difference whether the person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know, ten thousand miles away." On duty and charity: "The traditional distinction between duty and charity cannot be drawn, or at least, not in the place we normally draw it." Of the two versions: "I should also say that the strong version seems to me to be the correct one." The problem page maps the replies.
- **[The demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md).** Singer's reply in 1972: "I cannot see, though, why it should be regarded as a criticism of the position for which I have argued, rather than a criticism of our ordinary standards of behavior." Hurka ([SEP "Moral Demands and Permissions/Prerogatives"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-demands-permissions/), Fall 2024, §1) cites him for "But consequentialism says you must keep giving as long as the benefit to them is greater than the cost to you, which can be until you’ve reduced yourself to their welfare level (Singer 1972: 241)." Sinnott-Armstrong ([SEP "Consequentialism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/consequentialism/), §6) lists "P. Singer 1993" among utilitarians who say "we really are morally required to change our lives so as to do a lot more to increase overall utility".
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md)** ("All Animals Are Equal", *Philosophic Exchange* 5(1), 1974, pp. 103–116; *Animal Liberation*, 1975). "If a being suffers, there can be no moral justification for refusing to take that suffering into consideration." (1974, p. 107). Sentience, for him, is "the only defensible boundary of concern for the interests of others" (p. 108, full sentence in the raw file; see [sentience](../vocabulary/sentience.md)).
  His later summary: "I argued in the opening chapter of Animal Liberation that humans and animals are equal in the sense that the fact that a being is human does not mean that we should give the interests of that being preference over the similar interests of other beings." "That would be speciesism, and wrong for the same reasons that racism and sexism are wrong." (website, FAQ).
  On the word: "In the 1970s, Richard Ryder coined this term while campaigning in Oxford to denote a ubiquitous type of human centered prejudice, which he thought was similar to racism." "Peter Singer popularized the term and focused on the way speciesism, without moral justification, favors the interests of humans:" (Gruen & Monsó, [SEP "The Moral Status of Animals"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-animal/), Fall 2024, §1.1; `raw/sep-moral-animal-fall-2024-speciesism-preference-hedonism.md`).
  On killing animals, Wilson ([IEP "Animals and Ethics"](https://iep.utm.edu/anim-eth/), §3a): "Singer does not unequivocally claim that we must not eat animals if we are to correctly apply the Principle of Equal Consideration of Interests." Jaworska & Tannenbaum ([SEP "The Grounds of Moral Status"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/grounds-moral-status/), §2.4): "Both McMahan (2002) and Singer (1993) hold, on quite different grounds, that death is not very bad for most animals, while it is very bad for ordinary adult human beings." (`raw/sep-grounds-moral-status-fall-2024-singer.md`).
- **Personhood, newborn infants and disability** (*Practical Ethics*, 1979; 2nd ed. 1993). Singer (website, FAQ) uses "person" "to refer to a being who is capable of anticipating the future, of having wants and desires for the future." "So killing a newborn baby is never equivalent to killing a person, that is, a being who wants to go on living." Where parents and doctors decide that a baby should die: "I believe it should be possible to carry out that decision, not only by withholding or withdrawing life-support — which can lead to the baby dying slowly from dehydration or from an infection — but also by taking active steps to end the baby’s life swiftly and humanely." For a baby other couples would adopt: "Hence even if the parents do not want their own child, it would be wrong to kill it."
  The 1974 paper already compared animals with "humans with severe and irreparable brain damage, and also of infant humans" (p. 113; the argument the [animals page](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md) calls "from species overlap"). Jaworska & Tannenbaum (§5.3): "However, because he thinks that self-conscious beings have interests that merely conscious beings lack, he allows that the former beings might have rights that the latter lack." Reactions are under Reception.
- **[Abortion](../problems/abortion.md).** Gordon ([IEP "Abortion"](https://iep.utm.edu/abortion/), §1a) classes him: "The extreme liberal view is held by proponents (abortionists). They claim that human personhood begins immediately after birth or a bit later (Singer)." (Gordon's labels.)
- **[Euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md).** "I support law reform to allow people to decide to end their lives, if they are terminally or incurably ill." (website, FAQ). Young ([SEP "Voluntary Euthanasia"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/euthanasia-voluntary/), §4.5) cites "Kuhse and Singer 1985; Singer 1994" for advocates who support some non-voluntary euthanasia, "for certain severely disabled infants for whom the outlook is hopeless" (`raw/sep-euthanasia-voluntary-fall-2024-young.md`).
- **[Dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md).** Coady ([SEP "Dirty Hands"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/dirty-hands/), note 8): "The idea of a utilitarian version of the threshold story was suggested by Peter Singer." (`raw/sep-dirty-hands-fall-2024-coady-map.md`).
- **From preference to hedonistic utilitarianism.** Gruen & Monsó (§2.1): "Within animal ethics, Peter Singer’s Animal Liberation provided a strong defense of preference-satisfaction accounts of well-being, although Singer himself now endorses hedonism (Lazari-Radec & Singer 2016)." Singer (website, FAQ): "I am currently reconsidering some of the answers given above, especially those in which I regard the wrongness of killing as significantly affected by the capacity of those killed to see themselves as existing over time. This is a view that derived from my earlier acceptance of preference utilitarianism, but it does not fit well with hedonistic utilitarianism, which I am now more inclined to favour." Hooker ([SEP "Rule Consequentialism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/consequentialism-rule/), §2) lists their book among hedonisms that have grown: "It has grown in sophistication (Parfit 1984: Appendix I; Sumner 1996; Crisp 2006; de Lazari-Radek and Singer 2014: ch. 9)" (`raw/sep-consequentialism-rule-fall-2024-de-lazari-radek-singer.md`).
- **Moral intuitions and method** (see [reflective equilibrium](../methods/reflective-equilibrium.md)). Wasserman, Asch, Blustein & Putnam ([SEP "Cognitive Disability and Moral Status"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/cognitive-disability/), preamble): "Still others deny such convictions any presumptive weight or authority (Singer 2005)." Singer & de Lazari-Radek (2016, as Schultz quotes them): "As we have already indicated both in our book and in this response, we are skeptical about the validity of most of our moral intuitions." Singer 2005 is "Ethics and Intuitions", *Journal of Ethics* 9(3–4): 331–352, DOI [10.1007/s10892-005-3508-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10892-005-3508-y) (metadata verified, `raw/singer-bibliographic-records.md`; not read).

## In dialogue with

- **[Jeremy Bentham](bentham.md)** (earlier). Crimmins ([SEP "Jeremy Bentham"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/bentham/), §3) on Bentham's "Can they suffer?" note: "a proposition that lies at the beginnings of utilitarian arguments for the ethical treatment of animals (see Singer 1975)." The 1974 paper quotes the same note (pp. 106–107).
- **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (earlier). Singer 1972 names him on moral codes: "It has been argued by some writers, among them Sidgwick and Urmson, that we need to have a basic moral code which is not too far beyond the capacities of the ordinary man, for otherwise there will be a general breakdown of compliance with the moral code." and answers "This would seem to be an empirical question, although a very difficult one." Schultz ([SEP "Henry Sidgwick"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/sidgwick/), §2.2) on esoteric morality: "Yet even on the count of esoteric morality, Sidgwick’s views have attracted some forceful recent defenders (de Lazari-Radek & Singer 2010, 2014, 2016) who strike at the very heart of the Kantian and neo-Kantian publicity requirement." (Schultz's wording.)
- **[Thomas Aquinas](aquinas.md)** (earlier). Singer 1972 quotes *ST* II-II q. 66 a. 7: "whatever a man has in superabundance is owed, of natural right, to the poor for their sustenance."
- **[R. M. Hare](hare.md)** (contemporary). Schultz (§3): "Reactions to any such interpretation, which supposedly accorded a too generous role to “received opinion” in Sidgwick’s methodology, came from Peter Singer (1974) and many others influenced by Hare’s critique of Rawls" Singer's 2002 *Utilitas* paper on Hare is listed under Works. In the 1991 essay Singer reports that Hare was among the speakers whose invitation to the 1991 Wittgenstein Symposium drew protests.
- **[John Rawls](rawls.md)** (contemporary). The same Schultz sentence places Singer's 1974 *Monist* paper among reactions against a reading of Sidgwick as a method of reflective equilibrium associated with Rawls (Schultz §3).
- **[Derek Parfit](parfit.md)** (contemporary). Schultz (§2.2) on de Lazari-Radek and Singer: "Their argument owes much to Parfit’s demonstration that an ethical theory could be self-effacing without being self-defeating, incoherent or untrue (Parfit 1984)." (Schultz's wording "demonstration".)
- **[Bernard Williams](williams.md)** (contemporary). Jollimore ([SEP "Impartiality"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/impartiality/), §2.3) sets Williams against "the extreme position defended by Godwin, Singer, etc.": "(Williams (1981) holds that even to consider sacrificing one’s wife for the sake of impersonal justice constitutes a kind of moral error in its own right.)"
- **John Arthur, Travis Timmerman, Liam Murphy** (later critics of the 1972 argument) — on the [problem page](../problems/famine-affluence-and-morality.md), with Singer's Postscript reply to Arthur.
- **Tom Regan** (rights view, contemporary). Gruen & Monsó (§2.2): "The utilitarian position on animals, most commonly associated with Peter Singer and popularly, though erroneously, referred to as an animal rights position, is actually quite distinct." Regan's alternative is on the [animals page](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).
- **Harriet McBryde Johnson** (lawyer and disability rights activist, per her 2003 author note) — under Reception.

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **On the 1972 argument.** Beauchamp ([SEP "The Principle of Beneficence in Applied Ethics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/principle-beneficence/), §4): "Peter Singer’s theory has been the most widely discussed account." and, reporting critics, "Singer’s proposals have struck many as overly demanding, impracticable, and a significant departure from the demands of ordinary morality." Timmerman (2015, p. 204, DOI [10.1093/analys/anv015](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/anv015)), without a survey: "Yet, the majority of philosophers (including ethicists) reject Singer’s conclusion that we are morally required to donate to aid agencies whenever we can do so without sacrificing anything nearly as important as the good that our donations could bring about." Hurka (§2): "As Singer recognizes, a defence starting from his pond case can’t support a duty as strong as the fully maximizing one he himself prefers." On the defending side, Sinnott-Armstrong (§6), his own assessment: "Opponents of utilitarianism find this claim implausible, but it is not obvious that their counter-utilitarian intuitions are reliable or well-grounded (Murphy 2000, chs. 1–4; cf. Mulgan 2001, Singer 2005, Greene 2013)."
- **Effective altruism.** Jollimore (§2.3): "Singer’s work has given rise to the movement popularly known as ‘effective altruism,’ composed of people who attempt to dedicate their lives to doing as much practical good as possible. One popular strategy is to donate as much as possible to those charitable organizations judged to be most effective." Against: "Many have resisted the extreme position defended by Godwin, Singer, etc. If nothing else, such positions are quite clearly incompatible with the apparent existence of morally admirable partiality." (Jollimore §2.3.)
- **On animals.** Singer's own report: "Eventually I wrote Animal Liberation, which has been described as the “bible” of the animal rights movement. It was published in 1975 and has never been out of print." (website; the description is attributed by Singer to others, unnamed). Regan's criticism of utilitarianism, as Wilson (IEP §3b) reports it, is on the [animals page](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).
- **On disability comparisons.** Wasserman, Asch, Blustein & Putnam (SEP, preamble): "Philosophers who question the moral status of human beings with the most significant cognitive disabilities often compare them to animals claimed to have similar or greater cognitive abilities (McMahan 1996, 2002, 2009; Singer 1993, 2009; and Wilkinson 2008 in Other Internet Resources)." "Some critics find these comparisons unnecessary and offensive (e.g., Carlson 2009; Carlson and Kittay 2009)." (`raw/sep-cognitive-disability-fall-2024-singer-comparisons.md`).
- **Harriet McBryde Johnson (2003).** In ["Unspeakable Conversations"](https://web.archive.org/web/20090328063303id_/http://www.nytimes.com/2003/02/16/magazine/unspeakable-conversations.html) (*New York Times Magazine*, 16 February 2003; `raw/johnson-2003-unspeakable-conversations.md`), Johnson, described in the author note as "a disability rights activist and advocate for more than 25 years", states his view: "He insists he doesn't want to kill me. He simply thinks it would have been better, all things considered, to have given my parents the option of killing the baby I once was, and to let other parents kill similar babies as they come along and thereby avoid the suffering that comes with lives like mine and satisfy the reasonable preferences of parents for a different kind of child." She corrects herself: "He is the man who wants me dead. No, that's not at all fair. He wants to legalize the killing of certain babies who might come to be like me if allowed to live." Of his conduct as host: "A: He behaved in every way appropriately, treated me as a respected professional acquaintance and was a gracious and accommodating host."
- **Protests in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, 1989–1991, as Singer reports them** (NYRB 1991; the protesters' statements are known here only through his account). "Kliemt’s course, however, was subjected to organized and repeated disruption by protesters objecting to the use of the book on the grounds that in one of its ten chapters it advocates active euthanasia for severely disabled newborn infants." Of the June 1989 Marburg symposium: "Just a day or two before I was due to leave for Germany, my invitation to speak at the Marburg conference was abruptly withdrawn." and "Shortly before the symposium was due to open, Lebenshilfe and the Bishop Bekkers Institute canceled the entire event." Of the Wittgenstein Symposium: "There will be no Wittgenstein Symposium in 1991."
  In Zurich, May 1991, as he reports it: "Before it began, a group of disabled people in wheelchairs, who had been admitted to the flat area at the front of the lecture theater, staged a brief protest in which they said that, while it was all the same to them whether or not I lectured on the topic of animal rights, they objected to the fact that the University of Zurich had invited such a notorious advocate of euthanasia to discuss ethical issues that also concerned the disabled." and "At that point one of the protesters came up behind me and tore my glasses from my face, throwing them on the floor and breaking them."
- **Singer's replies.** On what he meant to defend: "My intention in these lectures was to defend a view for which I have argued in several previously published works: that the parents of severely disabled newborn infants should be able to decide, together with their physician, whether their infant should live or die." On the Nazi comparison: "In contrast to the Nazi ideology that the state should decide who was worthy of life, my view was designed to reduce the power of the state and allow parents to make crucial life and death decisions, both for themselves and, in consultation with their doctors, for their newborn infants." On disabled people's lives: "while I hold that some lives are so severely blighted from the beginning that they are better not continued, I also believe that once a life has been allowed to develop, then in every case everything should be done to make that life as satisfying and rich as possible." (all NYRB 1991). On the 1979 sentence often quoted against him: "I did write that, in the 1979 edition of Practical Ethics. Today the term “defective infant” is considered offensive, and I no longer use it, but it was standard usage then." (website, FAQ). And: "My views are not fixed. Many good philosophers disagree with me, and all philosophers should be open to changing their views on the basis of new arguments and objections." (website, FAQ).
- **On Sidgwick scholarship.** Schultz (preamble) lists "de Lazari-Radek & Singer 2014, 2021" among those who have endorsed Broad's assessment of the *Methods* (see the [Sidgwick page](sidgwick.md), Reception).

## Vocabulary

- *Person*: Singer (website, FAQ) uses the word for "a being who is capable of anticipating the future, of having wants and desires for the future"; compare the implant's [moral patient](../vocabulary/moral-patient.md).
- *Sentience*: in 1974 "using the term as a convenient, if not strictly accurate, shorthand for the capacity to suffer or experience enjoyment or happiness" (p. 108); see [sentience](../vocabulary/sentience.md).
- *Speciesism*: Ryder's coinage, popularised by Singer (Gruen & Monsó §1.1); no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Defective infant*: his 1979 term, which he reports he "no longer" uses (website, FAQ). His 1974 and 1991 wording ("permanently retarded humans", "mental retardation") is quoted as he wrote it in the raw files and not used in the implant's own voice.

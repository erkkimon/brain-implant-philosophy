## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth, family, education:** "Robert Nozick was born in Brooklyn in 1938 to a Russian Jewish immigrant family. He earned an undergraduate Philosophy degree from Columbia University in 1959 and a Ph.D. in Philosophy from Princeton University in 1963." (Mack §1).
- **Posts:** "He taught for a couple of years at Princeton, Harvard, and Rockefeller Universities before moving permanently to Harvard in 1969." (Mack §1). Feser (§1): "Robert Nozick was born in Brooklyn, New York in 1938, and he taught at Harvard University until his death in January 2002."
- **Death:** "Robert Nozick died in 2002 from stomach cancer for which he was first treated in 1994." (Mack §1).
- **Early politics and conversion:** "As an undergraduate student at Columbia and at least in his early days as a graduate student at Princeton, Nozick endorsed socialism." (Mack §1). "The major force in his conversion to libertarian views was his conversations at Princeton with his fellow philosophy graduate student, Bruce Goldberg. It was through Goldberg that Nozick met the economist Murray Rothbard who was the major champion of “individualist anarchism” in the later decades of the twentieth century" (Mack §1). Nozick's own report (ASU, p. ix): "With reluctance, I found myself becoming convinced of (as they are now often called) libertarian views, due to various considerations and arguments."
- **Award:** ASU "won the National Book Award for Philosophy and Religion in 1975." (Mack, preamble).
- **Newcomb's problem, provenance:** Nozick: "I first heard the problem, in 1963, from his friend Professor Martin David Kruskal of the Princeton University Department of Astrophysical Sciences." (1969, p. 143, note *; `raw/nozick-1969-newcombs-problem-two-principles-of-choice.md`).

## Problems addressed

- **Rights and the state (ASU, Preface and Part I).** ASU opens: "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)." (p. ix). Its conclusions: "Our main conclusions about the state are that a minimal state, limited to the narrow functions of protection against force, theft, fraud, enforcement of contracts, and so on, is justified; that any more extensive state will violate persons’ rights not to be forced to do certain things, and is unjustified; and that the minimal state is inspiring as well as right." (p. ix).
  Mack's summary (preamble): "Pressing further the anti-consequentialist aspects of John Rawls’ A Theory of Justice, Nozick argued that respect for individual rights is the key standard for assessing state action and, hence, that the only legitimate state is a minimal state that restricts its activities to the protection of the rights of life, liberty, property, and contract."
- **Side constraints and the separateness of persons (ASU ch. 3).** "Side constraints express the inviolability of other persons." (p. 32). "But there is no social entity with a good that undergoes some sacrifice for its own good. There are only individual people, different individual people, with their own individual lives." (pp. 32–33). Whether constraints are absolute he leaves open: "The question of whether these side constraints are absolute, or whether they may be violated in order to avoid catastrophic moral horror, and if the latter, what the resulting structure might look like, is one I hope largely to avoid." (p. 30 n.). Mack (§2.3): "For Nozick, rights express the moral inviolability of individuals;"; Mack illustrates the constraint with A, who may not kill B even to prevent W from killing X, Y and Z.
- **The minimal state against the anarchist (ASU Part I).** van der Vossen & Christmas: "Robert Nozick offered a controversial and subtle argument that a state, with strictly curtailed domain of activity, could exist without anyone’s rights being violated." Feser (IEP §2b) reconstructs it in steps: protective associations, a dominant agency, an ultra-minimal state, and a minimal state that compensates independents.
- **Distributive justice: the entitlement theory (ASU ch. 7).** "The complete principle of distributive justice would say simply that a distribution is just if everyone is entitled to the holdings they possess under the distribution." (p. 151), built from justice in acquisition, in transfer, and rectification. Mack (§4.1) on its relation to Rawls: "Whereas Rawls concludes that a due respect for the separateness of persons precludes imposing losses on individuals for the sake of the general welfare, Nozick concludes that it precludes imposing losses upon individuals for the sake of any conception of the overall social good—including deeply distribution-sensitive conceptions." Lamont and Favor (SEP "Distributive Justice", §7): "Robert Nozick advanced this version of libertarianism (Nozick 1974), and is its best known contemporary advocate." (`raw/sep-justice-distributive-fall-2024-rawls-difference-principle-critics.md`; their assessment).
- **The Wilt Chamberlain argument (ASU pp. 160–164, "How Liberty Upsets Patterns").** From "your favorite" distribution D1, one million spectators each give Chamberlain twenty-five cents, and he "winds up with $250,000"; Nozick asks: "Is he entitled to this income? Is this new distribution D2, unjust? If so, why?" (p. 161). His conclusion: "The socialist society would have to forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults." (p. 163). Mack (§4.2) calls it "The first, most famous, and most misunderstood of these arguments" (his assessment).
- **Taxation (ASU ch. 7).** "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor." (p. 169); Nozick adds that some find the claim "obviously true" and "Others find the claim absurd." (p. 169). Reception: Reception below.
- **[The liberal paradox](../problems/liberal-paradox.md) (ASU pp. 164–166).** On Sen's theorem: "Rights do not determine a social ordering but instead set the constraints within which a social choice is to be made, by excluding certain alternatives, fixing others, and so on." (p. 166; `raw/nozick-1974-anarchy-state-utopia-sens-argument.md`).
- **[The experience machine](../problems/experience-machine.md) (ASU pp. 42–45).** "Suppose there were an experience machine that would give you any experience you desired." (p. 42). His three reasons begin: "First, we want to do certain things, and not just have the experience of doing them." (p. 43). Conclusion: "We learn that something matters to us in addition to experience by imagining an experience machine and then realizing that we would not use it." (p. 44; `raw/nozick-1974-anarchy-state-utopia-experience-machine.md`). Buscicchi (IEP) reports a second, lifelong version in *The Examined Life*: "In the 1974’s EMTE the plugging in is for two years, while in the 1989’s EMTE the plugging in is for life." (`raw/iep-experience-machine-buscicchi.md`). The problem page sets out the replies and the experimental work.
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).** The machine passage opens: "But isn't utilitarianism at least adequate for animals? I think not." (p. 42). Wilson (IEP "Animals and Ethics", §1f(ii)) reports Nozick's "moral spillover" question (ASU p. 36; `raw/iep-animals-and-ethics-wilson-indirect-theories-marginal-cases.md`).
- **Utopia (ASU Part III).** "The framework for utopia that we have described is equivalent to the minimal state." (p. 333). The book ends: "How dare any state or group of individuals do more. Or less." (p. 334).
- **[Newcomb's problem](../problems/newcombs-problem.md) (1969).** "There are two plausible looking and highly intuitive arguments which require different decisions. The problem is to explain why one of them is not legitimately applied to this choice situation." (p. 115). His verdict: "I believe that one should take what is in both boxes." (p. 135). Weirich (SEP "Causal Decision Theory", §2.1): "Robert Nozick (1969) presented a dilemma for decision theory." (`raw/sep-decision-causal-fall-2024-newcombs-problem.md`). Kuhn (SEP "Prisoner’s Dilemma", §7) links it to the [prisoner's dilemma](../problems/prisoners-dilemma.md): "a puzzle popularized among philosophers in Nozick" (`raw/sep-prisoner-dilemma-fall-2024-newcomb-and-replicas.md`).
- **Knowledge as tracking (*Philosophical Explanations*, 1981).** Feser (§3): "the conditions that make S’s true belief that p count as knowledge are the counterfactuals: (a) that were p not true S would not believe it (the “variation” condition), and (b) that were p still true in somewhat different circumstances, S would still believe it and would not believe that not-p (the “adherence” condition)." Luper (§2.1) gives the core as "(BT) were p false, S would not believe p." and a later method-relative form, "(BMT) were p false, S would not believe p via M." On skepticism (Luper §5.2): "According to Dretske and Nozick, skepticism is appealing because skeptics are partially right." Feser (§3): "Nozick famously, and controversially, denies what is known among epistemologists as the “closure principle,”".
- **[Fitch's paradox of knowability](../problems/fitchs-paradox-of-knowability.md).** Brogaard and Salerno (SEP, §3.1): "Though some have argued that knowing a conjunction does not entail knowing the conjuncts (Nozick 1981), Williamson (1993) and Jago (2010) have shown that versions of the paradox do not require this distributive assumption." (`raw/sep-fitch-paradox-fall-2024-proof-history-and-responses.md`). Luper (§1) likewise reports that Nozick denies closure "across instantiation and simplification".
- **Personal identity and the [ship of Theseus](../problems/ship-of-theseus.md) (1981).** Feser (§4): "it is the later person who “most closely continues” the earlier one who is the one who is truly identical to the latter." With two equal continuers, "neither B nor C is identical to A". Deutsch & Garbacz (SEP "Relative Identity", §2.5): "There are, however, more sophisticated “best candidate” theories that are not vulnerable to this objection (Nozick 1982)." (`raw/sep-identity-relative-fall-2024-ship-of-theseus-paradox.md`; they date *Philosophical Explanations* 1982).

## In dialogue with

- **[John Rawls](rawls.md)** (Harvard colleague; *A Theory of Justice*, 1971). Nozick's side: patterned and end-state principles, including the difference principle (Mack §4.2: "Rawls’ difference principle will select D1 on the distinct ground that within it the lowest payoff is greater than the lowest payoff within any other available distribution."). Freeman (SEP "Original Position", §6) reports both sides: "(Nozick agrees and says the OP is incapable of yielding historical entitlement principles, but only patterned end-state principles instead. Nozick 1974, 198–204. Rawls replies that the difference principle does not conform to any observable pattern but grounds fair distributions in a fair social process that must actually be carried out. PL, 282–83)" (`raw/sep-original-position-fall-2024-veil-maximin-critics.md`). Wenar (SEP "John Rawls", §3.4): "By Rawls’s criteria, a libertarian conception of justice (such as Nozick’s in Anarchy, State, and Utopia) is not a liberal political conception of justice." (`raw/sep-rawls-fall-2024-life-justice-as-fairness-political-liberalism.md`).
- **[Jeremy Bentham](bentham.md)** and utilitarianism (earlier). Crimmins (SEP "Jeremy Bentham", §4.3) lists ASU among critics who "argue that calculations of total utility fail to respect the distinctiveness of persons and thereby place their interests at perpetual risk (Rawls 1971, 22–27; Nozick 1974, 28–35;" (`raw/sep-bentham-fall-2024-life-calculus-panopticon-reception.md`).
- **[Immanuel Kant](kant.md)** (earlier). Mack (§2.2) reports that Rawls reads utilitarianism as failing to treat individuals as ends-in-themselves and that "Nozick follows suit in appropriating this Kantian language (see Cohen 1995: 238–243)."
- **Murray Rothbard** (individualist anarchist; Mack §1, above). Part I answers the anarchist. Anarchist reply (van der Vossen & Christmas): "Anarchists argue that a de facto monopoly would not be just in using force to prohibit anyone else from forming a new protective association." (Chartier 2011).
- **Amartya Sen** (contemporary): "Our conclusions are reinforced by considering a recent general argument of Amartya K. Sen." (ASU p. 164); see [the liberal paradox](../problems/liberal-paradox.md).
- **William Newcomb and Martin Kruskal** (sources of the problem; Facts above). Nozick: "It is a beautiful problem. I wish it were mine." (1969, p. 143, note *).
- **G. A. Cohen** (later critic). van der Vossen & Christmas (§1): "G. A. Cohen was one of the most important of Nozick’s critics, but he nonetheless sympathized with what he took to be the core commitment of justice in Nozick’s theory – self-ownership." and "Cohen, however, took it that self-ownership explained the unjustly exploitative nature of capitalism." Cohen, *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995), DOI [10.1017/CBO9780511521270](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511521270) (verified by content negotiation; not read).
- **Fred Dretske** (contemporary epistemologist): "By contrast, Fred Dretske and Robert Nozick reject K and therefore GK as well." (Luper §1). **Saul Kripke** (later critic): Luper reports Kripke's red-barn objection ("Nozick on Knowledge", 2011).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing.** Feser (§2): "Anarchy, State, and Utopia is, together with Rawls’s A Theory of Justice, generally regarded as one of the two great classics of twentieth-century analytic political philosophy." van der Vossen & Christmas (§1): "The publication of Robert Nozick’s book, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) was the first time mainstream academic philosophy was forced to pay attention to libertarian ideas." Feser (§5): "In any event, it is almost certain that it is Nozick’s defense of libertarianism that will stand as his most significant contribution to philosophy."
- **Foundations of rights.** Mack (§2.2): "It is a commonplace to say that in ASU Nozick provides no foundation for his affirmation of such Lockean natural rights (Nagel 1975)." Mack's own assessment: "Nozick also fails to address the hard question of why—except perhaps to avoid “catastrophic moral horror” (29 footnote*)—rights always trump considerations of personal or social well-being." Nagel, "Libertarianism without Foundations" (1975; reprinted in Paul 1981), is cited by Mack and not read here.
- **Self-ownership: Cohen and the reply.** Cohen's reading is above. van der Vossen & Christmas reply that Cohen ascribed "full" self-ownership to Nozick and that "so-called full self-ownership does not appear to do that, and hence few if any libertarians support it." They also note: "Despite Nozick only mentioning self-ownership once, and developing a large number of arguments against redistributive conceptions of economic justice which do not explicitly invoke or rely on the idea of self-ownership throughout part II of Anarchy, State, and Utopia, self-ownership became synonymous with libertarianism." Mack (§4.1) on Cohen's rolling-pin criticism: "Although this sort of criticism rests on the mistaken idea that, for Nozick, just processes are simply not unjust processes, it does highlight how little Nozick does offer in the way of a positive account" (Mack's assessment).
- **Taxation as forced labour.** Murray (§2): "In fact, Nozick is both supported (most notably by Edward Feser (2000)) and criticized (most notably by Brian Barry (1975, 332)) for comparing taxation with forced labor."
- **The Lockean proviso.** Murray (§3h): "Nozick re-interprets the proviso to mean that if the initial acquisition fails to make anyone worse off who was using the resource before, then it is justly acquired." van der Vossen & Christmas: "Nozick interprets the proviso to require that no individual be made worse off by a particular appropriation of a natural resource. But this interpretation is problematic for at least two reasons." (their assessment).
- **The invisible-hand argument.** For: "Nozick’s argument is a potentially strong one as it uses anarchist premises to argue that a state could in principle emerge through just steps." Against: "Non-libertarian liberals argue that even if the argument is internally coherent in terms of showing that a de jure monopoly could emerge justly, it does not yield a state that wields public power, but only a form of overwhelming private power, such as the states of feudal Europe (Freeman 2001, Anderson 2017)." (both van der Vossen & Christmas).
- **The experience machine.** Crisp (2006, p. 620): "Finally, while hedonism was down, Robert Nozick dealt it a near-fatal blow with his famous example of the experience machine." (`raw/crisp-2006-hedonism-reconsidered-experience-machine.md`). Against its evidential use, Weijers (2014, p. 513): "Recently, however, it has been convincingly argued that Nozick’s scenario should not be used in this way because it elicits judgments marred by status quo bias and other irrelevant factors." (`raw/weijers-2014-experience-machine-is-dead.md`). Details on the problem page.
- **Tracking.** Luper (§2.1) sets aside Nozick's "thoroughly discredited fourth condition for knowledge" (his wording, citing Luper 1984, 2009 and Kripke 2011); on skepticism (§5.2): "At best, Dretske and Nozick have provided a way of dealing with the latter." (the latter = Cartesian skepticism).
- **Later revisions, as reported.** Mack (§1): "Nozick never attempted to further develop the views that he expressed in ASU, and he never responded to the extensive critical reaction to those views." "Nozick did seem to repudiate at least some aspects of the ASU doctrine in The Examined Life and The Nature of Rationality (Nozick 1989: 286–296)." Murray (§4), quoting *The Examined Life*: "He notes that libertarianism is seriously inadequate, partially because the theory does not “fully knit the humane considerations and joint cooperative activities it left room for more closely into its fabric”". Against reading this as abandonment, Nozick in 2001 (as Mack quotes him): "the rumors of my deviation (or apostasy!) from libertarianism were much exaggerated." Murray: "In an interview late in his life Nozick explained that his turn away from libertarianism was largely exaggerated, adding that he still endorsed the theory but was no longer a “hard core” follower." In *Invariances* (2001: 282, as Mack quotes): "All that any society should (coercively) demand is adherence to the ethics of respect".

## Vocabulary

- *Side constraint*: a limit on action, as against a goal to be maximised; "Side constraints express the inviolability of other persons." (ASU p. 32). No vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Entitlement*, *historical* and *patterned* principles: Nozick's terms for principles of justice in holdings (ASU ch. 7); "patterned" covers both end-state and track-the-feature principles at that point of the argument (Mack §4.2).
- *Minimal state* / *ultra-minimal state*: the protective state of Part I; *framework for utopia*: Part III's name for the same institution (ASU p. 333).
- *Tracking*: a belief "tracks the truth" when it meets the variation and adherence conditions (Feser §3); compare the implant's [knowledge](../vocabulary/knowledge.md) entry.
- *Dilemma* in Weirich's "a dilemma for decision theory": a choice between two principles, not necessarily the implant's normative [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md).

## Facts a claim depends on

Structural note: every fact below is reported from a source that, by its
own account, depends on second-hand testimony. Nails & Monoson: "All our information about him is second-hand and most of it vigorously disputed, but his trial and death at the hands of the Athenian democracy is nevertheless the founding myth of the academic discipline of philosophy" (SEP preamble).

- **Dates.** "The philosopher Socrates remains, as he was in his lifetime (469–399 B.C.E.), an enigma, an inscrutable individual who, despite having written nothing, is considered one of the handful of philosophers who forever changed how philosophy itself was to be conceived." (Nails & Monoson, preamble — the assessment is theirs). Born: "When Socrates was born in 469, a Persian invasion had been decisively repulsed at Plataea" (§3).
- **Wrote nothing** — same preamble sentence. Every doctrine below is therefore a report by someone else (see *Problems addressed*).
- **Family and deme.** "Socrates’s tribe was Antiochis, and his deme was Alopece (south-southeast of the city wall). Assuming that his stoneworker father, Sophroniscus, kept to the conventions" (§3). "Socrates’s mother, Phaenarete, later remarried and had a second son, Patrocles (Plato, Euthydemus 297e); she became a midwife sometime thereafter (Plato, Theaetetus149a)." (§3). "It was at about this time that Socrates married Xanthippe." (§3, after the 422 battle of Amphipolis).
- **Military service.** Nails & Monoson place him at Delium: "the battle of Delium (423) at which Socrates fought as a hoplite" (§2.1).
- **Charge, 399.** "a young poet named Meletus had composed a document charging Socrates with the capital crime of irreverence (asebeia): failure to show due piety toward the gods of Athens." (§3). "Socrates’s irreverence, Meletus claimed, had resulted in the corruption of the city’s young men (Euthyphro 3c–d)." (§3).
- **Death, 399.** "The poisoner described the physical effects of the Conium maculatum variety of hemlock used for citizen executions (Bloch 2001), then Socrates cheerfully took the cup and drank." (§3, narrating Plato's *Phaedo*).
- **Students.** [Plato](plato.md): "From that time onward, having reached his twentieth year (so it is said), he was the pupil of Socrates." (Diogenes Laertius 3.6, Hicks; `raw/diogenes-laertius-3-hicks-life-of-plato.md`). [Aristippus](aristippus.md): "Not only is it well attested that Aristippus of Cyrene (born c. 435 BCE) was a close associate of Socrates" (Tsouna, SEP "Cyrenaics", preamble; `raw/sep-cyrenaics-fall-2026-aristippus-presentism-epicurus.md`).

### The Socratic problem: which sources

Nails & Monoson: "So thorny is the difficulty of distinguishing the historical Socrates from the Socrateses of the authors of the texts in which he appears and, moreover, from the Socrateses of scores of later interpreters, that the whole contested issue is generally referred to as the Socratic problem." (preamble). "One thing is certain about the historical Socrates: even among those who knew him in life, there was profound disagreement about what his actual views and methods were." (§2). They treat three contemporaneous sources (§2.1); Aristotle, whom Kraut (SEP "Plato", preamble) describes as one "who studied with him" (Plato), is a further ancient witness.

- **Aristophanes** (±450–±386, per §2.1), *Clouds* (423). For: "Our earliest extant source—and the only one who can claim to have known Socrates in vigorous midlife—is the playwright Aristophanes." Against: "Comedy by its very nature is a tricky source for information about anyone." Dover (1968), as Nails & Monoson report him, holds that "Clouds amalgamates in one character, Socrates, features now well known to be unique to specific other fifth-century intellectuals (1968, xxxii-lvii)."
- **Xenophon** (±425–±386). For: "still it is sometimes argued that, having had no philosophical axes to grind, Xenophon may have presented a more accurate portrait of Socrates than Plato does." Against, in Nails & Monoson's own voice: "(1) The Socrates of Xenophon’s works is so pedestrian that it is difficult to imagine his inspiring fifteen or more people to write Socratic discourses in the period following his death." and "Xenophon’s memoirs are pastiches, several of which simply could not have occurred as presented." (§2.1).
- **Plato** (424/3–347). For: "Philosophers have usually privileged the account of Socrates given by their fellow philosopher, Plato. Plato was about twenty-five when Socrates was tried and executed, and had probably known the old man most of his life." Caveat: "(i) Plato may have shaped the character Socrates (or other characters) to serve his own purposes, whether philosophical or literary or both." (§2.1). Woodruff calls the *Apology* "probably the closest of Plato’s works to the historical Socrates" (SEP "Shorter Ethical Works" §1) — his assessment. Kraut (SEP "Plato" §11) argues the contrary for the definition dialogues: "But the twists and turns of the arguments in Euthyphro and other dialogues that search for definitions are more likely to be the products of Plato’s mind than the content of any conversations that really took place." (`raw/sep-plato-and-plato-ethics-fall-2024-life-chronology-forms-ethics.md`).
- **Aristotle** ascribes two things to him: "for two things may be fairly ascribed to Socrates-inductive arguments and universal definition, both of which are concerned with the starting-point of science):-but Socrates did not make the universals or the definitions exist apart" (*Metaphysics* XIII.4, Ross; `raw/aristotle-metaphysics-13-4-nicomachean-ethics-7-2-socrates-ross.md`). Vlastos used Aristotle as evidence for his chronology: "other ancient testimony (particularly that of Aristotle)" (Nails & Monoson §2.2).

## Problems addressed

Structural note: *Socrates says* below means *Plato's (or Aristotle's)
Socrates says*, for the reason in the section above.

- **[The Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md)** (*Euthyphro* 10a). His question, Fowler's translation: "Just consider this question:—Is that which is holy loved by the gods because it is holy, or is it holy because it is loved by the gods?" (`raw/plato-euthyphro-9e-11b-pious-loved-because-pious-jowett-fowler.md`). His conclusion: "Then that which is dear to the gods and that which is holy are not identical, but differ one from the other." (10e, Fowler). Hare (SEP "Religion and Morality" §1) reports his position: "Socrates makes it clear that his view is the second (though he does not argue for this conclusion in addressing this question, and he is probably relying on the earlier premise, at Euthyphro, 7c10f, that we love things because of the properties they have)." and adds: "But his view is not an objection to tying morality and religion together." (`raw/sep-religion-morality-fall-2024-euthyphro-ashari-voluntarists.md`). The dialogue ends without a definition: "For somehow or other our arguments, on whatever ground we rest them, seem to turn round and walk away from us." (11b, Jowett).
- **[Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md)** (*Meno* 80d–86c). Socrates restates Meno's challenge: "I know, Meno, what you mean; but just see what a tiresome dispute you are introducing." (80e, Jowett) and answers with recollection, "for all enquiry and all learning is but recollection." (81c–d, Jowett; `raw/plato-meno-80d-86c-98a-paradox-and-recollection-jowett-lamb.md`). His stated stake: "Some things I have said of which I am not altogether confident. But that we shall be better and braver and less helpless if we think that we ought to enquire" (86b–c, Jowett). Sorensen (SEP "Epistemic Paradoxes" §6.1) reads the paradox as aimed at Socrates: "Meno discerns a conflict between Socratic ignorance and Socratic inquiry (Meno 80d, in Cooper 1997)." (`raw/sep-epistemic-paradoxes-and-plato-entries-fall-2024-menos-paradox.md`). Woodruff (§8) notes the attribution question: "The Meno will explore a process of learning without teaching; this process is known as recollection." — the *Meno* is not on Vlastos's elenctic list (below).
- **[I know that I know nothing](../problems/i-know-that-i-know-nothing.md)** (*Apology* 21b–22d, 29b). What Plato's Socrates says: "I neither know nor think that I know. In this latter particular, then, I seem to have slightly the advantage of him." (21d, Jowett); he also claims knowledge: "but I do know that injustice and disobedience to a better, whether God or man, is evil and dishonourable" (29b, Jowett) (`raw/plato-apology-21b-22d-29b-socratic-ignorance-jowett-fowler.md`). The formula is Cicero's: "while he knows this one thing alone, that he knows nothing." (*Academica* I, Yonge; `raw/cicero-diogenes-laertius-socrates-knows-only-that-he-knows-nothing.md`). Readings: Vogt (SEP "Ancient Skepticism" §2.2): "Socrates knows that he does not know about important things." Fine (2021 abstract): "Socrates does not say, or imply, that he knows that he knows nothing in a way that involves self-contradiction." (`raw/fine-2008-vlastos-1985-does-socrates-claim-to-know-that-he-knows-nothing.md`). Vlastos 1985, "Socrates' Disavowal of Knowledge", is held bibliographically only.
- **Weakness of will (akrasia); "no one does wrong willingly"** (no problem page yet). *Protagoras* 358c, Lamb: "Then surely, I went on, no one willingly goes after evil or what he thinks to be evil;" and 358d: "it is not in human nature, apparently, to do so—to wish to go after what one thinks to be evil in preference to the good" (`raw/plato-apology-26a-38a-crito-49b-d-protagoras-358c-d-jowett-fowler-lamb.md`). Woodruff: "Socrates’ doctrine on this, apparently, is that knowledge of what is right cannot be overcome by pleasure or passion." and "Scholars dispute the question whether Socrates is committed to the conclusion he draws for Protagoras." (§9). In the trial speech the doctrine becomes a defence: "But if I corrupt them involuntarily, for such involuntary errors the law is not to hale people into court, but to take them and instruct and admonish them in private." (*Apology* 26a, Fowler). Woodruff on its scope: "A related doctrine is that no one errs voluntarily, a doctrine Plato holds consistently from the Apology to the Laws." and the counter-text: "In the Hippias Minor, by contrast, Socrates argues that it is better to do wrong voluntarily than in ignorance." (§9).
- **Intellectualism — is virtue knowledge?** (no problem page yet). Woodruff: "If Socrates takes virtue to be a kind of knowledge, then he holds a view known as intellectualism." (§8). He lists three camps, unranked: "On moral injury: Penner and Rowe 1994 and Reshotko 2013 take the intellectualist position; Brickhouse and Smith 2010 take a moderate position; Gerson 1997 Weiss 2006 and Woodruff 2023 argue for the non-intellectualist interpretation." (§8).
- **Returning wrong for wrong** (*Crito* 49b–d; no problem page yet). "Then we ought neither to requite wrong with wrong nor to do evil to anyone, no matter what he may have done to us." (49c, Fowler). Socrates himself notes how few hold it: "for I know that there are few who believe or ever will believe this." (49d, Fowler). Woodruff calls it "a principle that is the bedrock of Socratic ethics: that it is never right to do wrong, even in return for wrong (49ab)." (§1).
- **The examined life** (*Apology* 38a). "is the greatest good to man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you will believe me still less." (Fowler).
- **Obeying the law vs. philosophising** — a tension Nails & Monoson record between two dialogues: "despite Socrates’s commitment to Athenian law, expressed in the Crito, he vows in the Apology that he will disobey the lawful jury if it orders him to stop philosophizing." (§2.2).

## In dialogue with

- **[Plato](plato.md)** — student and main source. Kraut (SEP "Plato" §11): "The idea that it is important to search for definitions may have been Socratic in origin." Where Socrates ends and Plato begins is the Socratic problem above.
- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** — on akrasia, against him: "For Socrates was entirely opposed to the view in question, holding that there is no such thing as incontinence; no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best-people act so only by reason of ignorance. Now this view plainly contradicts the observed facts" (*NE* VII.2, Ross). At the end of his own analysis Aristotle concedes: "the position that Socrates sought to establish actually seems to result" (*NE* VII.3, Ross). Frede & Lee (SEP "Plato's Ethics" §2.1): "Socrates’ proof in the Protagoras that reason cannot be overcome by the passions has, from Aristotle on, been treated as a denial of akrasia" (`raw/sep-plato-and-plato-ethics-fall-2024-life-chronology-forms-ethics.md`).
- **[Aristippus](aristippus.md)** — associate (Tsouna, above) and founder of the Cyrenaic school.
- **[Epicurus](epicurus.md)** — the exception in Nehamas's survey, as Nails & Monoson quote him: "with the exception of the Epicureans, every philosophical school in antiquity, whatever its orientation, saw in him either its actual founder or the type of person to whom its adherents were to aspire." (§2).
- **[Augustine](augustine.md)** — on pagan virtue, Tornau (SEP "Augustine" §7.2): "From this point of view, Socrates is closer to Paul than to Nero, even though his virtue will not bring him happiness, i.e., eternal bliss." (`raw/sep-augustine-fall-2024-tornau-life-works-evil-will-war-legacy.md`).
- **[Confucius](confucius.md)** — set beside the *Euthyphro* by Wong (SEP "Chinese Ethics" §1): "The practical problem discussed by Confucius and Socrates is arguably a universal one: the conflict between loyalty owed to a family member and duty to uphold public justice within the larger community." (`raw/sep-ethics-chinese-and-mencius-fall-2024-confucius-ren-li-junzi-readings.md`).
- **Arcesilaus** (no page) — on Socratic ignorance, as Cicero reports: "therefore Arcesilas asserted that there was nothing which could be known, not even that very piece of knowledge which Socrates had left himself." (*Academica* I, Yonge). Vogt: "Socrates’ commitment to reason—examination as the way to find out—inspires the skepticism of the Hellenistic Academy (Cooper 2004b, Vogt 2013)." (§2.2).
- **Gregory Vlastos** (1907–1991; no page) and **his critics** — see *Reception*.

## Reception

Assessments, each with its owner; for and against are listed in parallel,
unranked.

- **Standing, per Nails & Monoson (SEP 2024).** He "is considered one of the handful of philosophers who forever changed how philosophy itself was to be conceived" and his trial and death are "the founding myth of the academic discipline of philosophy" (preamble). These are the encyclopedia authors' assessments.
- **Two twentieth-century strands.** "Hans-Georg Gadamer (1900–2002) was the doyen of the hermeneutic strand, and Gregory Vlastos (1907–1991) of the analytic." (Nails & Monoson §2.2).
- **Vlastos's model.** "Beginning in the 1950s, Vlastos (1991, 45–80) recommended a set of mutually supportive premises that together provide a plausible framework in the analytic tradition for Socratic philosophy as a pursuit distinct from Platonic philosophy." Its last premise: "“As Plato changes, the philosophical persona of his Socrates is made to change” (Vlastos 1991, 53)—a view sometimes referred to as the “mouthpiece theory.”" Its result: "a firm list (contested, of course, by others) of ten theses held by Socrates, all of which are incompatible with the corresponding ten theses held by Plato (1991, 47–49)." (§2.2). Note 6 credits others: "he shared credit with a number of other philosophers (e.g., W. David Ross, Richard Robinson, G. E. L. Owen) who argued that Plato’s views evolved. And it was Vlastos who took a special interest in Socrates." Woodruff follows the convention: "Following the lead of Vlastos (1971 and 1991), many scholars represent the philosophical content of these dialogues as teachings of the historical Socrates. This article follows that convention." (§1).
- **Against the model.** Nails & Monoson: "For many analytic philosophers, John Cooper (1997, xiv) sounded the end of the developmentalist era when he described the early- and middle-period dialogue distinctions as “an unsuitable basis for bringing anyone to the reading of these works." (§2.3). The model "would hold sway until the mid 1990s" (preamble). Kraut's "products of Plato’s mind" (above) is a further reported dissent on the definition dialogues.
- **On the elenchus — with Vlastos and against.** Woodruff states the rules: "The general rules of elenchus are these: Socrates’ partner (often called his interlocutor) must answer every question according to his own beliefs, and the partner (not the audience if there is one) judges the outcome." and the puzzle: "The philosophical puzzle of the elenchus is to see how it could lead to any firm result, if its premises have no authority beyond the partner’s agreement." The two answers, in his words: "Perhaps the method can do no more than convict a partner of confusion (as Hugh Benson has contended), but Vlastos has argued persuasively that elenchus in fact supports Socrates’ ethical doctrine: Socrates believes he has grounds for clinging to beliefs of his that have been well tested and not refuted." (§2) — "persuasively" is Woodruff's assessment. The literature he names: Vlastos 1983, "The Socratic Elenchus", *OSAP* 1: 27–58; Brickhouse and Smith 1984, "Vlastos on the Elenchus", *OSAP* 2: 185–95; Benson 1987, "The Problem of the Elenchus Reconsidered", *Ancient Philosophy* 7: 75–85 (bibliographic only).
- **On akrasia** — Aristotle against, then partly conceding (above); Woodruff reports a dialectical reading: "The point may be dialectically directed at Protagoras" (§9).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "1842. Born in New York City, first child of Henry James and Mary Walsh." (Goodman §1).
- **Training:** "1861. William abandons painting and enters Lawrence Scientific School at Harvard." "1869. Receives M. D. degree, but never practices." (§1).
- **Harvard:** "1872. Accepts offer from President Eliot of Harvard to teach undergraduate course in comparative physiology." "1874–5. Begins teaching psychology; establishes first American psychology laboratory." "1880. Appointed Assistant Professor of Philosophy at Harvard." (§1). "1907. Resigns Harvard professorship." (§1).
- **Marriage:** "1878. Marries Alice Howe Gibbens." (§1).
- **Politics:** under 1899, "Becomes active member of the Anti-Imperialist League, opposing U. S. policy in Philippines." (§1).
- **Pragmatism, the name:** "1898. Identifies himself as a pragmatist in “Philosophical Conceptions and Practical Results,” given at the University of California, Berkeley." (§1). James credits the method to Peirce: "It was first introduced into philosophy by Mr. Charles Peirce in 1878. In an article entitled 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear,' in the 'Popular Science Monthly' for January of that year" (*Pragmatism*, Lecture II).
- **Death:** 1910: "Dies of heart failure at summer home in Chocorua, New Hampshire." (§1).

## Problems addressed

- **The basis of ethics** ("The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life", 1891; no problem page yet). The occasion: "An Address to the Yale Philosophical Club, published in the International Journal of Ethics, April, 1891." (*WB* p. 215, n. 1). The aim: "The main purpose of this paper is to show that there is no such thing possible as an ethical philosophy dogmatically made up in advance." (p. 184). Good and obligation "are objects of feeling and desire, which have no foothold or anchorage in Being, apart from the existence of actually living minds." (p. 197), and "'The religion of humanity' affords a basis for ethics as well as theism does." (p. 198). The principle he proposes, as a question: "must not the guiding principle for ethical philosophy (since all demands conjointly cannot be satisfied in this poor world) be simply to satisfy at all times _as many demands as we can_?" (p. 205).
  Goodman (§4): "Morality for James rests on sentience—without it there are no moral claims and no moral obligations." and "Although James insists that there is no common essence to morality, he does find a guiding principle for ethical philosophy in the principle that we “satisfy at all times as many demands as we can” (WB 205)."
- **The lost soul** (same essay, p. 188). James asks, of "a world in which Messrs. Fourier's and Bellamy's and Morris's Utopias should all be outdone, and millions kept permanently happy on the one simple condition that a certain lost soul on the far-off edge of things should lead a life of lonely torture", "what except a specifical and independent sort of emotion" would make us feel "how hideous a thing would be its enjoyment when deliberately accepted as the fruit of such a bargain?" (full sentence in the raw file). The raw file's context note records that the example is one of several offered to show that moral feelings are not reducible to association of pleasures and pains, not an objection to a named author. On Le Guin's use of it, see In dialogue with.
- **Belief on insufficient evidence** ("The Will to Believe", 1896; no problem page yet). "An Address to the Philosophical Clubs of Yale and Brown Universities. Published in the New World, June, 1896." (*WB* p. 31, n. 1). The essay discusses Pascal: "In Pascal's Thoughts there is a celebrated passage known in literature as Pascal's wager." (p. 5). Against W. K. Clifford's maxim "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for every one, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." (quoted p. 8), James defines "a _genuine_ option when it is of the forced, living, and momentous kind." (p. 3) and states his thesis: "_Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds;" (p. 11; the sentence continues in the raw file). Goodman (§4): "James applies his analysis to religious belief, particularly to the possible case in which one’s salvation depends on believing in God in advance of any proof that God exists." "In such a case the belief may be justified by the outcome to which having the belief leads."
- **Free will and determinism** ("The Dilemma of Determinism", 1884). "An Address to the Harvard Divinity Students, published in the Unitarian Review for September, 1884." (*WB* p. 183, n. 1). James contrasts "a _soft_ determinism which abhors harsh words, and, repudiating fatality, necessity, and even predetermination, says that its real name is freedom;" (p. 149) with indeterminism, which "says that the parts have a certain amount of loose play on one another," (p. 150). His target is soft determinism, "the determinism which allows considerations of good and bad to mingle with those of cause and effect" (p. 166): "The dilemma of this determinism is one whose left horn is pessimism and whose right horn is subjectivism." (p. 166). From judgments of regret: "I cannot understand regret without the admission of real, genuine possibilities in the world." (p. 175); his own preference is stated as such: "For my own part, though, whatever difficulties may beset the philosophy of objective right and wrong, and the indeterminism it seems to imply, determinism, with its alternative of pessimism or romanticism, contains difficulties that are greater still." (p. 176).
  In *Pragmatism* (Lecture III): "Free-will pragmatically means NOVELTIES IN THE WORLD," and persons whom the past has made pessimistic "may naturally welcome free-will as a MELIORISTIC doctrine." Goodman (§3) reports the same tension in the *Principles*: science "must be constantly reminded that her purposes are not the only purposes," (full sentence in the raw file).
- **[Foreknowledge and freedom](../problems/argument-from-free-will.md)** (*WB* pp. 180–181). James's "final doubt": "Does not the admission of such an unguaranteed chance or freedom preclude utterly the notion of a Providence governing the world?" (p. 180). His answer: "The belief in free-will is not in the least incompatible with the belief in Providence, provided you do not restrict the Providence to fulminating nothing but _fatal_ decrees." (p. 180), illustrated by a chess game in which "The expert intends to beat. But he cannot foresee exactly what any one actual move of his adversary may be." (p. 181): "Let now the novice stand for us finite free agents, and the expert for the infinite mind in which the universe lies." (p. 181). *Structural note (this implant):* the problem page's sources do not list James; the link rests on James's own text, which addresses the same pair of beliefs (Providence and free will); no read source classifies his answer among that page's positions.
- **Truth and meaning** (*Pragmatism*, 1907; no problem page yet). The method: "The pragmatic method is primarily a method of settling metaphysical disputes that otherwise might be interminable." (Lecture II); "What difference would it practically make to anyone if this notion rather than that notion were true?" (Lecture II). Truth: "TRUE IDEAS ARE THOSE THAT WE CAN ASSIMILATE, VALIDATE, CORROBORATE AND VERIFY. FALSE IDEAS ARE THOSE THAT WE CANNOT." and "'The true,' to put it very briefly, is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as 'the right' is only the expedient in the way of our behaving." (Lecture VI). Goodman (§6): "Pragmatism emerges in James’s book as six things: a philosophical temperament, a theory of truth, a theory of meaning, a holistic account of knowledge, a metaphysical view, and a method of resolving philosophical disputes." Goodman (preamble) reports that essays of 1878–1882 "contain the first statements of his view that philosophical theories are reflections of a philosopher’s temperament." Russell's objection is under Reception.
- **Religion and religious experience** (*Varieties*, 1902; *Pragmatism* Lecture VIII). The lectures were "the Gifford Lectures on Natural Religion Delivered at Edinburgh in 1901‐1902" (title page). His definition, which he asks the audience "arbitrarily to take": "_the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine_." (Lecture II). Mystical states are marked by "Ineffability", a "Noetic quality" and "Transiency" (Lectures XVI–XVII; a fourth, passivity, follows). In 1907: "On pragmatistic principles, if the hypothesis of God works satisfactorily in the widest sense of the word, it is true." (Lecture VIII). Goodman (§5) reports that James "finds that religious experience is on the whole useful, even “amongst the most important biological functions of mankind,” but he concedes that this does not make it true." and that he "articulates his own belief—which he does not claim to prove—that religious experiences connect us with a greater, or further, reality".
- **[Consciousness](../problems/consciousness.md)** (*Principles of Psychology*, 1890, ch. IX). The problem page's timeline cites the five characters of thought and "the stream of thought, of consciousness" (*Principles* I, pp. 225, 239; `raw/james-1890-principles-ch9-stream-of-thought.md`). On [panpsychism](../positions/panpsychism.md), the SEP entry "Panpsychism" (§1) traces the combination problem to James's "mind dust" objections: "These objections are the inspiration for the so-called 'combination problem', around which much of the twenty first century literature on panpsychism focuses." (`raw/goff-2017-panpsychism-via-sep.md`).
- **[Zeno's paradoxes](../problems/zenos-paradoxes.md)** (*Some Problems of Philosophy*, 1911). Huggett (SEP "Zeno's Paradoxes", §5): "Such thinkers as Bergson (1911), James (1911, Ch 10–11) and Whitehead (1929) argued that Zeno’s paradoxes show that space and time are not structured as a mathematical continuum" (`raw/sep-paradox-zeno-fall-2024-motion-paradoxes-and-responses.md`).

## In dialogue with

- **[John Stuart Mill](mill.md)** (earlier). *Pragmatism* is dedicated "To the Memory of John Stuart Mill from whom I first learned the pragmatic openness of mind and whom my fancy likes to picture as our leader were he alive to-day." (dedication).
- **Charles Sanders Peirce**. Goodman (§6) names "the pragmatist tradition that he founded (along with Charles Peirce)". Legg & Hookway (preamble) report "arguments between Peirce and James which led Peirce to rename his view pragmaticism," (full passage in the raw file).
- **W. K. Clifford**: the maxim "The Will to Believe" argues against (above).
- **[Kant](kant.md)**, **[Schopenhauer](schopenhauer.md)** and others, named in "The Moral Philosopher": "think of Kant and Schopenhauer, of Herbert Spencer and John Henry Newman, no longer as one-sided champions of special ideals, but as schoolmasters deciding what all must think," (*WB* p. 204).
- **[Hegel](hegel.md)**, Royce, Fechner, Bergson: in *A Pluralistic Universe*, Goodman (§6) reports, "James passes from critical discussions of Josiah Royce’s idealism and the “vicious intellectualism” of Hegel to philosophers whose visions he admires: Gustav Fechner and Henri Bergson."
- **[Spinoza](spinoza.md)** and **[Hume](hume.md)**: Goodman (§2): "Spinoza’s unity of all things in one substance is “barren,” as is Hume’s “‘looseness and separateness’ of everything…” (WB 60)."
- **[John Dewey](dewey.md)** (later): named by Goodman (preamble) among those the *Principles* "influenced"; Legg & Hookway (preamble) group them on truth: "a more broad-based alethic pluralism (following James and Dewey)".
- **Bertrand Russell** (later): his objection is under Reception. Goodman (§6): "Bertrand Russell’s The Analysis of Mind is indebted to James’s doctrine of “pure experience,” (Russell, 1921, pp. 22–6)".
- **[Carl Gustav Jung](jung.md)** (later). *Psychological Types* (1921) ch. VIII discusses the two columns of *Pragmatism* Lecture I ("THE TENDER-MINDED Rationalistic" against "THE TOUGH-MINDED Empiricist"; full list in `raw/james-1907-pragmatism-temperaments-free-will-meliorism.md`). Jung: "James’ characterization deals with those same types which I have termed the introverted and the extraverted." (p. 382; `raw/jung-1923-psychological-types-baynes.md`; see [Psychological Types](../works/psychological-types.md)).
- **Ursula K. Le Guin** (later). Wikipedia's article on "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (rev. 1377245403), citing *The Wind's Twelve Quarters* (1975), pp. 275–276, reports: "Le Guin gave credit to William James, who mentioned the idea in 1891," and quotes her: "The fact is, I haven't been able to re-read Dostoyevsky, much as I loved him, since I was twenty-five, and I'd simply forgotten he used the idea." (`raw/wikipedia-ones-who-walk-away-from-omelas-james-credit.md`; Le Guin's book not read). The article quotes the lost-soul sentence with "sceptical" where the 1897 Gutenberg text reads "specifical".
- **Husserl, Wittgenstein, neopragmatists** (later): see Goodman's legacy paragraph under Reception.

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing (Goodman).** "William James was an original thinker in and between the disciplines of physiology, psychology and philosophy." (preamble). Of the *Principles*: "It contains seeds of pragmatism and phenomenology, and influenced generations of thinkers in Europe and America, including Edmund Husserl, Bertrand Russell, John Dewey, and Ludwig Wittgenstein." (preamble).
  Legacy (§6): "Edmund Husserl incorporated James’s notions of the “fringe” and “halo” into his phenomenology (Moran, pp. 276–80), Bertrand Russell’s The Analysis of Mind is indebted to James’s doctrine of “pure experience,” (Russell, 1921, pp. 22–6), Ludwig Wittgenstein learned about “the absence of the will act” from James’s Psychology (Goodman, Wittgenstein and William James, p. 81), and the versions of “neopragmatism” set out by Nelson Goodman, Richard Rorty and Hilary Putnam are saturated with James’s ideas."
  Goodman's own appraisal (§6): "James is one of the most attractive and endearing of philosophers: for his vision of a “wild,” “open” universe that is nevertheless shaped by our human powers and answers to some of our deepest needs, but also, as Russell observed in his obituary, because of the “large tolerance and … humanity” with which he sets that vision out."
- **"The Will to Believe" (Goodman §4).** "The title essay—published just two years earlier—proved to be controversial for seeming to recommend irresponsible or irrationally held beliefs." James's later reply, as Goodman reports it: "James later wrote that he should have called the essay “the right to believe,” to indicate his intent to justify holding certain beliefs in certain circumstances, not to claim that we can (or should) believe things simply by an act of will."
- **Russell's objection to the theory of truth (Legg & Hookway §3).** "The kind of passages just noted may seem to support Bertrand Russell’s famous objection that James is committed to the truth of “Santa Claus exists” (Russell 1946, p, 772)." Their assessment: "This is unfair; at best, James is committed to the claim that the happiness that belief in Santa Claus provides is truth-relevant." They add, per the raw file's context note, that James would call such a belief "wholly true" only if it did not "clash with other vital benefits". Russell 1946 was not read here; his objection is as Legg & Hookway report it.
- **Peirce's distancing (Legg & Hookway, preamble).** Peirce presented "pragmaticism" as his "new ‘baby’ which was, he hoped, ‘ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers’ (EP2: 355)."
- **On the temperaments (Jung 1921, ch. VIII).** "In my view, therefore, James’ “tender-minded” and “tough-minded” are manifestly but a one-sided terminology, and at bottom conceal a certain prejudice." (p. 382). James himself voices the objection in Lecture I: "Tender-minded and tough-minded, what a barbaric disjunction!"
- **On truth and postmodernism (Pratt, IEP "Nihilism", §4).** "Postmodern antifoundationalists, paradoxically grounded in relativism, dismiss knowledge as relational and “truth” as transitory, genuine only until something more palatable replaces it (reminiscent of William James’ notion of “cash value”)." (`raw/iep-nihilism-pratt-definition-and-nietzsche.md`; quoted on [the paradox of nihilism](../problems/paradox-of-nihilism.md)).
- **On religion (Goodman, preamble).** "James oscillated between thinking that a “study in human nature” such as Varieties could contribute to a “Science of Religion” and the belief that religious experience involves an altogether supernatural domain, somehow inaccessible to science but accessible to the individual human subject."

## Vocabulary

- *Dilemma* in "the dilemma of determinism": James names two horns, "pessimism" and "subjectivism" (*WB* p. 166), the two-horned form of the implant's [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md), not its moral-dilemma sense.
- *Soft determinism*: his name (p. 149) for determinism that calls itself freedom; *chance*: the word he prefers to "freedom" because "It is a word of _impotence_, and is therefore the only sincere word we can use," (p. 180).
- *Genuine option*: an option "of the forced, living, and momentous kind" (p. 3).
- *True* and *truth*: in *Pragmatism* (Lecture VI), what we can "VALIDATE, CORROBORATE AND VERIFY"; "Truth HAPPENS to an idea." No vocabulary page for truth yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Religion*: in the *Varieties*, a working definition he calls arbitrary (Lecture II).
- *Stream of consciousness*: his metaphor (*Principles* I, p. 239); see [consciousness](../vocabulary/consciousness.md).

Related thinkers: [Zeno of Elea](zeno-of-elea.md) (Huggett §5), [Russell](russell.md) (Legg & Hookway).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "was born in Starzeddel (now Starosiedle, Poland), in Brandenburg, Germany, on August 20, 1886, the son of a Lutheran pastor." (Scharlemann). Alston: "the son of a Lutheran pastor".
- **Education:** the BCE (§1): "In 1911, he received a doctoral degree in philosophy from the University of Breslau, and earned the licentiate in theology from the University of Halle in 1912." Scharlemann instead names Berlin for the Ph.D. and Halle for the theology doctorate; the sources differ.
- **Ordination:** "ordained in the Evangelical Lutheran Church in 1912." (Alston); "he was ordained into the ministry in 1912." (Scharlemann).
- **War and German posts:** "He served as an army chaplain during World War I and then taught theology and philosophy at Berlin, Marburg, Dresden, and Frankfurt." (Alston). The BCE dates Marburg to 1924, "in which the neo-orthodoxy theology of Karl Barth was incredibly popular", and reports that "Tillich met Martin Heidegger at the university and was deeply influenced by his thought." (§1).
- **Emigration:** Scharlemann reports that *Die sozialistische Entscheidung* (1933) was "a cautious analysis of socialism and a critique of unrestrained capitalism" and that its criticism of National Socialism led to his emigration: "Tillich left Germany in October 1933. In February 1934 he began his long teaching career at Union Theological Seminary in New York , remaining there until his retirement in 1955." Alston gives Union "from 1933 to 1956".
- **Later posts:** "He then became University Professor at Harvard — a great distinction — and in 1962 he became, with similar distinction, the Nuveen Professor of Theology at the University of Chicago ." (Scharlemann, whose valuation it is).
- **Death:** "his death on October 22, 1965," (Scharlemann).
- **Influences** (as the BCE §1 reports them): "Synthesizing what he had learned from Schelling, Kahler and Kierkegaard and Husserl, Tillich reflected himself in his subsequent theological works with tremendous indebtedness to the thought of Existentialism."

## Problems addressed

No problem page in this implant names Tillich yet (a grep of
[problems](../problems/index.md) finds none); the problems below are
stated as his sources and the encyclopedias frame them.

- **What grounds morality: autonomy, heteronomy, theonomy.** "Autonomy and heteronomy are rooted in theonomy, and each goes astray when their theonomous unity is broken. Theonomy does not mean the acceptance of a divine law imposed on reason by a highest authority; it means autonomous reason united with its own depth." (ST I, 85). "Since God (theos) is the law (nomos) for both the structure and the ground of reason, they are united in him, and their unity is manifest in a theonomous situation." (ST I, 85). On morality without religion: "Without an ultimate concern as its basis every system of morals degenerates into a method of adjustment to social demands, whether they are ultimately justified or not." (*Dynamics of Faith*, ch. VI §4). The neighbouring question of divine command is on the [Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md) page, which does not discuss Tillich.
- **Love as the ultimate moral principle.** "The law of love is the ultimate law because it is the negation of law; it is absolute because it concerns everything concrete." (ST I, 152). Asked how moral self-integration is possible, he answers: "We answered: By love in the sense of agape! For love includes the ultimate, though formal, principle of justice, and love applies it in an ever changing way to the concrete situation." (ST III, 46). "The principle of agape expresses the unconditional validity of the moral imperative, and it gives the ultimate norm for all ethical content. But it has still a third function: it is the source of moral motivation." (ST III, 48). On love's forms: "No love is real without a unity of eros and agape. Agape without eros is obedience to a moral law, without warmth, without longing, without reunion." (*Dynamics of Faith*, ch. VI §3).
- **Formalism and the content of the moral law.** He states the objection from Kant: "One can defend the pure formalism of ethics, as it appears, for example, in Kant and reject agape as ultimate principle just because it leads to ambiguous decisions which are lacking in unconditional validity." His reply: "It seems that radical ethical formalism is logically impossible because the form always keeps traits of that from which it has been abstracted." (ST III, 46).
- **Moral risk and conscience.** "Every moral decision is a risk because there is no guarantee that it fulfils the law of love, the unconditional demand coming from the encounter with the other one." (ST III, 47). "It is a risk to follow one’s conscience; it is a greater risk to contradict it." (ST III, 48).
- **Courage, anxiety and meaninglessness.** "Courage is an ethical reality, but it is rooted in the whole breadth of human existence and ultimately in the structure of being itself. It must be considered ontologically in order to be understood ethically." (*The Courage to Be*, 1). Alston summarises the book's three anxieties as "the anxiety of death, that of meaninglessness, and that of guilt" ("Anxiety"), answered by participation in God as "the infinite power to resist the threat of nonbeing".
- **Faith and its object (ultimate concern).** "Faith is the state of being ultimately concerned: the dynamics of faith are the dynamics of man’s ultimate concern." (*Dynamics of Faith*, ch. I §1). "The ultimate concern is unconditional, independent of any conditions of character, desire, or circumstance." (ST I, 12). He calls it "the abstract translation of the great commandment" (ST I, 11).
- **God and theism ("God above God").** "The content of absolute faith is the "God above God." Absolute faith and its consequence, the courage that takes the radical doubt, the doubt about God, into itself, transcends the theistic idea of God." (*Courage*, 182). "The ultimate source of the courage to be is the "God above God"; this is the result of our demand to transcend theism." (186). "There are no valid arguments for the "existence" of God, but there are acts of courage in which we affirm the power of being, whether we know it or not." (181). Scharlemann: "The correlation between the two is formulated in the statement "God is being-itself.""
- **Religious language as symbol.** Scott (SEP "Religious Language", §3.1): "Tillich’s (1951) theory that religious language is symbolic is perhaps an early expression of the view, and Anthony Kenny and Sally McFague are more recent proponents of a view of this type (see also Jüngel 1974; Sarot 1992). ## In dialogue with

- **[Karl Barth](barth.md)** (contemporary). Tillich classes him as kerygmatic: "Reformation theology and, in our own day, the neo-Reformation theology of Barth and his school are outstanding examples of kerygmatic theology." (ST I, 4). "Barth’s greatness is that he corrects himself again and again in the light of the “situation” and that he strenuously tries not to become his own follower. Yet he does not realize that in doing so he ceases to be a merely kerygmatic theologian." (ST I, 5); his example is Barth "deriving the duty of making war against Hitler from the resurrection of the Christ" (ST I, 5). Tillich records Barth's objection to his own method: correlation "has evoked the protest of theologians such as Karl Barth, who are afraid that any kind of divine-human correlation makes God partly dependent on man." His reply: God "is in no way dependent on man, God in his self-manifestation to man is dependent on the way man receives his manifestation." (ST I, 61). Aho (SEP "Existentialism") names both: "Its engagement with the relationship between faith and freedom and the incomprehensibility of God shaped theological debates through the lectures and writings of Karl Barth, Paul Tillich, and Martin Buber, among others."
- **[Kierkegaard](kierkegaard.md)** (earlier). Alston: "He was strongly influenced by existentialism, and he held, as did S ø ren Kierkegaard, that religious questions are appropriately raised only in relation to problems that are inherent in the "human situation" and that theological claims are not mere responses to theoretical puzzles." Tillich: "When Kierkegaard broke away from Hegel's system of essences he did two things: he proclaimed an existential attitude and he instigated a philosophy of existence." (*Courage*, 125). Bishop & McKaughan (SEP "Faith", §7) put both under the "doxastic venture" model: "So too is Paul Tillich’s account of faith as ‘the state of being ultimately concerned’,".
- **[Kant](kant.md)** (earlier). Tillich's reply to "the pure formalism of ethics, as it appears, for example, in Kant" (ST III, 46), quoted above.
- **[Nietzsche](nietzsche.md)** (earlier). Tillich on the God of "theological theism": "This is the God Nietzsche said had to be killed because nobody can tolerate being made into a mere object of absolute knowledge and absolute control. This is the deepest root of atheism." (*Courage*, 185).
- **[Aquinas](aquinas.md)** and **[Augustine](augustine.md)** (earlier). Bishop & McKaughan (§7): "Nevertheless, Aquinas’s own assumptions on these matters may leave him closer to Kierkegaard and Tillich than is commonly thought". Mander (SEP "Pantheism", §4): "The identification of God with being itself is a common Christian view, from Augustine to Tillich, but it is not exclusive to Christian thought."
- **Heidegger, Schelling, Husserl** (no pages yet): the BCE §1 influences quoted under Facts.
- **William P. Alston** and **Paul Edwards** (later critics): see Reception. Edwards, "Professor Tillich's Confusions", *Mind* 74 (1965): 192–214, [doi:10.1093/mind/lxxiv.294.192](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lxxiv.294.192), answered by Jerry H. Gill, "Professor Edwards' Confusions", *Mind* 76 (1967): 587–591, [doi:10.1093/mind/lxxvi.304.587](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lxxvi.304.587). ## Reception

Assessments for and against, each with its owner; none is the implant's.

- **Influence (Scharlemann).** "Tillich's wide influence, especially in the United States , is attributable to the ecumenical character of this theology, to the effectiveness of his teaching, the appeal of his work to professionals as well as to the laity, and, no doubt, to his extraordinary ability to relate theology to the issues of the time."
- **On ultimate concern (Alston, for and against).** Against: "But despite what Tillich said, it would seem that such orientation around a single object is a rare achievement." and "It is not clear, however, that if a concern is ultimate (in the sense of being the dominant interest of a person), the object of the concern is necessarily Ultimate in the relevant sense;" ("Faith"; "The Ultimate"). For: "Nevertheless, Tillich's analysis of religiosity is a penetrating one, and it reveals the important resemblances between religiosity and nonreligious modes of personal organization." ("Faith").
- **On symbols and meaningfulness (Alston).** "Unfortunately, Tillich never gave an intelligible account of these closely interrelated concepts of symbolizing and pointing to, which are so crucial for his position." ("Religious Symbols"). On idolatry: "He sometimes said that some ultimate concerns are "idolatrous" because they are directed at finite objects rather than at the Ultimate. But by his own principles Tillich could not say this," ("Defense of Christianity"). Scott (SEP) names Alston "the chief critic of the theory". Paul Edwards's critique in *Mind* (1965) and Gill's reply (1967) are on record (see above; not read).
- **On God and the atheism charge (Alston, both sides).** "Tillich did not, in fact, accept the notion of a personal deity." "If the God of theism is a person, the often repeated charge that Tillich is really an atheist thus seems justified; yet Tillich can point out that in the past Christian theology has repeatedly found difficulty in the notion that God is a person in any straightforward or literal sense." ("God"). Tillich's own position on theism is quoted under Problems (*Courage*, 182–187): "The God above the God of theism is present, although hidden, in every divine-human encounter." (187).
- **On faith and idolatry (Bishop & McKaughan, SEP "Faith").** "The theist traditions hold a deep fear of idolatry—of giving one’s ‘ultimate concern’ (Tillich 1957 [2001]) to an object unworthy of it." (§4); "Faith will not, however, be a virtue as such , if it is accepted that faith can be misplaced or, even, ‘demonic’, directed upon a ‘false ultimate’ (Tillich 1957 [2001, 21])." (§10).
- **Barth's side.** Barth's objection is recorded here only as Tillich reports it (ST I, 61, above); the [Barth](barth.md) page carries Barth's own texts.

Not covered for lack of a readable source: Mark Lewis Taylor, "Tillich's ethics between politics and ontology", in *The Cambridge Companion to Paul Tillich* (2009), 189–207, [doi:10.1017/ccol9780521859899.012](https://doi.org/10.1017/ccol9780521859899.012); Robert C. Coburn, "God, Revelation and Religious Truth", *Faith and Philosophy* 13 (1996): 3–33, [doi:10.5840/faithphil199613113](https://doi.org/10.5840/faithphil199613113).

## Vocabulary

None of these terms has a page in [vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md) yet.

- *Ultimate concern*: for Tillich, faith itself ("the state of being ultimately concerned", *Dynamics of Faith*, ch. I §1), not a psychological interest; Alston reads it as "the dominant interest of a person" when he criticises the inference to an Ultimate object.
- *Theonomy*: not divine command but "autonomous reason united with its own depth" (ST I, 85), set against autonomy and heteronomy.
- *Agape*: love as "the ultimate norm for all ethical content" and "the source of moral motivation" (ST III, 48); held together with eros (*Dynamics of Faith*, ch. VI §3).
- *God above God*: the content of "absolute faith", beyond "the theistic idea of God" (*Courage*, 182).
- *Being-itself*: Tillich's term for what the symbol God presents; Scharlemann: "being-itself, which, as such, is beyond both being and nonbeing."
- *Kerygmatic* versus *apologetic* theology and the *method of correlation*: ST I, 4–6 and 61; Barth is his example of the kerygmatic type.

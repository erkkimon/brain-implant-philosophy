## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and family:** "(1872) Born May 18 at Ravenscroft in Trelleck, Monmouthshire, UK." (Irvine §1, chronology). His grandfather was "Lord John Russell (the former Prime Minister)" (§1, 1876 entry).
- **Cambridge:** "(1890) Enters Trinity College, Cambridge; meets Whitehead." (§1).
- **The paradox and Frege:** "(1901) Reappointed lecturer at Cambridge; discovers Russell’s paradox." "(1902) Corresponds with Frege." (§1). On the month, Irvine & Deutsch (§2): "Exactly when the discovery took place is not clear." "Russell wrote to Frege with news of his paradox on June 16, 1902." (§2).
- **Descriptions:** "(1905) Develops his theory of descriptions." (§1).
- **First World War:** "(1916) Fined 100 pounds and dismissed from Trinity College as a result of anti-war writings; denied a passport and so unable to lecture at Harvard." "(1918) Imprisoned for five months as a result of anti-war writings." (§1). Irvine's narrative: "The result was five months in Brixton Prison as prisoner No. 2917." (§1).
- **City College, 1940:** "(1940) Appointment at City College New York revoked prior to Russell’s arrival as the result of public protests and a legal judgment in which Russell was found to be “morally unfit” to teach at the college; delivers the William James Lectures at Harvard." "The legal decision had been based partly on Russell’s atheism and partly on his fame as an advocate of free love and open marriages." (§1; the judgment's finding, as Irvine reports it).
- **Honours:** "(1950) Awarded Nobel Prize for Literature; visits Australia." The preamble adds the Order of Merit in 1949.
- **Nuclear campaigns:** "(1955) Releases Russell-Einstein Manifesto." "(1958) Becomes founding President of the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament." "(1961) Imprisoned for one week in connection with anti-nuclear protests." (§1).
- **Death:** "(1970) Dies February 02 at Penrhyndeudraeth, Wales." (§1).
- **Pacifism, as a secondary source names it:** Fiala (SEP "Pacifism") lists him among those Gandhi inspired: "Gandhi in turn inspired Western pacifists such as Albert Einstein, Bertrand Russell, and Martin Luther King Jr." (`raw/sep-pacifism-fall-2024-fiala-jesus-sermon-nonresistance-tolstoy-gandhi.md`).

## Problems addressed

- **[Russell's paradox](../problems/russells-paradox.md)** (1901; *Principles* 1903, ch. X; 1908). Russell (1919, ch. XIII, p. 136): "When I first came upon this contradiction, in the year 1901, I attempted to discover some flaw in Cantor's proof that there is no greatest cardinal, which we gave in Chapter VIII." (`raw/russell-1903-1919-contradiction-discovery-and-types.md` §B). In 1903 the remedy was offered with reserve: "The doctrine of types is here put forward tentatively, as affording a possible solution of the contradiction" (Appendix B, §497, p. 523). In 1908 he states the general diagnosis: "Thus all our contradictions have in common the assumption of a totality such that, if it were legitimate, it would at once be enlarged by new members defined in terms of itself." (p. 225; `raw/russell-1908-theory-of-types-richards-paradox.md`). Irvine & Deutsch (§3): "Russell’s type theory thus appears in two versions: the “simple theory” of 1903 and the “ramified theory” of 1908. Both versions have been criticized for being too ad hoc to eliminate the paradox successfully." The other response families are on the problem page.
- **[The barber paradox](../problems/barber-paradox.md)** (lectures of 1918, printed 1919). "I once had a form suggested to me which was not valid, namely the question whether the barber shaves himself or not." and "In this form the contradiction is not very difficult to solve." (*The Philosophy of Logical Atomism*, lecture VII, pp. 354–355; `raw/russell-1918-1919-logical-atomism-lecture-7-barber.md`). Irvine & Deutsch (§4) ask: "How do these “pseudo paradoxes,” as they are sometimes called, differ, if at all, from Russell’s paradox?"
- **[The liar](../problems/liar-paradox.md) and [the Epimenides](../problems/epimenides-paradox.md)** (1908). "The oldest contradiction of the kind in question is the Epimenides." (p. 222). His solution by orders: "Thus when a man says "I am lying," we must interpret him as meaning: "There is a proposition of order n, which I affirm, and which is false."" (p. 240; `raw/russell-1908-theory-of-types-epimenides.md`). Quine (1962) on the truth hierarchy: "This restriction can be relaxed somewhat by admitting a hierarchy of truth locutions, as suggested by the work of Bertrand Russell and Alfred Tarski." (`raw/quine-1962-1976-ways-of-paradox-quotation-antinomy.md`; see [Quine's paradox](../problems/quines-paradox.md)).
- **[Berry's paradox](../problems/berry-paradox.md)** (1906, 1908). "This contradiction was suggested to me by Mr. G. G. Berry of the Bodleian Library." (1908, p. 223, footnote); his diagnosis: "Hence "all names" is an illegitimate notion." (p. 225; `raw/russell-1908-theory-of-types-berry-paradox.md`). Cantini & Bruni (SEP "Paradoxes and Contemporary Logic", §3.1): "Under the influence of Poincaré, Russell (1906, p. 634) accepted the vicious circle principle" (`raw/sep-paradoxes-contemporary-logic-fall-2024-berry-paradox.md`).
- **[Richard's paradox](../problems/richards-paradox.md)** (1908, contradiction (6)). "This is solved, like (5), by remarking that "all definitions" is an illegitimate notion." (p. 225; `raw/russell-1908-theory-of-types-richards-paradox.md`).
- **[Curry's paradox](../problems/currys-paradox.md)** (as later authors relate it to his). Shapiro & Beall (SEP "Curry's Paradox", §4.1): "In the context of set theory Curry-incompleteness responses include Russellian type theories and various theories that restrict the “naive” set abstraction principle (Set)." Prior (1955: 180), as they quote him: "We can … say not only that Curry’s paradox does not involve negation but that even Russell’s paradox presupposes only those properties of negation which it shares with implication." (`raw/sep-curry-paradox-fall-2024-construction-lemma-and-responses.md`).
- **[The barbershop paradox](../problems/barbershop-paradox.md)** (*Principles* §19 n. 1). "The principle that false propositions imply all propositions solves Lewis Carroll’s logical paradox in Mind, N. S. No. 11 (1894)." (`raw/russell-1903-principles-19-note-carrolls-paradox.md`).
- **[What the Tortoise said to Achilles](../problems/tortoise-and-achilles.md)** (*Principles* §38). "We need, in fact, the notion of therefore, which is quite different from the notion of implies, and holds between different entities." (`raw/russell-1903-principles-38-tortoise-therefore.md`).
- **[Zeno's paradoxes](../problems/zenos-paradoxes.md)** (1914, Lecture VI). "They are not, however, on any view, mere foolish quibbles: they are serious arguments, raising difficulties which it has taken two thousand years to answer" and "The definitive solution of the difficulties is due, not to Bolzano, but to Georg Cantor, whose work on this subject first appeared in 1882." (`raw/russell-1914-external-world-lecture-6-zeno.md`). Huggett (SEP "Zeno's Paradoxes", §1.2): "Following a lead given by Russell (1929, 182–198), a number of philosophers—notably Grünbaum (1967), but also see Salmon (2001)—took up the task of showing how modern mathematics could solve all of Zeno’s paradoxes" (`raw/sep-paradox-zeno-fall-2024-motion-paradoxes-and-responses.md`).
- **Self-reference as the common source.** Russell (1908, p. 224): "In all the above contradictions (which are merely selections from an indefinite number) there is a common characteristic, which we may describe as self-reference or reflexiveness." Yablo (1993, p. 251): "Why are some sentences paradoxical while others are not? Since Russell the universal answer has been: circularity, and more especially self-reference." (`raw/yablo-1993-paradox-without-self-reference.md`; see [Yablo's paradox](../problems/yablos-paradox.md)).
- **Denoting and descriptions** (1905; no problem page yet). "This is the principle of the theory of denoting I wish to advocate: that denoting phrases never have any meaning in themselves, but that every proposition in whose verbal expression they occur has a meaning." Of the puzzle about the present King of France and excluded middle: "Hegelians, who love a synthesis, will probably conclude that he wears a wig." (`raw/russell-1905-1912-on-denoting-problems-of-philosophy.md` §A). Irvine (§4): descriptions are "not to be viewed as referring terms but, rather, as “incomplete symbols.” In other words, they are to be viewed as symbols that take on meaning within appropriate contexts, but that remain meaningless in isolation." Moore's paper on the theory is where he states [Moore's paradox](../problems/moores-paradox.md) (Schilpp ed., 1944, p. 204; `raw/moore-1944-russells-theory-of-descriptions-he-has-gone-out.md`).
- **Logicism and logical atomism** (no problem pages yet). Irvine (§2): "Russell’s logicism consisted of two main theses." "The first was that all mathematical truths can be translated into logical truths or, in other words, that the vocabulary of mathematics constitutes a proper subset of the vocabulary of logic." The second, in his words, "was that all mathematical proofs can be recast as logical proofs"; "Even so, the issue of whether set theory itself can be said to have been successfully reduced to logic remained controversial." (§2). On atomism (§3): "On the metaphysical side, Russell developed his famous theory of logical atomism, in which the world is said to consist of a complex of logical atoms (such as “little patches of colour”) and their properties and relations." The 1918 lectures, in Russell's words, explain "certain ideas which I learnt from my friend and former pupil Ludwig Wittgenstein." (`raw/russell-1918-1919-logical-atomism-lecture-7-barber.md`).
- **Knowledge and induction** (1912; no problem page yet). "We shall say that we have _acquaintance_ with anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths." (ch. V); "The man who has fed the chicken every day throughout its life at last wrings its neck instead, showing that more refined views as to the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken." (ch. VI; `raw/russell-1905-1912-on-denoting-problems-of-philosophy.md` §B).
- **Metaethics** (no problem page yet). Pigden (§1): "After 1903, he became an enthusiastic but critical convert to the doctrines of Principia Ethica"; "Russell subscribed to this thesis—with certain important reservations—until 1913." The later view: "He tended to call it subjectivism or “the subjectivity of moral values” though it is nowadays known as non-cognitivism, expressivism or emotivism." On it, "What “X is good” means is “Would that everyone desired X!”." In 1922 he briefly held an error theory (preamble). Russell's own perplexity, as Pigden quotes it: "I cannot see how to refute the arguments for the subjectivity of ethical values, but I find myself incapable of believing that all that is wrong with wanton cruelty is that I don’t like it." (RoE 165). Normative ethics: "Russell was an act-consequentialist rather than a rule-consequentialist." (§6).
- **Religion** (no problem page yet). Russell, as Irvine (§6) quotes him: "My own view on religion is that of Lucretius. I regard it as a disease born of fear and as a source of untold misery to the human race” (A1957, 18)." (Russell's claim; Irvine notes the 1948 BBC debate on the existence of God with the Jesuit Frederick Copleston, §1.)

## In dialogue with

- **Gottlob Frege** (contemporary). Irvine & Deutsch (§2): "The paradox was of significance to Frege’s logical work since, in effect, it showed that the axioms Frege was using to formalize his logic were inconsistent." and "Frege added to the Grundgesetze a hastily composed appendix discussing Russell’s discovery." Russell in 1903, of Frege's vol. II: "As it seems very likely that this is the true solution, the reader is strongly recommended to examine" (Appendix A note, p. 522). No Frege page yet.
- **A. N. Whitehead** (co-author). Irvine & Deutsch (§3) call *Principia* "the monumental work he co-authored with Alfred North Whitehead, Principia Mathematica (1910, 1912, 1913)." (their wording).
- **[G. E. Moore](g-e-moore.md)** (contemporary). Baldwin (SEP "Moore", §1) reports Moore "actually leading Russell away from the idealist philosophy of McTaggart and others which was then dominant in Britain." (`raw/sep-moore-fall-2024-baldwin-life-ethics-common-sense.md`). Moore, as Pigden quotes him: "“I certainly have been more influenced by [Russell] than any other single philosopher”" (Schilpp 1942: 16). Pigden (§4) on Moore's identification of the right act with the best: "But in Russell’s view this claim is neither analytic nor true." Baldwin (§3): Moore's "conservative form of rule consequentialism, for which he was criticised by Keynes and Russell."
- **[William James](william-james.md)** (earlier contemporary). Legg & Hookway (SEP "Pragmatism", §3): "The kind of passages just noted may seem to support Bertrand Russell’s famous objection that James is committed to the truth of “Santa Claus exists” (Russell 1946, p, 772)." Their assessment: "This is unfair; at best, James is committed to the claim that the happiness that belief in Santa Claus provides is truth-relevant." (`raw/sep-pragmatism-fall-2024-james-truth-russell-peirce.md`).
- **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (teacher). Schultz (SEP "Sidgwick", §1): "If such students as Moore and Russell owed him much," (`raw/sep-sidgwick-fall-2024-life-methods-dualism-reception.md`).
- **[Hegel](hegel.md)** (earlier). Redding (SEP "Hegel", §1): "For Russell, the revolutionary innovations in logic starting in the last decades of the nineteenth century with the work of Frege and Peano had destroyed Hegel’s metaphysics by overturning the Aristotelian logic on which, so Russell claimed, it was based," (`raw/sep-hegel-fall-2024-life-readings-recognition-reception.md`).
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein** (student). Irvine (§3): "(The theory was crucial for influencing Wittgenstein’s theory of the same name.)"; (§8): "Despite the fact that Wittgenstein was less than satisfied with Russell’s Introduction to his Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921),".
- **[Alfred Tarski](tarski.md)** (later), on the hierarchy of languages (Quine 1962, quoted above); **[Graham Priest](priest.md)** (later), on the paradoxes; **[the Vienna Circle](../schools/vienna-circle.md)**, whose minimal consensus followed "the logicism of Frege and Russell" (that page).
- **Ayer and Stevenson** (later). Pigden (preamble): "And Russell’s version of emotivism (which anticipates Ayer’s Language, Truth and Logic (1936) by one year, and Stevenson’s “The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms” (1937) by two)". On the 1922 paper, Pigden says it "anticipates Mackie’s “The Refutation of Morals” by over twenty years" — see [J. L. Mackie](mackie.md).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Irvine (preamble).** "Together with G.E. Moore, Russell is generally recognized as one of the founders of modern analytic philosophy." "In the public mind, he was famous as much for his evangelical atheism as for his contributions to technical philosophy."
- **Ayer, as Irvine quotes him (§1):** "As Ayer writes, “The popular conception of a philosopher as one who combines universal learning with the direction of human conduct was more nearly satisfied by Bertrand Russell than by any other philosopher of our time” (1972a, 127)."
- **For his inclusion in the canon (Irvine §8).** "Advocates of Russell’s inclusion in the canon remind readers that few have done more to advance both formal logic and analytic philosophy." Strawson: "As P.F. Strawson concludes, Russell’s influence “on the philosophy of his and our time has perhaps been greater than that of any other single individual” (Strawson 1984, 104)."
- **Against (Irvine §8).** "Critics of Russell’s inclusion in the canon, or at least of his canonization, remind readers of Russell’s early enthusiasm for British imperialism (1967, 134) and of his controversial comments about eugenics and race (1929, 259, 266)." "Others have noted his apparent early antisemitism and his advocacy of a preemptive nuclear war against the Soviet Union following World War II (Hook 1976, Stone 1981, Perkins 1994, Blitz 2002)." Russell's side: "On the issue of a preemptive war, Russell himself later denied he had ever advocated such a course of action." Clark's: "“If the suggestion that he deliberately tried to conceal his earlier views is repugnant, the record does not really allow any other conclusion to be drawn” (Clark 1975, 530)."
- **Wittgenstein and Stone (Irvine §8).** "Monk relates Wittgenstein’s humorous suggestion that all of Russell’s books should be bound in two colours, “those dealing with mathematical logic in red – and all students of philosophy should read them; those dealing with ethics and politics in blue – and no one should be allowed to read them” (Monk 2000, 278)." Against such caricatures, Stone (2003, 85) as quoted: "“Whatever one thinks of Russell’s politics, he was one of the few public figures in the west to stand against capitalism without succumbing to illusions about Stalinist Russia. If for no other reason than this, Russell deserves some credit for his political instincts” (2003, 85)."
- **On the type theory.** Irvine (§2): "Even so, critics claimed that the axiom was simply too ad hoc to be justified philosophically." (the axiom of reducibility); "The theory thus admits of two versions, the “simple theory” of 1903 and the “ramified theory” of 1908. Both versions of the theory came under attack: the simple theory for being too weak, the ramified theory for being too strong." Church (1932), as quoted by Deutsch & Marshall (SEP "Alonzo Church"): "Rather than adopt the method of Russell for avoiding the paradoxes of mathematical logic, or that of Zermelo, both of which appear somewhat artificial, we introduce for this purpose, as we have said, a certain restriction on the law of excluded middle" (`raw/sep-church-fall-2024-kleene-rosser-richard.md`; see [Kleene–Rosser paradox](../problems/kleene-rosser-paradox.md)).
- **On the ethics (Pigden).** Neglect: "But though Russell was famous as a moralist and famous as a philosopher, he does not have much of a reputation as a moral philosopher in the more technical sense of the term." Pigden's own verdicts: "Every meta-ethic that he developed seems to be subject to insuperable, objections." and "Russell’s arguments against objectivism are often quite powerful, and one anticipates Gilbert Harman’s, influential argument that objective values can be safely dismissed since they lack explanatory power" (§12). Russell on himself: "I have no difficulty in practical moral judgments, which I find I make on a roughly hedonistic [i.e., utilitarian] basis, but, when it comes to the philosophy of moral judgments, I am impelled in two opposite directions and remain perplexed. (RoE: 165–6/Papers 11: 311)"

## Vocabulary

- *Denoting phrase*: in 1905, phrases such as "a man", "every man" and "the present King of France"; for Russell "A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'." (§A of the raw file). No vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Contradiction*: Russell's word (1903, 1908) for what this implant calls a [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md). *Valid*, when he calls the barber form "not valid" (1918, lecture VII), is said of a modification of the contradiction, not of an argument; the lecture does not say more (raw file context note), so it is not the implant's [validity](../vocabulary/validity.md).
- *Incomplete symbol*: an expression with meaning only in context (Irvine §4; Russell 1918 lecture VII on classes).
- *Acquaintance*: direct awareness "without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths" (1912, ch. V); compare [knowledge](../vocabulary/knowledge.md).
- *Subjectivism* / *the subjectivity of moral values*: his name for the view now called emotivism or expressivism (Pigden §1).

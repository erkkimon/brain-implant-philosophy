## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "Of these parents I was born at Porbandar, otherwise known as Sudamapuri, on the 2nd October, 1869." (Autobiography I.1). The chronology (1869 October 2) adds that he was born "of a Bania (Vaishya or trading caste) family at Porbunder, Kathiawar".
- **Law and London:** sailed for England 4 September 1888; "Called to the Bar." 10 June 1891 (chronology).
- **South Africa:** April 1893, "Leaves for South Africa, being engaged by a Muslim firm for legal work." 10 January 1908: "Adopts word : ‘Satyagraha’ in place of ‘Passive Resistance’." (chronology).
- **Hind Swaraj:** 13 November 1909, "Leaves England for South Africa." — and, the same row, writes Hind Swaraj on board the s. s. Kildonan Castle (chronology). Gandhi's 1921 "Reply to Critics" (1922 ed.) says instead: "It was written in 1908 during my return voyage from London to South Africa in answer to the Indian school of violence, and its prototype in South Africa." Kohn & Reddy (SEP "Colonialism" §5) give "published in 1909". Both dates are reported; neither is chosen here.
- **India:** reached India 9 January 1915; founded the Satyagraha (Sabarmati) Ashram at Ahmedabad 20 May 1915; Dandi salt march begun 12 March 1930; founded *Harijan* 11 February 1933 (chronology).
- **Autobiography:** Desai (Translator's Preface, 1940): "The first edition of Gandhiji’s Autobiography was published in two volumes. Vol. I in 1927 and Vol. II in 1929."
- **Death:** 30 January 1948, "Is assassinated on way to evening prayer." (chronology).
- **Name:** the sources read here write "Mahatma Gandhi" (Kohn & Reddy §5; Furtak §4) or "the Mahatma" (Ambedkar 1936); Fiala writes "Mohandas Gandhi".

## Problems addressed

- **[Just war](../problems/just-war.md) — pacifism.** Fiala (§2.1): "There is an open question about how “absolute” Gandhi was in his pacifism (Fiala 2022a), but it is clear that he is often viewed as a paradigmatic figure of what we Allen calls “absolute nonviolence” (Allen 2022)." Merton, as Fiala quotes him: "as Gandhi saw, the fully consistent practice of nonviolence demands a solid metaphysical basis both in being and in God" (Merton 1971, 209). The other reading, in Delmas & Brownlee (SEP "Civil Disobedience" §3.3), is Mantena's, and the verb is the entry authors':
  "For her part, Mantena debunks the common understanding of Gandhi and King as committed in principle and absolutely to non-violence, showing that their endorsement of non-violence reflected concerns of political efficacy."
  "In Gandhi’s view, violence would cultivate the wrong kind of independence for India and breed the wrong kind of polity, amounting to a mere change of personnel in a violent state and generating unstable conditions." (Mantena 2012, DOI [10.1017/s000305541200010x](https://doi.org/10.1017/s000305541200010x), not read here.) Structural note (G4): the two readings are listed side by side, unranked.
- **Ahimsa and the [moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).** Fiala (§2.2): "Gandhi, for example, extended ahimsa maximally to include avoiding harm to sentient beings." Gandhi (Autobiography IV.39): "Ahimsa is a comprehensive principle. We are helpless mortals caught in the conflagration of himsa." Fiala (§2.1) quotes the continuation: "A votary of ahimsa (nonviolence) therefore remains true to this faith if the spring of all his actions is compassion, if he shuns to the best of his ability the destruction of the tiniest creature, tries to save it, and thus incessantly strives to be free from the deadly coil of himsa."
- **Satyagraha and civil disobedience.** Hind Swaraj ch. XVII ("Passive Resistance"): "Passive resistance is a method of securing rights by personal suffering; it is the reverse of resistance by arms." "If I do not obey the law and accept the penalty for its breach, I use soul-force. It involves sacrifice of self." The name (Autobiography IV.26): "As a result Maganlal Gandhi coined the word ‘Sadagraha’ (Sat=truth, Agraha=firmness) and won the prize. But in order to make it clearer I changed the word to ‘Satyagraha’ which has since become current in Gujarati as a designation for the struggle." Why not "passive resistance": the term "was supposed to be a weapon of the weak, that it could be characterized by hatred, and that it could finally manifest itself as violence," (IV.26).
  Fasts: Delmas & Brownlee (§1.2) report that Gandhi "thought that hunger strikes were coercive and violent but that fasts of moral pressure and Satyagrahic fasts were persuasive and non-violent (Sharp 2012a, 134, 151, 262)". No problem page on civil disobedience exists yet.
- **Means and ends.** Hind Swaraj ch. XVI ("Brute Force"): "The means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree; and there is just the same inviolable connection between the means and the end as there is between the seed and the tree." No problem page yet; the [dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md) page treats a neighbouring question, and no read source ties Gandhi to it.
- **Swaraj (self-rule) and civilisation.** Ch. XIV: "It is Swaraj when we learn to rule ourselves. It is therefore in the palm of our hands." Ch. XIII: "Civilization is that mode of conduct which points out to man the path of duty." Ch. XVII: "Real home-rule is possible only where passive resistance is the guiding force of the people. Any other rule is foreign rule." In 1921 (Reply to Critics): "The booklet is a severe condemnation of 'modern civilization.' It was written in 1908. My conviction is deeper to-day than ever." Kohn & Reddy (§5) summarise: "The text provides an account of British domination in India, an analysis of the meaning of swaraj (self-rule), a discussion of political strategy, and a critique of Western civilization."
- **Truth and God** (Gandhi's own religious claims, reported as his). Autobiography, Introduction: "But I worship God as Truth only. I have not yet found Him, but I am seeking after Him." "Farewell": "My uniform experience has convinced me that there is no other God than Truth." and "a perfect vision of Truth can only follow a complete realization of Ahimsa."
- **Religion and politics.** "Farewell": "those who say that religion has nothing to do with politics do not know what religion means." Introduction: "All that I do by way of speaking and writing, and all my ventures in the political field, are directed to this same end." (the end being "to see God face to face, to attain Moksha").
- **Caste and untouchability** — see Ambedkar under In dialogue with.

## In dialogue with

- **[Jesus of Nazareth](jesus.md)** (earlier; the Sermon on the Mount). Autobiography I.20: "But the New Testament produced a different impression, especially the Sermon on the Mount which went straight to my heart. I compared it with the Gita." "young mind tried to unify the teaching of the Gita, the Light of Asia and the Sermon on the Mount." Fiala (§5.2): "Gandhi and King both claim that one of the most important ideas underlying this sort of pacifism is love, especially the disinterested brotherly love that is described in the Greek New Testament using the word agape."
- **Leo Tolstoy** (earlier; no page). Autobiography II.15: "Tolstoy's The Kingdom of God Is Within You overwhelmed me. It left an abiding impression on me." Chronology: "Writes to Tolstoy regarding Passive Resistance movement." (1 October 1909); "Tolstoy replies: question of Passive Resistance of greatest importance not only for India but for humanity." (8 May 1910); "Founds Tolstoy Farm." Fiala (SEP "Anarchism" §3.1): "Gandhi was influenced by Tolstoy and the anarchists." Gandhi on himself (1916, as Fiala quotes): "I myself am an anarchist, but of another type".
- **John Ruskin and Raychandbhai** (earlier; no pages). Autobiography II.1: "Three moderns have left a deep impress on my life and captivated me: Raychandbhai by his living contact; Tolstoy by his book, The Kingdom of God Is Within You; and Ruskin by his Unto this Last."
- **Henry David Thoreau** (earlier; no page). Hind Swaraj's appendix "Some Authorities" lists "On the Duty of Civil Disobedience" and "Life Without Principle". Fiala (§5.2): "Gandhi was inspired by Thoreau and by Tolstoy; and Gandhi in turn inspired Western pacifists such as Albert Einstein, Bertrand Russell, and Martin Luther King Jr." Furtak (SEP "Thoreau" §4) on Thoreau's noncompliance: "articulating a principle of noncompliance that would inspire the philosophically informed nonviolent resistance of Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King, among others."
- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** (earlier). The same appendix lists "Defence and Death of Socrates"--From _Plato_; no read source analyses the link.
- **B. R. Ambedkar** (contemporary; no page). Ambedkar's undelivered 1936 address, published as *Annihilation of Caste*, drew Gandhi's review in *Harijan* (11 and 18 July 1936), printed by Ambedkar as Appendix I. Gandhi: "Dr. Ambedkar is a challenge to Hinduism." "Caste has nothing to do with religion." "The law of Varna teaches us that we have each one of us to earn our bread by following the ancestral calling." "And there is nothing in the law of Varna to warrant a belief in untouchability."
  Ambedkar's reply (Appendix II): "From a perusal of his review of my speech it is clear that the Mahatma completely dissents from the views I have expressed on the subject of Caste." "It will be noticed that the questions raised by the Mahatma are absolutely beside the point and show that the main argument of the speech was lost upon him." (§II); "Why does the Mahatma cling to the theory of every one following his or her ancestral calling?" (§VIII). Text: 3rd ed. 1945, [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.71655), OCR corrections marked in the raw file.
- **Martin Luther King Jr.** (later; no page). "Pilgrimage to Nonviolence" (*Christian Century*, 13 April 1960): "Christ furnished the spirit and motivation while Gandhi furnished the method." Fiala (§3.1): "Although the roots of this approach can be found in the long history of pacifism from Jesus onward, the Gandhi-King approach both clarified the basic principles of nonviolent resistance and successfully put these principles into action in the Indian struggle for self-determination and in the American civil rights movement." (Fiala's assessment.)
- **Frantz Fanon** (later; no page). Delmas & Brownlee (§2.7): "revolutionary activists and thinkers like Frantz Fanon (2004, ch. 1) and Gandhi disagreed about the effectiveness of violence in emancipatory struggles, but not about its justifiability, as Karuna Mantena (2018, 83–4) has shown" ("has shown" is their wording).
- **Muslim contemporaries.** Fiala (§5.2): "In the South Asian context of the 20th Century, Muslims such as Maulana Abul Kalam Azad and Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan joined Gandhi in affirming the power of nonviolence."

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's. Favourable and
critical ones are interleaved, unranked.

- **Encyclopedists.** Fiala (§5.2): "Mohandas Gandhi is perhaps the most famous adherent of ahimsa of the last century." Kohn & Reddy (§5): "Another seminal critic of colonialism is Mahatma Gandhi." Delmas & Brownlee (§1) list him among "the most famous civil disobedients".
- **King (1960):** "The whole Gandhian concept of satyagraha ( satya is truth which equals love, and graha is force; satyagraha thus means truth-force or love-force) was profoundly significant to me."
- **Orwell (1949), critical:** "Anarchists and pacifists, in particular, have claimed him for their own, noticing only that he was opposed to centralism and State violence and ignoring the other-worldly, anti-humanist tendency of his doctrines." "No doubt alcohol, tobacco and so forth are things that a saint must avoid, but sainthood is also a thing that human beings must avoid." On totalitarian states: "It is difficult to see how Gandhi’s methods could be applied in a country where opponents of the regime disappear in the middle of the night and are never heard of again." "Applied to foreign politics, pacifism either stops being pacifist or becomes appeasement."
  He reports, from Louis Fischer's *Gandhi and Stalin*, that "Gandhi’s view was that the German Jews ought to commit collective suicide, which ‘would have aroused the world and the people of Germany to Hitler’s violence’." Orwell continues: "After the war he justified himself: the Jews had been killed anyway, and might as well have died significantly." (Orwell's report of Fischer's report of a 1938 answer; Gandhi's text not read here.)
- **Orwell (1949), favourable:** "I have never been able to feel much liking for Gandhi, but I do not feel sure that as a political thinker he was wrong in the main, nor do I believe that his life was a failure." "His main political objective, the peaceful ending of British rule, had after all been attained." His last sentence ends: "but regarded simply as a politician, and compared with the other leading political figures of our time, how clean a smell he has managed to leave behind!"
- **Ambedkar (1937), critical**, on why he replied: "This I have done not because what he has said is so weighty as to deserve a reply but because to many a Hindu he is an oracle, so great that when he opens his lips it is expected that the argument must close and no dog must bark." Gandhi's side of the exchange is under In dialogue with.
- **Efficacy objection and reply (Fiala §6).** Objection, as Fiala reports critics: "Critics will argue that Gandhi’s success was made possible by British exhaustion from the World Wars; and they will argue that the success of Martin Luther King Jr. was made possible by the threat of violence from radicals such as the Black Panthers." "Moreover, critics will argue that Gandhi and King were successful because their opponents were for the most part sympathetic to their cause from the beginning." Obama (2009, per Fiala):
  "He said, “there is nothing weak, nothing passive, nothing naive in the creed and lives of Gandhi and King.”"
  "A nonviolent movement could not have halted Hitler’s armies." Reply, as Fiala gives it: "The examples of King and Gandhi may in fact require careful historical analysis; and different historical circumstances will require different sorts of nonviolent action." "But, for the pacifist, history shows us the horror of war; and the success of Gandhi and King reminds us that there is an alternative."
- **Reappraisals.** Delmas & Brownlee (§1): "several scholars have reassessed the complex legacy of Thoreau and Gandhi to the civil disobedience tradition, in order to both show the misappropriation of their writings on political resistance and to call for a reappropriation and appreciation of their visions (Mantena 2012; Hanson 2021; Livingston 2018; Scheuerman 2018, ch. 1, 4)."

## Vocabulary

- *Satyagraha*: Gandhi's coinage from Maganlal Gandhi's "Sadagraha" (Sat=truth, Agraha=firmness) (Autobiography IV.26); Kohn & Reddy: "While the literal meaning is closer to “holding on to truth,” the term is often used to describe non-violent resistance or civil disobedience."; King: "truth-force or love-force"; Orwell: "‘firmness in the truth’". In Hind Swaraj (1909/1922) Gandhi still writes "passive resistance" and "soul-force" for it.
- *Ahimsa*: nonviolence, non-harm; Kohn & Reddy: "the Hindu concept ahimsa , or avoiding harm". *Himsa*: violence, harm (Fiala's gloss).
- *Swaraj*: "self-rule" (Kohn & Reddy). In the 1921 Reply to Critics Gandhi distinguishes the Swaraj "pictured" in Hind Swaraj, for which he works "individually", from "Parliamentary Swaraj in accordance with the wishes of the people of India", the aim of his "corporate activity".
- *Brahmacharya*: Fiala: "self-renunciation ( brahmacharya )"; *Sarvodaya*: "the welfare of all" (IV.18). No vocabulary pages yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "Dewey was born in Burlington, Vermont on October 20, 1859 to Archibald Dewey, a merchant, and Lucina Rich Dewey." (Hildebrand §1).
- **Education:** "Dewey entered the University of Vermont at fifteen and graduated in 1879 at nineteen." At Johns Hopkins from 1882 "his study included logic with Charles S. Peirce (which Dewey found too “mathematical”, and did not pursue), the history of philosophy with George Sylvester Morris, and physiological and experimental psychology with Granville Stanley Hall" (§1).
- **Dissertation:** "In 1884, two years after matriculating, Dewey graduated with a dissertation criticizing Kant from an Idealist position (“The Psychology of Kant”); it remains lost." (§1).
- **Michigan:** "At Michigan, Dewey developed long-term professional relationships with James Hayden Tufts and George Herbert Mead." There "he taught James’ Principles of Psychology (1890), and labeled his own view “experimental idealism” (1894a, The Study of Ethics, EW4: 264)." (§1).
- **Chicago, 1894–1904:** "Dubbed the “Chicago School” by William James, Dewey, Tufts, Angell, Mead and several others developed “psychological functionalism”." "At Chicago, Dewey founded The Laboratory School, a site to test psychological and educational theories." (§1).
- **Columbia:** "In 1904, conflicts related to the Laboratory School lead Dewey to resign his Chicago positions and move to the philosophy department at Columbia University in New York City." "Dewey retired from Columbia in 1930, going on to produce eleven more books." (§1).
- **Lectures:** "in 1922, he inaugurated the Paul Carus Lectures (revised and published as Experience and Nature, 1925), gave the 1928 Gifford Lectures (revised and published as The Quest for Certainty, 1929), and gave the 1933–34 Terry Lectures at Yale (published as A Common Faith, 1934a)." (§1).
- **Public life:** the Chronology lists "1937 Chair of the Trotsky Commission, Mexico City"; Anderson (§6): "Dewey was a founding member of the NAACP and the ACLU."
- **Output:** "forty books and approximately seven hundred articles in over one hundred and forty journals." (Hildebrand §1).
- **Death:** "John Dewey died of pneumonia in his home in New York City on June 1, 1952." (§1).
- **His own label:** "Dewey’s “cultural naturalism” (which he favored over “pragmatism” and “instrumentalism”) is a critique and reconstruction of philosophy within the ambit of a Darwinian worldview (Lamont 1961; MW4: 3)." (Hildebrand, preamble).

## Problems addressed

- **What ethics is for** (no problem page yet). Anderson (preamble): "Dewey’s ethics replaces the goal of identifying an ultimate end or supreme ethical principle with the goal of identifying a method for improving our value judgments." Hildebrand (§6): "Dewey argued for a more experimental approach. Rather than an ultimate explanatory account of moral life, ethics should describe intelligent methods for dealing with novel and morally perplexing situations. No ultimate values should be stipulated or sought."
  Dewey (*HNC* IV.I): "Because there is no final recipe by which to decide this question all moral judgment is experimental and subject to revision by its issue."
  The 1908 *Ethics* Preface (joint): "On the one hand, as against the _a priori_ claims of both individualism and socialism, the need of the hour seems to us to be the application of methods of more deliberate analysis and experiment."
- **How value judgments are tested** (metaethics; no problem page yet). Anderson (§2.4): "Similarly, we test value judgments by acting on them and seeing if we value the consequences in the way the judgment predicted." and "Even the best confirmed value judgments can hold only provisionally." The basis: "Dewey’s metaethics is grounded in the distinction between valuing and evaluation (also denoted as “prizing” vs. “appraising”, or “esteeming” vs. “estimating,”)." (§2.1). Her summary of the warrant: "The conditions of warrant for value judgments lie in human conduct, not in any a priori fixed reference point outside of conduct, such as God’s commands, Platonic Forms, pure reason, or nature. Dewey offers a naturalistic metaethic of value judgments, grounded in developmental and social psychology." (preamble).
- **Means and ends** (*HNC* 1922; *Theory of Valuation* 1939). Dewey: "Means and ends are two names for the same reality. The terms denote not a division in reality but a distinction in judgment." (*HNC* I.II); "End" names the series of acts "taken collectively", "Means" the same series "taken distributively" (I.II, in the raw file).
  On ends-in-view: "Consequently ends arise and function within action. They are not, as current theories too often imply, things lying beyond activity at which the latter is directed." "Ends are foreseen consequences which arise in the course of activity and which are employed to give activity added meaning and to direct its further course." "In a strict sense an end-in-view is a _means_ in present action; present action is not a means to a remote end." (*HNC* III.VI).
  Anderson (§3): "He argued that the character and value of means and ends was reciprocally determined." and, citing *Theory of Valuation*: "It is irrational to take one’s end as fixed before investigating the costs of the means and the consequences of achieving the end (TV 214)." (§3.1; Anderson's statement of Dewey's view).
- **Intrinsic value** (no problem page yet). Anderson (§2.5): "He rejected any conception of intrinsic value as some kind of existence or property that has value in itself, regardless of context, which is the object of practice to bring about, realize, or conform to." Zimmerman & Bradley (§3) list "Another type of metaphysical challenge to intrinsic value stems from the theory of “pragmatism,” especially in the form advanced by John Dewey [1859–1952] (see Dewey 1922)."
- **Deliberation.** Dewey (*HNC* III.III): "We begin with a summary assertion that deliberation is a dramatic rehearsal (in imagination) of various competing possible lines of action." and "Deliberation is an experiment in finding out what the various lines of possible action are really like."
- **The three types of moral theory.** Anderson (§4): "Dewey declined to offer substantive answers to the traditional questions posed by these theories, arguing that no fixed ends or moral rules could be adequate in a world of constant change and plural and conflicting values." On "Three Independent Factors in Morals" (1930): "Hence, the tension among the three types of moral consideration is permanent and cannot be resolved by reducing one to another or insisting that one automatically overrides the others (TIF)." On the right: "Pragmatism in ethics is often regarded as a form of teleology or consequentialism. Yet Dewey rejected accounts of the right that defined it in terms of promoting the good (E 214–216)." (§4.2). On the good: "Informed desire theories of the good, which define the good in terms of what an individual would desire if fully informed, come closest to Dewey’s own account." (§4.1).
- **Conflict between incompatible ends** (related to [moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md); structural note: Dewey is not among the authors that problem page reports, and the McConnell entry it follows does not name him). In Part II of the 1908 *Ethics* (Dewey's part, Ch. X): "When ends are genuinely incompatible, no common denominator can be found except by deciding what sort of character is most highly prized and shall be given supremacy." and "This is the question finally at stake in any genuinely moral situation: What shall the agent _be_? What sort of a character shall he assume?"
- **[The justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md)** and responsibility (*HNC* I.I, IV.III). Dewey: "Causes for an act always exist, but causes are not excuses." "Questions of causation are physical, not moral except when they concern future consequences." "Our entire tradition regarding punitive justice tends to prevent recognition of social partnership in producing crime; it falls in with a belief in metaphysical free-will." (I.I) and "The reference in blame and every unfavorable judgment is prospective, not retrospective." (IV.III). Anderson (§4.3): "This forward-looking view of praise and blame enabled Dewey to avoid the problem of free will in its connection with responsibility." (Anderson's reading.)
- **Education and growth** (*Democracy and Education*, 1916). "Our net conclusion is that life is development, and that developing, growing, is life." "Since growth is the characteristic of life, education is all one with growing; it has no end beyond itself." (Ch. 4). "If we are willing to conceive education as the process of forming fundamental dispositions, intellectual and emotional, toward nature and fellow men, philosophy may even be defined as the general theory of education." (Ch. 24). Hildebrand (§6): "The only value Dewey celebrated as (something like) ultimate was “growth”."
- **Democracy.** "A democracy is more than a form of government; it is primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience." (*DE* Ch. 7). Anderson (§4.4): "Democracy, in Dewey’s view, is the means by which we practice intelligent moral inquiry together, seeking solutions to the problems we face together (PP)." and (§6): "Experimentalism in ethics leads to a democratic political philosophy."
- **Inquiry.** Legg & Hookway (§4.2): "Dewey sees inquiry as beginning with a problem; it aims for ‘the controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate situation into one that is so determinate in its constituent distinctions and relations as to convert the elements of the original situation into a unified whole’ (ED2: 171)."

## In dialogue with

- **[William James](william-james.md)** (earlier contemporary). Hildebrand (preamble) names Dewey "one of American pragmatism’s early founders, along with Charles Sanders Peirce and William James". On the *Principles*: "The Principles’ emphatically biological conception of mind, Dewey recalled, gave his thinking “a new direction and quality” and “worked its way more and more into all my ideas and acted as a ferment to transform old beliefs” (FAE, LW5: 157)." (§2.2). "Following William James, Dewey thought philosophy had become overly technical and intellectualistic, divorced from assessing everyday social conditions and values (FAE, LW5: 157–58)." (preamble). On instinct: "James had already attacked attempts to explain complex, developed behavior by reference to preexisting impulses and instincts (e.g., “Habit”, James 1890: chapter 4); Dewey continued the assault." (§2.3). Legg & Hookway group them on truth: "a more broad-based alethic pluralism (following James and Dewey)" (`raw/sep-pragmatism-fall-2024-james-truth-russell-peirce.md`).
- **Charles Sanders Peirce** (teacher at Johns Hopkins, above). Legg & Hookway (§4.2) quote John E. Smith: "As John E. Smith notes, where ‘Peirce aimed at “fixing” belief, Dewey aimed at “fixing” the situation’ (1978: 98)."
- **G. W. F. Hegel** (earlier). "While scholars still debate the degree to which Dewey’s mature philosophy retained early Hegelian influences, Hegel’s personal influence on Dewey was profound." (Hildebrand §1). Anderson (§6): "Dewey, like Hegel, stressed the ways abstract philosophical doctrines are socially embodied."
- **[Kant](kant.md)** (earlier). The lost 1884 dissertation criticised Kant (Facts). Anderson (§4.2): "Dewey endorsed the “empty formalism” critique of Kant’s Categorical Imperative, insofar as it aspires to reach moral conclusions without presupposing anything to be good."
- **[Mill](mill.md)**, **[Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** and others (earlier). The 1908 Preface: "After the discussions of Kant and Mill, Sidgwick and Green, Martineau and Spencer, it is possible to affirm that there is a place in the moral life for reason and a place for happiness,--a place for duty and a place for valuation." Against the utilitarians and their critics alike, *HNC* III.VI: "The doctrine of fixed ends not only diverts attention from examination of consequences and the intelligent creation of purpose, but, since means and ends are two ways of regarding the same actuality, it also renders men careless in their inspection of existing conditions."
- **[Hume](hume.md)** (earlier, as the alternative the objection names). Anderson (§3) states the objection that Dewey's view "reduces to a form of Humean instrumentalism, in which ends are given by our desires or immediate likings, and the only question is how to satisfy them." (in full under Reception).
- **James H. Tufts, George Herbert Mead, Jane Addams** (colleagues). Tufts co-wrote *Ethics* (Works). "Dewey and his biographer, daughter Jane Dewey, credited Addams with helping him develop his views on democracy, education, and philosophy." (Hildebrand §1).
- **Monroe Beardsley** (later). Zimmerman & Bradley (§3): "This theme has been elaborated by Monroe Beardsley, who attacks the very notion of intrinsic value (Beardsley 1965; cf. Conee 1982)." Beardsley, "Intrinsic Value", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 26 (1965), DOI [10.2307/2105465](https://doi.org/10.2307/2105465) (verified by content negotiation; not read here).
- **Richard Rorty, Hilary Putnam** (later): under Reception. Of Putnam, Legg & Hookway (§5.2): "However, he has written extensively on James, Peirce, and Dewey—often in collaboration with Ruth Anna Putnam—and has provided insightful accounts of what is distinctive about pragmatism, and what can be learned from it (See Putnam 1994a)."

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing.** Hildebrand (preamble): "John Dewey (1859–1952) was one of American pragmatism’s early founders, along with Charles Sanders Peirce and William James, and arguably the most prominent American intellectual for the first half of the twentieth century." and "Dewey developed extensive and often systematic views in ethics, epistemology, logic, metaphysics, aesthetics, and philosophy of religion." Legg & Hookway (§6.4), on education: "The giant figure in philosophy of education is of course Dewey, who pioneered and established it as a separate sphere of study when he first assumed the chair in Philosophy at University of Chicago in 1894."
- **The standard objection to the instrumental theory, and the reply.** Anderson (§3): "The standard objection to Dewey’s instrumental theory of value judgments is that it concerns the value of things as means only, and not as ends." On her statement of it, the theory otherwise needs a summum bonum "or Dewey’s theory reduces to a form of Humean instrumentalism, in which ends are given by our desires or immediate likings, and the only question is how to satisfy them." Her account of the reply: "Dewey’s reply to this objection goes to the heart of his moral philosophy. He argued that the character and value of means and ends was reciprocally determined." On character and motive she judges that his view keeps what intention-based moralities hold: "This is the truth that moralities of intention grasp, which narrowly consequentialist theories do not (E 173–5, 286–9)." (§3.4; Anderson's assessment.)
- **Classification.** Anderson (§4.2) reports that "Pragmatism in ethics is often regarded as a form of teleology or consequentialism" and argues that Dewey rejected that reading (Problems addressed). Legg & Hookway (§6.2): "Dewey also sought to steer ethics between the traditional poles of an objectivism derived from some kind of human-transcendent authority, and a subjectivism derived from individual preference."
- **Scope of his ethics.** Anderson (§6): "Thus, in contrast with his voluminous political commentaries, Dewey published very little on personal “applied ethics.”"
- **Bernstein (1961) on the metaphysics.** Hildebrand (§3.8): "Richard Bernstein (1961) seized on this issue, and claimed that Dewey harbored two irreconcilable strains, a “metaphysical strain” and a “phenomenological strain”, but failed to sufficiently account for them with his “principle of continuity”." Bernstein, *John Dewey's Metaphysics of Experience*, *Journal of Philosophy* 58 (1961): 5–14, DOI [10.2307/2023564](https://doi.org/10.2307/2023564) (verified by content negotiation; not read here).
- **Rorty and Seigfried against the metaphysics; the defenders.** Hildebrand (§3.8): "Richard Rorty and Charlene Haddock Seigfried argued that Dewey’s critique of traditional metaphysics was as far as he should have gone; his further efforts diverted him from more important ethical work (Seigfried 2001a, 2004) or plunged him into foundationalist projects previously disavowed (“Dewey’s Metaphysics” in Rorty 1977)." The other side: "Defenders argue that Dewey’s genuinely new approach to metaphysics avoids old problems while contributing something salutary to culture at large (Myers 2020, Garrison 2005, Boisvert 1998a, Alexander 2020)." Rorty's essay: *New Studies in the Philosophy of John Dewey*, ed. Cahn (1977), pp. 45–74, reprinted in *Consequences of Pragmatism* (1982) (per Hildebrand's Bibliography; not read here).
- **Rorty on "experience".** Legg & Hookway (§4.3): "On this basis, Rorty declares that ‘Dewey should have dropped the term “experience” rather than redefining it… He should have agreed with Peirce that a great gulf divides sensation and cognition, [and] decided that cognition was possible only for language users…’ (Rorty 1998: 297)." (*Truth and Progress*, Cambridge University Press, 1998). The reply they report: "This neopragmatist neglect of one of classical pragmatism’s central concepts has become a major point of contention on the part of many New Pragmatists (e.g. Koopman 2007; Levine 2010, 2012; Sachs 2015)." Their contrast: "Where Peirce and Dewey—and even perhaps James—were engaged in working out systematic philosophical visions, Rorty treated ‘pragmatism’ as a more critical or therapeutic philosophical project." (§5.2).

## Vocabulary

- *End-in-view*: a foreseen consequence that directs present activity; "In a strict sense an end-in-view is a _means_ in present action" (*HNC* III.VI). Not a final end in the sense of the [vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md) branch; no entry yet.
- *Valuing / evaluation* ("prizing" / "appraising"): Anderson §2.1 (above); Dewey's "valuation" covers both.
- *Habit*: "is an acquired predisposition to ways or modes of response, not to particular acts" (*HNC*, MW14: 32, as Hildebrand §2.5 quotes it) — wider than the everyday sense.
- *Growth*: the one value Hildebrand (§6) reports Dewey treating as "(something like) ultimate"; *DE* Ch. 4.
- *Experimental*: in "all moral judgment is experimental" (*HNC* IV.I), revisable by its outcome in conduct, not laboratory experiment.
- *Cultural naturalism*: the name Hildebrand (preamble) reports Dewey "favored over “pragmatism” and “instrumentalism”".

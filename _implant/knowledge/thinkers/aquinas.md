## Facts a claim depends on

- **Born** near Aquino, around 1225: "Thomas Aquinas was born near Aquino, halfway between Rome and Naples, around the year 1225." (Pasnau, SEP §1.1). The preamble gives "(ca. 1225–1274)".
- **Order**: Dominican. "Joining the order at the age of nineteen, he was assigned to Paris for further study" (SEP §1.1).
- **Teacher**: Albert the Great, at Cologne from 1248: "Thomas spent three years in Paris, studying philosophy, and then was sent to Cologne, in 1248, under the supervision of Albert the Great." (SEP §1.1). Pasnau reports the shared view: "It was Albert’s firm conviction, which became Aquinas’s own, that the Christian faith could only benefit from a profound engagement with philosophy and science." (§1.1).
- **Posts**: master of theology at Paris, 1256–1259 ("From 1256 until 1259, he held the Dominican chair of theology in Paris, preaching, lecturing on the Bible, and presiding over various philosophical and theological disputations."); Orvieto and Rome in the 1260s; "In 1268, Aquinas was asked to return to Paris for an unusual second term as master of theology." (SEP §1.1); Naples from 1272.
- **Died** 7 March 1274 at Fossanova: "A few months after that he died, in the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, on March 7, 1274." (SEP §1.1).
- **Reliability of the biography**, per Pasnau: "This chronology of Aquinas’s life is fairly well attested. There are also many colorful stories about his life, many of which stem from testimony given during his canonization inquiry in 1319. The authenticity of these stories is unknowable." (SEP §1.1).
- **Canonized** 18 July 1323 ("This process culminated in his canonization on July 18, 1323."); named a doctor of the Church in 1567: "In 1567, Pope Pius V numbered Aquinas among the doctors of the Church, a title previously reserved for the ancient Church fathers." (SEP §9).

## Problems addressed

- **[Just war](../problems/just-war.md)** — *ST* II-II q. 40 a. 1. Three conditions: "I answer that, In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary. First, the authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged." Then: "Secondly, a just cause is required, namely that those who are attacked, should be attacked because they deserve it on account of some fault." And: "Thirdly, it is necessary that the belligerents should have a rightful intention, so that they intend the advancement of good, or the avoidance of evil." (respondeo; `raw/aquinas-st-2-2-q40-a1-and-augustine-contra-faustum-22-74-city-of-god-19-7-war.md`). The objection he answers cites Matthew 26:52, "All that take the sword shall perish with the sword." (obj. 1). Finnis (SEP §7): "Aquinas regards as legitimate ground for going to war not only the defence of this or another political community, but also the purpose of justly punishing and/or securing compensation." Moseley (IEP "Just War Theory" §1) places him in the tradition: "the most systematic exposition in the Western tradition and one that still attracts attention was outlined by Saint Thomas Aquinas in the 13th century." (`raw/iep-just-war-theory-moseley-ad-bellum-in-bello-post-bellum.md`).
- **Self-defence and double effect** — *ST* II-II q. 64 a. 7, see [double-effect conditions](../arguments/double-effect-conditions.md). "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention." With a proportion clause: "And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end." Intended killing is reserved to public authority: "it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority" (respondeo; `raw/aquinas-st-2-2-q64-a7-self-defense-two-effects.md`). Who credits him, and how:
  - McIntyre (SEP "Doctrine of Double Effect" §1): "Thomas Aquinas is credited with introducing the principle of double effect in his discussion of the permissibility of self-defense in the Summa Theologica (II-II, Qu. 64, Art.7)." (`raw/sep-double-effect-and-doing-allowing-fall-2024-trolley.md`).
  - Mangan (1949, p. 51), of Aquinas enunciating the principle: "he seems to have been the first to do so." He reports a dissent: "Vicente M. Alonso, S.J., concludes that St. Thomas held merely that the killing of an unjust aggressor may be willed as a means but not as an end in itself." (p. 45; `raw/mangan-1949-historical-analysis-principle-of-double-effect.md`).
  - Finnis (SEP §3.4.1) calls the article "the locus classicus for what later became known, unhappily, as the “principle” of double effect" and states its "real core" as a difference between acts "specified by intention" and behaviour chosen "with foresight that it will (probably or even certainly) kill as a side-effect". He also reports: "Aquinas wavers between suggesting that the use of lethal public force, e.g. in capital punishment, intends (“is referred to”) justice rather than killing, and plainly accepting that in such cases death is indeed intended."
  - Fiala (SEP "Pacifism" §4), on the use made of the article: "This idea is derived in the Christian tradition from Aquinas, who holds that a single act may have two effects." His own reading of its scope: "It is significant that Aquinas does not expand this discussion to make it permissible to kill an innocent third party." (`raw/sep-pacifism-fall-2024-fiala-absolute-contingent-just-war-pacifism.md`).
  - Through this principle he appears on the [trolley problem](../problems/trolley-problem.md) page as the ancestor of the double-effect family of answers; the cases themselves are Foot's (1967), not his.
- **[The omnipotence paradox](../problems/omnipotence-paradox.md)** — *ST* I q. 25 a. 3. "It remains therefore, that God is called omnipotent because He can do all things that are possible absolutely; which is the second way of saying a thing is possible." And: "Hence it is better to say that such things cannot be done, than that God cannot do them." (respondeo; `raw/aquinas-st-1-q25-a3-omnipotence-contradiction.md`). Hoffman and Rosenkrantz (SEP "Omnipotence" §1): "Yet, Aquinas and Maimonides held the view that this sense of ‘omnipotence’ is incoherent." — the sense being power over "necessary and impossible states of affairs" (`raw/sep-omnipotence-fall-2024-paradox-of-the-stone.md`). The stone is not in his text (raw file, Context).
- **[The Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md)** — Hare (SEP "Religion and Morality" §3): "God's will is not exercised by arbitrary fiat" and "Aquinas held that reason, in knowing these principles, is participating in the eternal law, which is in the mind of God (Summa Theologiae I, q.91. a.2)." (`raw/sep-religion-morality-fall-2024-euthyphro-ashari-voluntarists.md`).
- **Natural law** (no problem page yet) — *ST* I-II q. 94 a. 2: "Hence this is the first precept of law, that "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided." All other precepts of the natural law are based upon this" and "Wherefore according to the order of natural inclinations, is the order of the precepts of the natural law." (respondeo; `raw/aquinas-st-1-2-q94-natural-law-first-precept.md`). The inclinations listed run from self-preservation to this: "thus man has a natural inclination to know the truth about God, and to live in society". On universality (a. 4): "Consequently we must say that the natural law, as to general principles, is the same for all, both as to rectitude and as to knowledge." Detailed conclusions can fail, with his example: "thus formerly, theft, although it is expressly contrary to the natural law, was not considered wrong among the Germans, as Julius Caesar relates (De Bello Gall. vi)." Pasnau's gloss of its place in the scheme: "natural law: the distinctive way rational beings participate in the eternal law (q. 94);" (SEP §8.2).
- **[Foreknowledge and free will](../problems/argument-from-free-will.md)** — the SEP entry (Zagzebski/Hunt, §2.3) reports "the circle analogy" at *SCG* I.66 and that "Boethius and Aquinas also denied premise (1), but on the grounds that God and his beliefs are not in time, a solution that has always had some adherents." (`raw/sep-free-will-foreknowledge-fall-2024-theological-fatalism.md`).
- **[The murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md)** — Mahon (SEP "The Definition of Lying" §2.3) lists "Aquinas 1972" among those for whom lying to a would-be murderer is impermissible (`raw/sep-lying-definition-fall-2024-murderer-and-kant.md`). Finnis (SEP §3.4.3): "Aquinas’ thesis is that lying, properly defined, is always to be excluded as to some extent wrong."
- **[Dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md)** — Coady (SEP §3) names him among absolutists: "Absolutist positions have been explicitly held by many philosophers, notably Augustine, Aquinas, and Kant, and implicitly perhaps by many more." (`raw/sep-dirty-hands-fall-2024-coady-map.md`).
- **[Famine, affluence and morality](../problems/famine-affluence-and-morality.md)** — Singer (1972) quotes *ST* II-II q. 66 a. 7: "whatever a man has in superabundance is owed, of natural right, to the poor for their sustenance." (Dawson tr., per Singer's note; `raw/singer-1972-famine-affluence-and-morality.md`).
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md)** — Wilson (IEP "Animals and Ethics") names him among authors of indirect theories, which "deny animals moral status or equal consideration with humans due to a lack of consciousness, reason, or autonomy." (`raw/iep-animals-and-ethics-wilson-indirect-theories-marginal-cases.md`).
- **Existence of God** (no problem page yet) — Pasnau (SEP §2): "This is the result he gets in his much discussed “five ways” of proving God’s existence (ST 1a 2.3c). Aquinas denies that Anselm’s ontological argument has any force as a proof (ST 1a 2.1 ad 2; SCG I.10–11)."
- **Unjust law** (no problem page yet) — Finnis (SEP §7.5): "If the law purports to require actions that no-one should ever do, it cannot rightly be complied with; one’s moral obligation is not to obey but to disobey: ST I-II q. 96 a. 4."

## Works

Works pages are not yet written ([works](../works/index.md)). Pasnau
(SEP §1.2) sorts the corpus into five kinds: theological *summae*, disputed
questions, *opuscula*, commentaries on Aristotle and others, and biblical
commentaries; "Over a mere two decades of literary activity, Aquinas left behind more than eight million words (eight times more than has survived, for instance, from Aristotle)."

- *Summa Theologiae* (*ST*), four parts (Ia, Ia-IIae, IIa-IIae, IIIa); the tertia pars is complete through q. 90 (SEP §1.2). Pasnau: "The Summa theologiae (ST) generally represents Aquinas’s most considered thought on a given topic" (§1.2). Locator: part, question, article, with objection, respondeo or reply. Read here in the 1920 Dominican translation: I q. 25 a. 3; I-II q. 94 aa. 2, 4; II-II q. 40 a. 1; II-II q. 64 a. 7.
- *Summa contra gentiles* (*SCG*), written with *ST* in the 1260s (SEP §1.1); cited here only through SEP (I.10–11, I.66, III.129).
- Treatise on Law, *ST* I-II qq. 90–108: eternal (q. 93), natural (q. 94), human (qq. 95–97) and divine law (qq. 98–108), per Pasnau (SEP §8.2).
- Leonine edition: *Opera omnia*, ed. Fratres Praedicatores, Rome: Commissio Leonina, 1882– (SEP bibliography); bibliographic only.

## In dialogue with

- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** — Pasnau (SEP preamble): "Like Dante or Michelangelo, Aquinas takes inspiration from antiquity, especially Aristotle, and builds something entirely new." In *ST* I q. 25 a. 3 he cites *Metaph.* v, 17 for the distinction of possibilities (raw file, Context).
- **[Augustine](augustine.md)** — on just war, Aquinas quotes a definition he gives as Augustine's: "A just war is wont to be described as one that avenges wrongs, when a nation or state has to be punished, for refusing to make amends for the wrongs inflicted by its subjects, or to restore what it has seized unjustly." (II-II q. 40 a. 1). The 1920 translators note of a further quotation: "The words quoted are to be found not in St. Augustine's works, but Can. Apud. Caus. xxiii, qu. 1" (same raw file). Pasnau: "Viewed through a theological lens, Aquinas has often been seen as the summit of the Christian tradition that runs back to Augustine and the early Church." (preamble).
- **Albert the Great** — teacher (Facts, above).
- **Robert Kilwardby, Scotus, Ockham** — critics and rivals, per Pasnau: "Later in March 1277, in Oxford, Archbishop Robert Kilwardby condemned a series of theses that explicitly includes those two distinctively Aquinian doctrines." (SEP §9, the unitarian theory of substantial form and prime matter as pure potentiality). "For the quarter century after Aquinas’s death, scholasticism took shape around his Dominican supporters and his Franciscan critics." (§9).
- **Martin Luther, Thomas Hobbes** — as Pasnau quotes them (SEP §9): Luther, "“soon no Thomist, Albertist, Scotist, or Ockhamist will be left in the world, but all will be simple children of God and true Christians” (Oberman 1966, 18–19)."; "By the seventeenth century, Thomas Hobbes and his contemporaries flaunted their scorn for the scholastics, who “converse in questions of matters incomprehensible” (Leviathan viii.27)."
- **Cajetan, Gury, Mangan** — the later double-effect tradition: "Cajetan seems to be the first to apply the principle of the double effect explicitly to the killing of innocent people." (Mangan 1949, pp. 52–54); "In Gury's early editions, the first of which appeared in 1850, the treatment is a complete modern one in brief form." (p. 59).
- **Vitoria and the School of Salamanca** — built on Aquinas in the law of war, per the SEP "School of Salamanca" excerpt (`raw/sep-school-salamanca-fall-2024-vitoria-de-iure-belli.md`, Context).
- **[Elizabeth Anscombe](anscombe.md)** — listed by Pasnau among twentieth-century Thomists: "the twentieth century witnessed a flourishing of Thomisms of all kinds, in scholars like Jacques Maritain, Etienne Gilson, Elizabeth Anscombe, and Alasdair MacIntyre." (SEP §9).
- **Germain Grisez, John Finnis, Martin Rhonheimer vs Ralph McInerny, Anthony Lisska, Steven Long** — the interpretive dispute, set out under Reception.

## Reception

Assessments, each with its owner; readings are listed side by side,
unranked.

- **Standing, per Pasnau (SEP 2022/2024).** "Between antiquity and modernity stands Thomas Aquinas (ca. 1225–1274). The greatest figure of thirteenth-century Europe in the two preeminent sciences of the era, philosophy and theology, he epitomizes the scholastic method of the newly founded universities." (preamble). And: "Aquinas today, viewed as a philosopher, looks not so much like the culmination of an era, but rather like the brilliant beginning of Europe’s philosophical renaissance." (§9). These are the encyclopedia author's assessments.
- **The 1277 condemnations and the 1879 revival, as Pasnau reports them.** "Among the 219 articles condemned in Paris in 1277 are a significant number that seem to implicate Aquinas’s teachings (Hissette 1977; Wippel 1995b)." (§9). Later: "In 1879, Pope Leo XIII called for a revival in the study and teaching of Aquinas." (§9).
- **Natural law — champions and critics, per Pasnau.** "Among its champions, this approach has been celebrated for the way it grounds traditional values in facts about human nature. To its critics, the view looks intellectually dubious twice over: on scientific grounds inasmuch as it makes false claims about the universality of various inclinations (Massey 1999); and philosophically inasmuch as it grounds normativity in metaethics on descriptive facts about human nature." (§8.2). Pasnau's own reply: "Such criticisms, however, miss the philosophical sophistication of the overarching framework." (§8.2). He also notes: "(The literature on the natural law in Aquinas is large and contentious. For a sample see Grisez 1965; Lisska 1996; Murphy 2001; Jensen 2015; Porter 2018." (§8.2).
- **How to read his ethics — two readings side by side.** The SEP entry on his moral philosophy is written by John Finnis, a party to the dispute; it "proceeds on the basis that there is merit in these objections" (§1, raw file).
  - *The reading Finnis calls "standard"* (McInerny, O’Callaghan, Celano and others): "Along with very many other Thomistic commentators, McInerny and O’Callaghan 2005 and Celano 2003 treat Aquinas’ moral philosophy as founded, like his moral theology, upon his determination of what felicitas (= perfecta beatitudo and Aristotle’s eudaimonia) truly is" (§1.2). On is and ought: "There remain, however, a number of contemporary Thomists who deny that such a deduction or inference need be fallacious, and regard Aquinas as postulating some such deduction or inference." (§2). Against Finnis's own reading: "Criticisms of the interpretation of Aquinas’ theory that is proposed in that work can be found in Paterson 2006, Wheatley 2015, Long 2004, and earlier in Lisska 1998 and McInerny 1997. These works argue in various ways that that interpretation denies or neglects the metaphysical foundations of the principles of practical reason that it offers to identify." (§1). These critics' works are held bibliographically only, as SEP lists them.
  - *The Grisez–Finnis–Rhonheimer reading*: "To this standard interpretation other interpreters, such as Grisez, Finnis, and Rhonheimer, object on grounds such as these:" (§1.1). On the first principle: "But Grisez 1963 gave reason to think these abbreviations both exegetically and philosophically unsound." (§2, of "Do good and avoid evil"). Pasnau (SEP §8.2) cites Finnis and Rhonheimer for this claim: "In fact, however, Aquinas’s theory offers something more complex and nuanced, because he thinks the moral law arises not out of brutely innate inclination but out of rational reflection on the good (Finnis 1998 ch. 3; Rhonheimer 1987 [2000])."
- **On double effect.** Finnis's "unhappily" (§3.4.1) and Fiala's scope reading (SEP "Pacifism" §4) are under Problems addressed; Alonso's "means but not as an end" reading against Mangan's "more or less traditional interpretation" is reported by Mangan (1949, pp. 45–46).

## Vocabulary

- *Natural law* — in Aquinas, rational creatures' participation in the eternal law (Pasnau, SEP §8.2; Hare, SEP §3); distinct from human and divine law. No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Praeter intentionem* ("beside the intention") — Finnis, SEP §3.4.1; the Dominican translation's "beside the intention" (II-II q. 64 a. 7). See [double-effect conditions](../arguments/double-effect-conditions.md).
- *Omnipotent* — "possible absolutely", i.e. not implying contradiction (I q. 25 a. 3), narrower than the unrestricted sense Hoffman and Rosenkrantz attribute to Descartes. No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Synderesis* — the intellect's power of grasping the first practical principles (Pasnau, SEP §8.2, citing *ST* 1a 79.12). No vocabulary entry yet.

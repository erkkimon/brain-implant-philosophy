## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates and birthplace:** "Augustine (Aurelius Augustinus) lived from 13 November 354 to 28 August 430. He was born in Thagaste in Roman Africa (modern Souk Ahras in Algeria)." (Tornau §1).
- **Philosophy and Manicheism:** "Augustine tells us that at the age of eighteen Cicero’s (now lost) protreptic dialogue Hortensius enflamed him for philosophy (Confessiones 3.7)" (§4). "Ca. 373 Augustine became a “hearer” (auditor) of Manicheism, a dualistic religion with Persian origins" (§1).
- **Milan, conversion, baptism:** "In 383 he moved to Milan, then the capital of the western half of the Empire, to become a publicly paid professor of rhetoric of the city"; the period of doubt "ended in summer 386, when Augustine converted to ascetic Christianity and gave up both his chair of rhetoric and his further career prospects."; "Augustine was baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387" (§1). His "conversion was greatly furthered by his Neoplatonic readings (ib. 7.13) as well as by the letters of Paul" (§4).
- **Hippo:** "In 391 he was, apparently against his will, ordained a priest in the diocese of the maritime city of Hippo Regius (modern Annaba/Bône in Algeria)." "About five years later (ca. 396) he succeeded the local bishop." (§1).
- **Death:** "Augustine’s life ended when the Vandals besieged Hippo" (§1).
- **Output:** "about 100 books, 300 letters, and 600 sermons have survived." (§2).

## Problems addressed

- **[The problem of evil](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md)** — evil as
  privation (*Confessions* VII; *Enchiridion* 11; *De natura boni*, 399).
  Tornau (§7.5): "At first, he accepted the dualist solution of the Manicheans, which freed God from the responsibility for evil but compromised his omnipotence"; then
  "After having encountered the books of the Platonists, Augustine rejected the existence of an evil substance and endorsed the Neoplatonic view (argued, e.g., in Plotinus, Enneads I.8) that evil is in fact unsubstantial and a privation or corruption of goodness."
  Augustine: "And I sought whence is evil? And sought in an evil way; nor saw I the evil in my very search." (*Confessions* VII.5.7);
  "So long, therefore, as they are, they are good; therefore whatsoever is, is good. That evil, then, which I sought whence it was, is not any substance; for were it a substance, it would be good." (VII.12.18);
  "For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?" (*Enchiridion* 11).
  On moral evil: "And I inquired what iniquity was, and ascertained it not to be a substance, but a perversion of the will, bent aside from You, O God, the Supreme Substance, towards these lower things" (VII.16.22);
  Tornau (§7.5, on *City of God* 12.1–9): "Therefore, an evil will has no “efficient” but only a “deficient” cause, which is none other than the will’s spontaneous defection from God."
  Readings side by side: Calder (SEP "The Concept of Evil" §2.1): "The Neoplatonist theory of evil provides a solution to the problem of evil because if evil is a privation of substance, form, and goodness, then God creates no evil."
  Against it, Calder: "One problem with the privation theory’s solution to the problem of evil is that it provides only a partial solution to the problem of evil since even if God creates no evil we must still explain why God allows privation evils to exist (See Calder 2007a; Kane 1980)."
  and "Pain is a distinct phenomenological experience which is positively bad and not merely not good."
  Jung (*Answer to Job*, Prefatory Note; see [Answer to Job](../works/answer-to-job.md)): "In connection with the discussion of these problems and of the doctrine of Redemption, I criticized the idea of the privatio boni as not agreeing with the psychological findings."
  (The sentence names the doctrine, not Augustine.) Ryan (1983, dissertation abstract) "attempts to answer his objections in light of Christian tradition."
- **Free will as the source of moral evil** (*On Free Choice of the Will*,
  "begun in 388 and completed perhaps as late as 395", Tornau §2).
  Tornau (§7.4): "In De libero arbitrio, free will appears as the condition of possibility of moral goodness and hence as a great good itself; but as it is not an absolute good (which is God alone) but only an intermediate one, it is liable to misuse and, hence, also the source of moral evil"; and
  "By c. 400 CE, Augustine had come to the conclusion that our ability to make choices was seriously impaired by the fallen condition of humankind and that it made little sense to talk about free will without reference to grace."
  On sin and grace: "Augustine therefore defines sin as “the will to keep or pursue something unjustly” (De duabus animabus 15)."; "Original sin transformed our initial ability not to sin into an inability not to sin; grace can restore ability not to sin in this life and will transform it into inability to sin in the next" (§7.4).
- **[Foreknowledge and free will](../problems/argument-from-free-will.md)**
  (*City of God* V.9–10; *On Free Choice of the Will* III.1–4). Augustine
  reports that "Cicero chooses to reject the foreknowledge of future things, and shuts up the religious mind to this alternative, to make choice between two things, either that something is in our own power, or that there is foreknowledge — both of which cannot be true; but if the one is affirmed, the other is thereby denied." (V.9) and answers:
  "Now, against the sacrilegious and impious darings of reason, we assert both that God knows all things before they come to pass, and that we do by our free will whatsoever we know and feel to be done by us only because we will it." (V.9);
  "For a man does not therefore sin because God foreknew that he would sin." (V.10).
  Readings side by side (SEP "Foreknowledge and Free Will", Zagzebski and Hunt, revision 2021):
  "the denial of (9) is arguably the key to the solution proposed by Augustine" (§1; premise (9) is the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, PAP: "(9) If you cannot do otherwise when you do an act, you do not act freely. [Principle of Alternate Possibilities]");
  "Hunt (1999a) argues that this is essentially the solution put forward by Augustine in On Free Choice of the Will III.1–4, though Augustine’s own considered position on free will was not libertarian." (§2.8);
  "The difference is that the Dependence Solution retains PAP by denying the general necessity of the past, while the Augustinian/Frankfurtian approach is to abandon PAP and stick with the necessity of the past." (§2.8).
- **Lying, and [the murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md)**
  (*De mendacio*; not dated in the sources read).
  The question: "There is a great question about Lying, which often arises in the midst of our every day business, and gives us much trouble, that we may not either rashly call that a lie which is not such, or decide that it is sometimes right to tell a lie, that is, a kind of honest, well-meant, charitable lie." (1).
  The definition: "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." (3).
  The refugee case, put to the party that forbids every lie: "suppose a man should seek shelter with you who by your lie may be saved from death?"; their reply:
  "How then can it be said without the greatest perverseness, that to the end one man may have life of the body, it is another man's duty to incur death of the soul?" (9).
  The conclusion of the work: "In these eight kinds, however, a man sins less when he tells a lie, in proportion as he emerges to the eighth: more, in proportion as he diverges to the first."
  "But whoever shall think there is any sort of lie that is not sin, will deceive himself foully, while he deems himself honest as a deceiver of other men." (42).
  How others place him: Mahon (§2.3) lists "Augustine 1952" among those for whom lying to a would-be murderer is "impermissible, as some absolutist deontologists maintain", opposite consequentialists and moderate deontologists (Constant, Mill, Sidgwick, Bok);
  Timmermann (*Kant and the Supposed Right to Lie*, 2025, DOI [10.1017/9781108992435](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108992435), publisher's abstract) names "the history of the example (which was also discussed by, amongst others, Augustine, Fichte and Johann David Michaelis)".
  Mahon (§2.4): "For Simple Non-Deceptionists (Augustine 1952 (cf. Griffiths 2003, 31); Aquinas 1952; Shibles 1985), there is nothing more to lying than making an untruthful statement."
  and "This position is not defended by contemporary philosophers." Mahon (§1.4) also cites Augustine for the "altruistic lie" (Fallis 2009, 50; cf. Augustine 1952, 57).
  Coady (SEP "Dirty Hands" §3) lists him among absolutists: "Absolutist positions have been explicitly held by many philosophers, notably Augustine, Aquinas, and Kant, and implicitly perhaps by many more." (see [Dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md)).
- **[Just war](../problems/just-war.md)** (*Contra Faustum* XXII.74, ca. 400;
  *City of God* XIX.7; Letter 138). Augustine: "The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power, and such like; and it is generally to punish these things, when force is required to inflict the punishment, that, in obedience to God or some lawful authority, good men undertake wars" (XXII.74);
  "For it is the wrongdoing of the opposing party which compels the wise man to wage just wars" (XIX.7, in the sentence beginning "But, say they, the wise man will wage just wars.").
  Tornau (§8): "War results from sin and is the privileged means of satisfying lust for power (ib. 18.2; 19.7)."; "Nevertheless, Augustine wrote a letter to refute the claim that Christianity advocated a politically impracticable pacifism (Letter 138; Tornau 2019)."
  "To be truly just according to Augustinian standards, a war would have to be waged for the benefit of the adversary and without any vindictiveness, in short, out of love of neighbor, which, in a fallen world, seems utopian (Letter 138.14)."
  "Wars may however be relatively just if they are defensive and properly declared (cf. Cicero, De officiis 1.36–37) or commanded by a just authority even short of the special case of the wars of the People of Israel that were commanded by God himself (Contra Faustum 22.74–78)."
  Aquinas quotes him for just cause (*ST* II-II q. 40 a. 1): "A just war is wont to be described as one that avenges wrongs, when a nation or state has to be punished, for refusing to make amends for the wrongs inflicted by its subjects, or to restore what it has seized unjustly."
  Moseley (IEP "Just War Theory" §1) on the weight of his contribution: "Although St. Augustine provided comments on the morality of war from the Christian perspective (railing against the love of violence that war can engender) as did several Arabic commentators in the intellectual flourishing from the 9th to 12th centuries, but the most systematic exposition in the Western tradition and one that still attracts attention was outlined by Saint Thomas Aquinas in the 13th century."
  (`raw/iep-just-war-theory-moseley-ad-bellum-in-bello-post-bellum.md`).

## Works

Dates are Tornau's (§2); the locator scheme is book.chapter (*City of God*,
*Confessions*) or section (*De mendacio*, *Enchiridion*). No work has a page in
[works](../works/index.md) yet.

- *Contra Academicos*, *De beata vita*, *De ordine*, *Soliloquia* — Cassiciacum dialogues, 386/7.
- *De libero arbitrio* (*On Free Choice of the Will*) — "begun in 388 and completed perhaps as late as 395".
- *De natura boni* — "(399, a concise anti-Manichean argument for the doctrine that evil is a privation of goodness rather than an independent substance)".
- *Confessiones* — "The Confessiones (ca. 396–400; Toom 2020), probably his most original work, is “philosophy in autobiography” (Mann 2014) rather than an autobiography in a modern sense." (Tornau's assessment, citing Mann).
- *Contra Faustum Manichaeum* — "around 400".
- *De civitate dei* (*City of God*) — "The monumental apologetic treatise De civitate dei (begun in 412, two years after the sack of Rome, and completed in 426; Meconi 2021) argues that happiness can be found neither in the Roman nor the philosophical tradition but only through membership in the city of God whose founder is Christ."
- *De mendacio* (*On Lying*), *Enchiridion*, Letter 138 (to Marcellinus) — read here in the 1887 translations; not dated in the sources read.

## In dialogue with

- **Cicero** — on foreknowledge: Augustine answers *De divinatione* and *De fato*: "However, in his book on divination, he in his own person most openly opposes the doctrine of the prescience of future things." (*City of God* V.9); on war, Tornau cites *De officiis* 1.36–37 (§8).
- **Plotinus and the Platonists** — evil as privation, taken from "Plotinus, Enneads I.8" (Tornau §7.5); Tornau adds that Augustine "rejects Plotinus’ view that prime matter is equivalent to prime evil".
- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** — on pagan virtue, Tornau (§7.2): "From this point of view, Socrates is closer to Paul than to Nero, even though his virtue will not bring him happiness, i.e., eternal bliss."
- **The Manicheans and Pelagians** — evil and grace (Tornau §§1, 7.4–7.6).
- **[Aquinas](aquinas.md)** — just war, lying (Mahon §§2.3–2.4); Tornau (§11): "Thomas Aquinas and others had little interest in Augustine’s Platonism".
- **Anselm** — "Anselm’s proof of the existence of God develops the argument of De libero arbitrio, bk. 2" (Tornau §11).
- **[Descartes](descartes.md)** — the cogito: "Augustine’s most famous anti-skeptical argument is what is commonly called his “cogito-like” argument because it is similar to (and probably inspired) the Cogito of Descartes (Matthews 1992; Menn 1998; Fuchs 2010)." (§5.1).
- **Kant** — the murderer at the door, grouped with Augustine by Mahon (§2.3) and Coady (§3).
- **Hunt and Frankfurt** — foreknowledge without alternative possibilities (Zagzebski §2.8).
- **[Jung](jung.md)** — the privatio boni (*Answer to Job*, Prefatory Note).
- **Wittgenstein** — "Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) takes issue with what he considers Augustine’s view of language and language acquisition throughout his analysis of language in the Philosophical Investigations." (Tornau §11).

## Reception

Assessments, each its author's:

- Tornau (preamble): "Augustine of Hippo was perhaps the greatest Christian philosopher of Antiquity and certainly the one who exerted the deepest and most lasting influence."
- Tornau (preamble), on his views on sin, grace, freedom and sexuality: "These views, deeply at variance with the ancient philosophical and cultural tradition, provoked however fierce criticism in Augustine’s lifetime and have, again, been vigorously opposed in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries from various (e.g., humanist, liberal, feminist) standpoints."
- Tornau (§11): "Augustine’s impact on later philosophy is as enormous as it is ambivalent".
- Luther and Calvin (Tornau §11): "Martin Luther (1483–1546) agrees with Augustine on the absolute gratuitousness of grace but does not follow the Augustinian (and scholastic) ideal of intellectus fidei and makes faith in the Gospel the decisive condition of salvation."; "The variety of Protestantism that was inaugurated by Jean Calvin (1509–1564) accepts double predestination."
- Early modern and modern (§11): "In the seventeenth century Descartes’ cogito was soon recognized as an Augustinian idea and triggered a refreshed interest in Augustine’s epistemology."; of the Enlightenment-to-Romantic philosophers, Tornau writes they showed little interest in Augustine and "even, in the case of Nietzsche, outright contempt."
- Arendt (as Tornau §11 reports): "Hannah Arendt (1906–1975) wrote her doctoral dissertation on Augustine’s philosophy of love and blamed him, not uncommonly for her epoch, for degrading love of neighbor to an instrument of personal happiness."
- Korab-Karpowicz (SEP "Political Realism in International Relations" §1.2), of Cicero: "His ideas concerning righteousness in war were carried further in the writings of the Christian thinkers St. Augustine and St. Thomas Aquinas."
- SEP "Aquinas" (preamble): "Viewed through a theological lens, Aquinas has often been seen as the summit of the Christian tradition that runs back to Augustine and the early Church."

## Vocabulary

- **Evil (*malum*)** — for Augustine the absence or privation of good, not a
  substance (*Enchiridion* 11; *Confessions* VII.12.18); this implant has no
  vocabulary page for evil.
- **Lie (*mendacium*)** — having "one thing in his mind" and uttering
  another (*De mendacio* 3), with or without intent to deceive per Mahon's
  "Simple Non-Deceptionist" reading (§2.4).
- **Will (*voluntas*)** — Tornau (§7.4) reports a faculty of choice
  "reducible neither to reason nor to non-rational desire" in Augustine's
  use; free choice is *liberum arbitrium*.
- **Privatio boni** — the term Jung uses for the doctrine
  ([Answer to Job](../works/answer-to-job.md)).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "Philippa Foot was born Philippa Ruth Bosanquet on October 3, 1920, in Owston Ferry, Lincolnshire, and grew up in Kirkleatham, in North Yorkshire, England." (Hacker-Wright §1).
- **Oxford:** "Foot earned a BLitt from Somerville for her research on Kant in 1942, and then worked in London for the remainder of the war." After the war she took a teaching post at Somerville "and then, in 1949, began a fellowship there" (§1).
- **Anscombe:** "G.E.M. Anscombe had started a research fellowship at Somerville in 1946." "Foot credits her introduction to analytic philosophy to Anscombe, who introduced Foot to a style of philosophy Anscombe picked up from Wittgenstein." (§1).
- **United States:** "Foot resigned her fellowship at Somerville in 1969 and began a period of freelancing in the United States" (§1); "In 1976, she took up a permanent position at the University of California, Los Angeles, where she remained until retiring in 1991, then returning to Oxford, where she completed her monograph Natural Goodness." (§1).
- **Death:** "Foot’s last philosophical article appeared in 2004, and she died on her ninetieth birthday, October 3, 2010." (§1).
- Hacker-Wright's §1 draws on Rosalind Hursthouse's British Academy memoir
  (2012, *Biographical Memoirs of Fellows of the British Academy* XI:
  176–196, per his bibliography); the memoir page returned 403 and was not
  read.

## Problems addressed

- **[Trolley problem](../problems/trolley-problem.md)** ("The Problem of
  Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect", 1967). Hacker-Wright
  (§4): "In “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of Double Effect”, (1967) Foot raises a related case that has been the subject of much subsequent discussion: a runaway trolley is headed toward five people who will be killed by the collision, but it could be steered onto a track on which there is only one person (1967 [VV 23])."
  Woollard (§3) reports the pair: the judge who must frame an innocent and
  the driver, and "Foot, claimed that it was wrong to kill in the first case, but not wrong in the second."
  Woollard: "Foot noted that such cases might motivate someone to accept the Doctrine of Double Effect, which distinguishes between harm that is strictly intended and harm that is merely foreseen."
  Hacker-Wright's reconstruction in terms of rights: "In Trolley, by contrast, we are not violating negative rights to meet positive rights; the situation pits the negative rights of the five against the negative rights of one, and both choices involve violating someone’s negative rights."
- **Doing and allowing** (1967; "Killing and Letting Die", 1984; "Morality,
  Action, and Outcome", 1985). The Rescue pair: "Consider a pair of scenarios called Rescue I and Rescue II (1984 [MD 80])."
  "She thinks it is impermissible to save the five in Rescue II; whereas it is permissible (at least) to save the five in Rescue I." (Hacker-Wright §4).
  Foot, as quoted there: "it takes more to justify an interference than to justify the withholding of goods and service" (1984 [MD 83]).
  "Foot’s contention is that negative rights carry greater weight than positive rights." (§4).
  Woollard (§6): "Foot distinguishes between initiating (setting the harmful sequence going); sustaining (keeping the harmful sequence going when it would otherwise have stopped); enabling (removing some barrier which would have brought the harmful sequence to a halt) and forbearing to prevent (failing to take some action which would have brought the sequence to a halt)."
  and "Foot is clear that, in her view, the doing/allowing distinction is not the same as the action/omission distinction."
- **Double effect** ([the conditions](../arguments/double-effect-conditions.md)).
  Foot's 1967 definition: "By ‘the doctrine of the double effect’ I mean the thesis that it is sometimes permissible to bring about by oblique intention what one may not directly intend."
  and her conclusion there: "I have only tried to show that even if we reject the doctrine of the double effect we are not forced to the conclusion that the size of the evil must always be our guide."
  (excerpt: `raw/foot-1967-problem-of-abortion-double-effect-closeness.md`).
  Hacker-Wright (§4) on her later papers: "Foot thereby defends a principle that draws a moral distinction between doing and allowing; she also defends a version of the doctrine of double effect, which states that it is sometimes permissible to bring about a result that one foresees as a consequence of one’s action but does not intend that it would be impermissible to aim at either as a means or an end (1985 [MD 91])."
  "Yet the doctrine of double effect cannot stand on its own, according to Foot; we need both the principle of double effect and the principle of doing and allowing." (Hacker-Wright §4).
- **[Abortion](../problems/abortion.md)** (1967; 1970; 1984).
  "Foot is skeptical of any attempt to resolve the abortion debate without settling the issue of whether and when the fetus has moral rights, and she regards this as an intractable issue (1984 [MD 87])."
  Foot, as quoted by Hacker-Wright: "the fetus is not in jeopardy because it is in its mother’s womb; it is merely dependent on her in the way children are dependent on their parents for food. (1984 [MD 87])"
  On who decides fetal status, Hacker-Wright (§4) writes: "On this issue, she rather surprisingly abandons her commitment to objectivity, claiming that it is a matter for a community to decide whether to count the fetus as a human being (1970 [MD 7])."
- **[Euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md)** ("Euthanasia", *Philosophy
  and Public Affairs* 6(2), 1977, pp. 85–112, per Hacker-Wright's
  bibliography; reprinted *Virtues and Vices* pp. 33–59, DOI
  [10.1093/0199252866.003.0003](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199252866.003.0003)).
  "Foot defines euthanasia as a killing that is for the good of the individual in question, and she asks whether such an act can ever be permissible."
  "Hence, on her view, involuntary active euthanasia can never be permissible."
  "According to Foot, nonvoluntary passive euthanasia is permissible in such an instance." (the dying soldier case, Hacker-Wright §4).
  On voluntary active euthanasia, Foot as quoted: "If someone gives you permission to destroy his property it can no longer be said that you have no right to do so, and I do not see why it should not be the same with taking a man’s life." (1977 [VV 53]); and
  "Foot also has concerns about the practice of euthanasia that make her hesitate to suggest that the practice ought to be more widely legalized." (§4).
  Young (SEP "Voluntary Euthanasia", §1) cites Foot 1977 on whether a life
  can have "no positive value" for its subject
  (`raw/sep-euthanasia-voluntary-fall-2024-young.md`).
- **[Moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)** ("Moral Realism and
  Moral Dilemma", *The Journal of Philosophy* 80(7), 1983, pp. 379–398;
  DOI [10.2307/2026455](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026455)). The paper is
  listed in McConnell's SEP "Moral Dilemmas" bibliography; it was not read,
  and no reading source held here states its thesis on dilemmas. Hacker-Wright
  (§4) quotes it on charity and justice: "in so far as a man’s charity is limited only by his justice… he is not less than perfect in charity" (1983 [MD 57]).
- **Virtue** ("Virtues and Vices", 1978). "On Foot’s account, there are three essential features of a virtue: first, a virtue is a disposition of the will; second, it is beneficial either to others, or to its possessor as well as to others; third, it is corrective of some bad general human tendency." (Hacker-Wright §5).
- **Natural goodness** (*Natural Goodness*, 2001). "The leads to the second central idea of Foot’s late work is that moral goodness is a form of what she calls ‘natural goodness’." (verbatim, §6).
  "The norms which determine what makes something a good member of its kind she calls ‘natural norms’." Foot, as quoted: "the evaluation of human action depends… on essential features of specifically human life" (NG 14).
- **Rationality of morality** ("Morality as a System of Hypothetical
  Imperatives", 1972, DOI [10.2307/2184328](https://doi.org/10.2307/2184328)).
  Hacker-Wright (§6) reports a later change: "In her late period, she believes that she erred in her middle period by embracing a mistaken view of reasons for action."

## In dialogue with

- **[Judith Jarvis Thomson](thomson.md)** — trolley: Woollard (§3) reports that Thomson (1986) "modified the case so that it was a bystander, not the driver, who had to make the choice." Abortion: "In this view, she takes sides against Judith Jarvis Thomson, who argued that some abortions are permissible even if we allow for the sake of argument that the fetus has full moral status." "Foot argues that the analogy has a devastating fault." (Hacker-Wright §4).
- **[G. E. M. Anscombe](anscombe.md)** — Somerville colleague (above).
  Wiland & Driver (SEP "Elizabeth Anscombe", §1) report a friendship: "During her early years at Oxford she met three young women who also had remarkable careers in philosophy: Iris Murdoch, Mary Midgley, and, above all, Philippa Foot, with whom she developed a deep friendship her entire life (Mac Cumhaill & Wiseman 2022)."
  and (§2) "Anscombe’s thought here resembled that of her contemporaries Philippa Foot and Iris Murdoch. All were opposed to Utilitarianism," (raw C).
  McIntyre (SEP "Doctrine of Double Effect", §4.5) lists Anscombe (1982)
  among those who deny that double effect permits swerving the trolley.
- **[Bernard Williams](williams.md)** — Chappell & Smyth (SEP "Bernard Williams", §3) compare his thick-concept project: "(For a strikingly similar philosophical project to that suggested by this talk of thick concepts, cp. Anscombe 1958a, and Philippa Foot’s papers “Moral Beliefs” and “Moral Arguments”, both in her Virtues and Vices.)"
  and (§5) "This line of thought does echo a pattern of argument that is found in many ethicists, from Plato’s Republic to Philippa Foot’s “Moral Beliefs”." (raw E).
- **James Rachels** (1975) — "She dismisses James Rachels’ claim that if we permit passive euthanasia then the only consistent position is to permit active euthanasia in similar circumstances because it is ‘more humane’ than passive euthanasia (Rachels 1975)." (Hacker-Wright §4; see [euthanasia](../problems/euthanasia.md)).
- **R. M. Hare** — "She argues against Hare that there is a sense of ‘good cactus’ that is not merely a matter of preferences that we have in cacti: there are features of a good cactus that depend on what it is to flourish as a cactus (1961 [VV 141])." (§6).
- **Warren Quinn** — "Foot came to think that these views are open to a serious objection that was pointed out by Warren Quinn, who argues that on such instrumentalist conceptions of reasons for action we can be rationally shameless." (§6). Woollard (§6): "Warren Quinn (1989) shares Foot’s view that the doing/allowing distinction rests on the difference between positive and negative rights, but offers both an alternative analysis and a deeper defense of the claim that negative rights are stronger than positive rights."
- **Michael Thompson** — "The first premise draws on Michael Thompson’s work on living things." (§6).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's. Grouped by
whether the cited statement supports or questions a claim of Foot's.

- **Assessments of standing (Hacker-Wright, preamble and §§3–4).**
  "To the wider world, and perhaps especially to undergraduate philosophy students, she is best known for inventing the Trolley Problem, which raises the question of why it seems permissible to steer a trolley aimed at five people toward one person while it seems impermissible to do something such as killing one healthy man to use his organs to save five people who will otherwise die."
  "Foot was among the pioneers of writing on issues in applied ethics, treating abortion and euthanasia."
  "Perhaps Foot’s most penetrating article on applied ethics is her “Euthanasia” (1977)."
  "Among Foot’s most anthologized, celebrated, controversial, and widely cited articles is undoubtedly “Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives” (1972, MSHI)."
- **Supporting statements.** Woollard (§6) reports Quinn sharing Foot's
  rights-based view (above). Hursthouse & Pettigrove (SEP "Virtue Ethics",
  §3) name Foot among those answering critics of neo-Aristotelian
  naturalism: "But McDowell, Foot, MacIntyre and Hursthouse have all outlined versions of a third way between these two extremes." (raw D).
- **Questioning statements.** Woollard (§3) on the bystander case: "The difference was important, since the bystander is clearly choosing between killing and letting die and yet it still seems permissible to turn the trolley. This undermines Foot’s claim that the difference between doing and allowing explains our intuitions about these cases."
  Woollard (§6) on the sequence account: "However, she argues that Foot’s account is incomplete because it does not tell us how to distinguish enabling from sustaining, why enabling is grouped with forbearing to prevent, or what sustaining and initiating have in common." (the "she" is Woollard).
  Hacker-Wright (§3) on the 1972 paper: "Prominent defenders of moral rationalism criticize Foot’s analogy with etiquette." and "Unfortunately, Foot’s actual position is sometimes misrepresented in the debate."
- **On the label "virtue ethics".** Hacker-Wright (preamble): "Foot is also known for contributing to the revival of Aristotelian virtue ethics in contemporary philosophy, though it is less well known that she emphatically disavowed being an adherent of this view as it is currently understood."
  (§5): "It should be noted that, although Foot obviously made significant contributions to the development of neo-Aristotelian virtue theory, she explicitly disavowed allegiance to virtue ethics (RG 2)."
  "Her reasons for distancing herself from virtue ethics are somewhat obscure, and possibly misconstrue philosophers who advocate views under that heading, but it seems from her writings on the nature of morality that she considers morality something apart from virtue, something that is determined by an idealized, contractual moral code." (Hacker-Wright's reading).

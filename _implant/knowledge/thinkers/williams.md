## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates:** "Bernard Williams (1929–2003)" (Chappell & Smyth, preamble).
- **Place and education:** "Bernard Williams was born in Essex in 1929, and educated at Chigwell School and Balliol College, Oxford" (§1).
- **Teacher and chair:** "On his return to Britain in 1990 (incidentally the year of Mrs. Thatcher’s resignation) he succeeded his old tutor Richard Hare as White’s Professor of Moral Philosophy at Oxford" (§1).
- **Posts:** "Williams worked in Britain until 1987, when he left for Berkeley in protest at the impact of the Thatcher government’s policies on British universities." (§1).
- **Knighthood and death:** "After 1999—when he was knighted—he began to be affected by the cancer which eventually killed him" (§1).

## Problems addressed

- **[Jim and the Indians](../problems/jim-and-the-indians.md)** and the
  **[demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md)**
  (*Utilitarianism: For and Against*, 1973, UFA 97–99, 116–117). Position:
  the integrity objection. Chappell & Smyth (§4) state it as: "In a slogan, the integrity objection is this: agency is always some particular person’s agency; or to put it another way, there is no such thing as impartial agency, in the sense of impartiality that utilitarianism requires."
  Williams, as the entry quotes him, says utilitarianism would make the
  agent "a channel between the input of everyone’s projects, including his
  own, and an output of optimific decision" (UFA 116–117, via §4). The
  entry reports that Williams does not deny the utilitarian verdict on
  Jim: "…if (as I suppose) the utilitarian is right in this case…" (UFA
  117, via §4); the point concerns "what sort of considerations come into
  finding the answer" (UFA 99, via §4).
- **[Moral luck](../problems/moral-luck.md)** ("Moral Luck", 1976,
  symposium with Nagel, pp. 115–135; Chappell & Smyth cite *Moral Luck*,
  1981). In the 1976 text: "Morality itself cannot be rendered immune to luck" (p. 116), with the
  Gauguin case, "the only thing that will justify his choice will be
  success itself" (p. 118), and the lorry driver who "will feel
  differently from any spectator" (p. 124) (excerpts:
  `raw/williams-nagel-1976-moral-luck-symposium.md`). Chappell & Smyth
  (§3) summarise: "our practice of blame is not in fact tied exclusively to voluntary control."
  They also name "ineliminable agent-regret" (1981: 27–30, via §3).
- **[Moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)** ("Ethical
  Consistency", 1965; PS 166–186). Chappell & Smyth (§3): "In real life, Williams argues, there surely are cases where we find ourselves under ethical demands which conflict."
  Williams, as quoted there: "it is surely falsifying of moral thought to
  represent its logic as demanding that in a conflict… one of the
  conflicting oughts must be totally rejected" (PS 183–4). McConnell
  (SEP "Moral Dilemmas", §5) lists Williams 1965 among those who took
  dilemmas as "a refutation of the agglomeration principle", and §4 says
  the principle was "dubbed by some the agglomeration principle (Williams
  1965)" (`raw/sep-moral-dilemmas-fall-2024-concept-and-consistency-arguments.md`;
  derivation: [consistency arguments](../arguments/consistency-arguments-against-moral-dilemmas.md)).
  McConnell (§6) names "Williams 1965 and Marcus 1980" as "The best known
  proponents of arguments for dilemmas that appeal to moral residue"
  (`raw/sep-moral-dilemmas-fall-2024-dirty-hands-and-moral-residue.md`).
- **Personal identity** ("Personal Identity and Individuation", 1956–7;
  "The Self and the Future", 1970; no problem page yet). Olson (SEP
  "Personal Identity", Fall 2024) lists Williams (1956–7, 1970) among the
  advocates of "brute-physical views" (§4), attributes to Williams 1956–7
  the case in which "Charlie’s memories are erased and replaced" by those
  of Guy Fawkes (§2), and says of an argument that one need not go with
  one's transplanted brain: "(Williams 1970 argues in a similar way.)"
  (§7) (`raw/sep-identity-personal-fall-2024-williams-brute-physical.md`).
- **Practical reason** ("Internal and external reasons", *Moral Luck*
  1981, 101–113; no problem page yet). Position: the internal reasons
  thesis, "the claim that all reasons are internal, and that there are no
  external reasons" (Chappell & Smyth, §5). On the internal reading, "A has reason to φ" implies that "A has some motive which will be served or furthered by his φing" (1981: 101, via §5).
  The entry stresses that "Williams is only offering a necessary condition" (§5).

## In dialogue with

- **J. J. C. Smart (1973):** co-author of UFA; Chappell & Smyth (§1) call
  Williams's contribution "the Against bit".
- **[Thomas Nagel](nagel.md) (1976, 1979):** replied in the same
  symposium: "Williams sidesteps the fascinating question raised in his paper." (Nagel 1976, p. 137);
  "he has argued not that an agent's moral view of his life can depend on
  luck but that ultimate regret is not immune to luck because ultimate
  regret need not be moral" (p. 137). Nelkin (SEP "Moral Luck", §1):
  "Nagel’s article began as a reply to Williams’ paper of the same name"
  (`raw/sep-moral-luck-fall-2024-nelkin-map.md`).
- **[Ruth Barcan Marcus](marcus.md) (1980):** paired with Williams 1965 by
  McConnell (§6) as a proponent of residue arguments for dilemmas.
- **Railton and Sen:** the indirect utilitarians Chappell & Smyth (§4)
  contrast with Williams's view of integrity; "Williams sees an incoherence at the very heart of the project of indirect utilitarianism" (§4).
- **Christine Korsgaard (1996)** and **John McDowell:** cited by Chappell
  & Smyth (§5) in the internal-reasons debate — Korsgaard as having
  argued that Kantian theories do not invoke external reasons, McDowell as
  presenting an external reasons theory that the entry's authors think
  "is probably not best understood that way".
- **[Anscombe](anscombe.md):** her phrase for an absolute prohibition
  holding "whatever the consequences" is the one Chappell & Smyth (§4)
  say Williams's integrity objection does not rely on.

## Reception

Assessments below are the named authors'; the implant ranks none of them.

- **Assessments favourable to Williams's positions.** Chappell & Smyth
  (preamble) call Williams "a leading influence in philosophical ethics in
  the latter half of the twentieth century", and describe his part of UFA
  as, "in the present writer’s view, a tour de force of philosophical
  demolition" (§1). Cox, La Caze and Levine (SEP "Integrity") call the
  integrity argument "An important and influential line of argument, first
  developed by Bernard Williams"
  (`raw/sep-integrity-fall-2024-williams-critique-and-replies.md`).
  Chappell & Smyth (§3) point to Joseph Raz's "Agency and Luck" as "a
  sustained defense of Williams on these basic points". Nelkin (preamble)
  writes that "Williams (1976) and Thomas Nagel (1979) showed in their now
  classic pair of articles, it appears that our everyday judgments and
  practices commit us to the existence of moral luck".
- **Objections to Williams's positions.** Chappell & Smyth (§4) anticipate: "On the issue of impartiality, it will no doubt be objected that Williams overstates his case."
  Cox, La Caze and Levine report "three main lines of response" to the
  integrity critique, among them the reply that "Were George a utilitarian, he would not have been acting against his convictions" (Carr 1976, Trianosky 1986, Blustein 1991).
  Nagel (1976, p. 137) says Williams "sidesteps" the question. Nelkin
  (§4.2.2) reports that "Others suggest that the Control Principle does
  not have nearly the hold on us that Nagel and Williams assume". Opponents
  of dilemmas, per McConnell (§5), "have generally held that the crucial
  principles in the two arguments above are conceptually true" — among
  them agglomeration, which McConnell (§5) reports Williams 1965 took
  dilemmas to refute.
- **Readings of Williams disputed in the sources.** Chappell & Smyth (§4)
  argue against reading the integrity objection as a counter-example
  argument, "because in fact Williams denies (2)"; on internal reasons
  they write "Since he is widely misinterpreted on this point" (§5).
  Nelkin (§4.1.2) reports that "Many commentators have read Williams as
  advocating the position that moral luck exists", while "in the
  Postscript, Williams makes a distinction between morality and ethics
  that allows him to deny the existence of moral luck".

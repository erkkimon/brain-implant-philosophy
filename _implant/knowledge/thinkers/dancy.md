## Problems addressed

- **Do moral thought and judgement depend on moral principles?** (no problem page yet; particularism vs generalism). Dancy's definition: "Moral Particularism, at its most trenchant, is the claim that there are no defensible moral principles, that moral thought does not consist in the application of moral principles to cases, and that the morally perfect person should not be conceived as the person of principle." (Dancy SEP, preamble). The version he calls the strongest defensible one "holds that though there may be some moral principles, still the rationality of moral thought and judgement in no way depends on a suitable provision of such things;" and "Moral principles are at best crutches that a morally sensitive person would not require, and indeed the use of such crutches might even lead us into moral error." (preamble).
  The opposing view, in his words: "Ethical generalism is the view that the rationality of moral thought and judgement depends on a suitable provision of moral principles." (preamble). He distinguishes the "‘absolute’ conception" of a principle, "a universal claim to the effect that all actions of a certain type are overall wrong (or right)", from the "‘contributory’" one (§1).
  McKeever & Ridge (SEP §1): "Jonathan Dancy has done more than anyone to articulate and defend an especially radical form of particularism." Publisher's abstract of *Ethics Without Principles* (2004): it constructs "an argument from that holism to particularism in ethics, which is characterised as the view that moral thought and judgement in no way depend on a suitable provision of moral principles."
- **How do reasons behave across cases — the holism of reasons.** "This claim emerges as the consequence of the core particularist doctrine, which we can call the holism of reasons. This is the doctrine that what is a reason in one case may be no reason at all in another, or even a reason on the other side." (Dancy SEP §3); "In ethics, a feature that makes one action better can make another one worse, and make no difference at all to a third." (§3). His non-moral example is a drug that swaps red and blue: "the appearance of a red-looking thing before me is reason for me to believe that there is a blue, not a red, thing before me." (§3). Against W. D. Ross's prima facie duties: "Suppose, for instance, that I have promised not to keep my next three promises; what then?" (§5).
  The opposing view is named by McKeever & Ridge (SEP §1): "Dancy calls this idea, which he also attributes to Ross, “atomism” in the theory of reasons and argues against it and in favour of what he calls “holism”."
- **Reasons and the other roles a consideration can play — defeaters, enablers, intensifiers.** The publisher's abstract of *Ethics Without Principles*: "It introduces and defends a distinction between reasons and enabling conditions, and considers some other roles that considerations that are morally relevant but not reasons might play." Dancy's example (SEP §6): that a promise was not made under duress "functions as an enabling condition, one in whose absence the first feature (that I promised) would not have been the reason it is." and "So the distinction between ‘counting in favour’ and ‘enabling something else to count in favour’ is significant, as particularists see things." McKeever & Ridge (SEP §3) give the general scheme: "For example, a putative reason might be defeated, enabled, or intensified by specific elements of the context." "Other factors could function as attenuators, weakening the force of a reason (see Dancy 2004: ch 5)."
- **Conflict of reasons.** Dancy (SEP §4) argues against a morality of absolute principles: "The first reason is that absolute principles cannot conflict, and that if they cannot conflict a vital aspect of our moral lives (that is, conflict) has been left out of account altogether by any theory that supposes that morality is entirely governed by absolute principles." He specifies: "What is meant by moral conflict here is not conflict between two individuals, but conflict between reasons for and against in a given case." Against monism: "We know of one position that offers only one principle: classical utilitarianism." (§4). Structural note (G4): no source read here ties Dancy to the problem of [moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md), so he is not placed there.
- **Moral knowledge without principles.** "I suggest that what the experienced moral judge knows is a range of ways in which a feature can contribute to determining how to act." (Dancy SEP §3). McKeever & Ridge (SEP §4): "For example, Dancy described the virtuous agent as someone possessed of a “contentless ability” to discern what matters when it matters (Dancy 1993: 50)." On a priori knowledge of contingent truths: "In his defense of particularism, Dancy has been willing to embrace this apparent consequence (Dancy 2004; for criticism, see McKeever and Ridge 2006)." (§4). On thick concepts, McKeever & Ridge quote him (§5) as holding that "almost all the standard thick concepts…are of variable relevance" (Dancy 2004: 121).
- **The reasons why we act and the reasons why we should** (*Practical Reality*, 2000). Publisher's abstract: "Practical Reality is about the relation between the reasons why we do things and the reasons why we should." and "Motivating reasons are not mental states of the agent, but states of affairs." Alvarez (SEP §1) places him among those for whom the normative/motivating distinction "is just a distinction concerning different things reasons can do", quoting him: "When I call a reason “motivating”, all that I am doing is issuing a reminder that the focus of our attention is on matters of motivation, for the moment." (Dancy 2000: 2–3).
  The normative constraint: "Another important argument turns on the idea that any account of motivating reasons must meet what Dancy (1995; 2000) calls “the normative constraint”:" and "Dancy’s point is that psychologism fails to meet this constraint—in fact, he says, psychologism has the consequence that “the reasons why we act can never be among the reasons in favour of acting” (2000: 105)." (Alvarez §3.1). Dancy, as quoted by Alvarez (§4.2): "[i]n the best case, there is some good reason for doing the action, and the reason that motivates the agent coincides with that reason. (2000: 3)"
- **Imaginary cases in ethics.** "The Role of Imaginary Cases in Ethics", *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly* 66 (1985): 141–153 (CV). Brown & Fehige (SEP "Thought Experiments", §2) cite it in the sentence "In fact, thought experiments as a method in ethics have their critics (see, e.g., Dancy 1985)." (`raw/sep-thought-experiment-privacy-self-defense-foot-fall-2024-on-thomson.md`).

## In dialogue with

- **W. D. Ross** (earlier; no page yet). Dancy (SEP §5): "The classic example of such a theory is W. D. Ross’s theory of Prima Facie Duties (Ross 1930, ch. 2)." "Particularists applaud Ross’s insistence that there can be many features of the situation each of which makes some difference to how one should act; they merely want to say that the matter is not regular in the way that Ross, as a generalist, supposes." McKeever & Ridge (SEP §1): "Although Ross was both a foil and an inspiration for Dancy, R.M. Hare was a more immediate opponent."
- **[R. M. Hare](hare.md)** (earlier). McKeever & Ridge (SEP §1) report that Dancy objected to an idea "he took to be implicit in Hare’s universalizability principle" — that a reason in one context keeps the same valence in every context — and add: "(This reading of Hare is open to objection. See McNaughton and Rawlings (2000) for discussion.)"
- **[Immanuel Kant](kant.md)** (earlier, as cited by the critics). McKeever & Ridge (SEP §3) offer Kant as a generalist who "arguably" treats the fact that an action would advance someone's happiness as of variable moral significance (McKeever and Ridge 2005) — their example that holism and principles can coexist.
- **David McNaughton and John McDowell** (contemporaries; no pages yet). "As Dancy’s early work came to fruition, it inspired his then-colleague David McNaughton to advance distinct but complementary arguments for particularism." (McKeever & Ridge §1). The Hooker & Little volume's abstract names "reactions to the writings of John McDowell and Jonathan Dancy" as its spur.
- **Roger Crisp** (critic; no page). Dancy (SEP §6) reports that "invariant reasons derive from the virtues (Crisp 2000)" and replies with a counter-example: "It may be considerate to wipe the torturer’s brow, but this fact hardly functions as a reason to wipe, or makes his sweat a reason for us to wipe it off." His verdict on Crisp's view: "But I would say that the supposed virtues do not in fact play the role required here." (§6).
- **Brad Hooker, Margaret Little and the 2000 volume** (critics and sympathisers). Hooker & Little (eds.), *Moral Particularism* (Oxford, 2000), DOI [10.1093/oso/9780198238843.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198238843.001.0001), with Hooker, "Moral Particularism: Wrong and Bad", pp. 1–22, Crisp, "Particularizing Particularism", pp. 23–47, Raz, "The Truth in Particularism", pp. 48–78, and Dancy's own reply-chapter. Hooker's argument from predictability, as McKeever & Ridge (§6) report it: "One such argument appeals to the value of predictability (Hooker 2000, 2008)."; Dancy's answer, as they quote it: "“People are quite capable of judging how to behave case by case, and in a way that would enable us to predict what they will in fact do” (Dancy 2004: 83)."
- **Sean McKeever and Michael Ridge** (critics). *Principled Ethics: Generalism as a Regulative Ideal* (Oxford, 2006), DOI [10.1093/0199290652.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199290652.001.0001); see Reception.

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's. Dancy's own
replies are placed beside each objection.

| Objection (owner, locator) | Dancy's reply (locator) |
|---|---|
| Holism does not yield particularism: "Generalists have rejected the inference on the grounds that holism leaves open the possibility that the behaviour of reasons, defeaters, enablers, and intensifiers/attenuators is codifiable (see Väyrynen 2004; McKeever and Ridge 2005)." (McKeever & Ridge SEP §3) | "Particularists have countered that even if holism is logically consistent with principles it would nevertheless render them “cosmic accidents” (Dancy 2004: 82)." (as McKeever & Ridge report, §3); they rejoin: "Generalists counter that whether principles are cosmic accidents depends entirely upon underlying metaphysical issues and not on whether principles tolerate holism." |
| *Whole reasons*: the contextual factors are parts of one larger reason — "Such views have been proposed by Bennett (1995), Crisp (2000), Hooker (2003) and Raz (2000), and rejected by Dancy (2004: 6.2)." (McKeever & Ridge §3) | The complicated principle yields "not itself a reason at all, but only a guarantee (when finally complete) that there is a reason somewhere within it." (Dancy SEP §6) |
| Consistency: "thinking rationally requires at least that one think consistently, and in ethics this just means taking the same feature to be the same reason wherever it occurs." (objection as Dancy states it, SEP §8) | For belief, consistency "is merely that we do not adopt beliefs that cannot all be true together"; "Simply to insist that this is so must be to beg the question against particularism." (§8) |
| Laxity: "The general idea here is that a particularist morality is a lax morality: without principles, anything goes." (objection as stated, §8) | "But really the remedy for poor moral judgement is not a different style of moral judgement, principle-based judgement, but just better moral judgement." (§8) |
| Special pleading: "Generalists worry that the exhortation to look again is simply unrealistic, given human nature, and is therefore not only fallible but unlikely to do much good." (McKeever & Ridge §6) | Principles distort: "We all know the sort of person who refuses to make the decision here that the facts are obviously calling for, because he cannot see how to make that decision consistent with one he made on a different occasion. (Dancy 1993: 64)" (quoted §6) |

- **Learning from experience.** Of the three rationality objections he lists, Dancy writes: "Of these three points, the third is the hardest." (SEP §8) — the objection that the generalist explains moral learning as extracting a principle.
- **McKeever and Ridge, 2006 (publisher's abstract).** The particularist attack holds "that the traditional link between morality and principles is little more than an unwarranted prejudice"; their book replies that "Generalism as a regulative ideal incorporates what is most insightful in particularism (e.g., the possibility that reasons are context sensitive - ‘holism about reasons’) while rejecting every major particularist doctrine." and "It argues that in so far as moral knowledge and wisdom are possible, all of morality can and should be codified in a manageable set of principles, even if we are not yet in possession of those principles."
- **Hooker & Little, 2000 (editors' abstract).** "Spurred in large part by reactions to the writings of John McDowell and Jonathan Dancy, philosophers continue to divide between those who find particularism’s claims insightful and those who find them exaggerated or wrong-headed."
- **On Practical Reality.** Alvarez (SEP §4.1) calls it "Dancy’s influential argument against psychologism about motivating reasons" (her word). Against the factualist view he shares, she reports: "Against this, it has been argued that the proposal leads to paradox or infelicitous claims." (§3.1).
- **Festschrift.** Bakhurst, Hooker & Little (eds.), *Thinking About Reasons: Themes from the Philosophy of Jonathan Dancy* (Oxford, 2013), DOI [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199604678.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199604678.001.0001) (not read).
- **Joint review.** Mark Schroeder reviewed *Ethics Without Principles* and *Principled Ethics* together in *Noûs* 43 (2009), DOI [10.1111/j.1468-0068.2009.00719.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0068.2009.00719.x) (not read; McKeever & Ridge's bibliography gives pp. 341–347, Crossref 568–580).

## Vocabulary

- *Particularism / generalism*: in Dancy's sense, theses about whether the rationality of moral thought "depends on a suitable provision of moral principles" (SEP preamble); no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Holism* (of reasons) vs *atomism*: holism is "the doctrine that what is a reason in one case may be no reason at all in another, or even a reason on the other side" (SEP §3); atomism is Dancy's name, as McKeever & Ridge report, for the view he attributes to Ross and Hare.
- *Contributory* reason: Dancy's term for what others call pro tanto — McKeever & Ridge (SEP §1) write "pro tanto (or “contributory” in Jonathan Dancy’s terminology) reasons" — the mark of the 'contributory' conception of principles (SEP §1).
- *Enabler, defeater, intensifier, attenuator*: considerations that are relevant without being reasons (SEP §6; McKeever & Ridge §3).
- *Motivating / normative* reason: for Dancy two roles of one kind of thing, not two kinds (Alvarez §1); *the normative constraint* (Dancy 1995, 2000), as above.
- *Dilemma* in "Prisoner’s Dilemma": Dancy uses the [prisoner's dilemma](../problems/prisoners-dilemma.md) only as an example of reasons that depend on one another (SEP §5: "in the Prisoner’s Dilemma one prisoner only has reasons if the other one does"), not in the implant's sense of [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md).

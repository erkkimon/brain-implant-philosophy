## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates and name:** "G.E. Moore (1873-1958) (who hated his first names, ‘George Edward’ and never used them — his wife called him ‘Bill’) was an important British philosopher of the first half of the twentieth century." (Baldwin, preamble).
- **Education:** "In 1892 he went to Trinity College Cambridge to study Classics." Encouraged by Russell and McTaggart, "Moore decided to add the study of Philosophy to his study of Classics, and he graduated in 1896 with a First Class degree in the subject." (§1).
- **Fellowship and Russell:** he won a Prize Fellowship at Trinity College in 1898: "In 1898 he was successful and over the next six years he matured as a dynamic young philosopher, actually leading Russell away from the idealist philosophy of McTaggart and others which was then dominant in Britain." (§1).
- **Career:** "Moore's Fellowship ended in 1904; after a spell away from Cambridge, Moore returned there in 1911 to a lectureship in the University and he then lived there for the rest of his life (apart from an extended visit to the U.S.A. in 1940-44)." "In 1921 he became editor of Mind, the leading British philosophical journal, and in 1925 he became a Professor at Cambridge." (§1).
- **Retirement:** "Moore retired as Professor in 1939 (to be succeeded by Wittgenstein) and as editor of Mind in 1944; these retirements marked not only the end of his pre-eminence, but also of the golden age of Cambridge philosophy." (§1; the second clause is Baldwin's assessment).
- **Teacher:** Schultz (SEP "Henry Sidgwick", §2.1) calls Moore Sidgwick's "student": "his student G. E. Moore was willing to declare the Methods untainted by the “naturalistic fallacy”" (`raw/sep-sidgwick-fall-2024-life-methods-dualism-reception.md`).
- **Bloomsbury and the Apostles:** "Early in his time in Cambridge Moore became friends with some of the young men who went on to form the ‘Bloomsbury Group’, such as Lytton Strachey, Leonard Woolf and Maynard Keynes." (Baldwin §1). Hurka (§5) names "Keynes, Lytton Strachey, and Leonard and Virginia Woolf, several of whom had come to know Moore while members with him of the Apostles."

## Problems addressed

- **What "good" means: indefinability and the open question** (metaethics; *Principia Ethica* ch. I). Moore: "If I am asked ‘What is good?’ my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter." (§6). The test he applies to every proposed definition: "whatever definition be offered, it may be always asked, with significance, of the complex so defined, whether it is itself good." (§13, on "that which we desire to desire").
  Hurka (§1): "His main argument against their view was what has come to be known as the “open-question argument,” though he actually stated in two slightly different ways." On its origin: "Sidgwick had used one form of this argument against Bentham and Spencer, but only in passing (1907: 26n, 109); Moore developed it a greater length and made it central to his metaethics."
- **What Moore called the naturalistic fallacy** (1903, ch. I §10; ch. III). Moore names it: "This view I propose to call the ‘naturalistic fallacy’ and of it I shall now endeavour to dispose." (§10). Baldwin (§3) summarises the charge as "the thesis that the position involves a fallacy, the ‘naturalistic fallacy’, of supposing that goodness, which Moore takes to be the fundamental ethical value, can be defined in naturalistic terms, in terms, say, of pleasure or desire or the course of evolution."
  Hurka (§1) lists the targets: "Views that denied this thesis committed what he called “the naturalistic fallacy” (1903: 10/1993: 62), which he found in hedonists such as Jeremy Bentham, evolutionary ethicists such as Herbert Spencer, and metaphysical ethicists such as T.H. Green." Against Mill, see In dialogue with.
- **[The paradox of analysis](../problems/paradox-of-analysis.md)** (reply to Langford, 1942, pp. 665–667). Hurka (§1) reports that one objection to the open-question argument "said the argument’s persuasiveness depends on the “paradox of analysis”: that any definition of a concept will, if it is successful, appear uninformative." Moore on Langford's puzzle: "Now I own I am not at all clear as to what the solution of the puzzle is." (1942, p. 665; `raw/langford-moore-1942-paradox-of-analysis-schilpp.md`). Positions and replies are on the problem page.
- **Right action and consequences** (ch. V §89). Moore: "‘right’ does and can mean nothing but ‘cause of a good result,’ and is thus identical with ‘useful’;" Hurka (§3) names the view: "One was impersonal consequentialism, the view that what is right is always what produces the greatest total good impartially considered, or counting all goods equally."
  In practice, Baldwin (§3) reports, "Moore ends up recommending a conservative form of rule consequentialism, for which he was criticised by Keynes and Russell." Hurka (§3): "This indirect consequentialism had again been defended earlier, by Sidgwick and John Stuart Mill, but Moore gave it a very conservative form, urging adherence to the rules even in the face of apparently compelling evidence that breaking them now would be optimific."
- **The ideal: what is good in itself** (ch. VI §113). "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects." Hurka (§3): "According to his ideal consequentialism, what is right is in large part what most promotes loving personal relationships and aesthetic appreciation for all persons everywhere." The label *ideal utilitarianism* for this view and Rashdall's appears in the title of a work Hurka's bibliography lists: Skelton, "Ideal Utilitarianism: Rashdall and Moore" (2011).
- **Organic unities** (ch. I §18). "The paradox, to which it is necessary to call attention, is that _the value of such a whole bears no regular proportion to the sum of the values of its parts_." (underscores mark italics in the Gutenberg text). Hurka (§4) states the principle as one that "says the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values its parts would have on their own (1903: 27–29/1993: 78–80)", and traces it: "This principle had been accepted by Idealists such as Bradley, who gave it a characteristically anti-theoretical formulation."
- **Egoism and the dualism of practical reason** (ch. III §59). Moore: "The only reason I can have for aiming at ‘my own good,’ is that it is _good absolutely_ that what I so call should belong to me--_good absolutely_ that I should _have_ something, which, if I have it, others cannot have." Schultz (SEP "Henry Sidgwick", §3): "Moore (1903) famously dismissed the problem on the grounds that the notion of one’s “own good” was nonsense, but Mackie (1976) sharply countered that Moore was begging the question." (see [Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)).
- **Common sense and the external world** (1925, 1939). Baldwin (§6): "Moore first explicitly championed this position in his 1910-11 lectures Some Main Problems of Philosophybut he made it his own when he responded in 1925 to an invitation to describe his ‘philosophical position’ by setting this out as ‘A Defence of Common Sense’." ("Philosophybut" sic in the SEP HTML.) "Moore begins the paper by listing a large number of ‘truisms’ such as that ‘the earth had existed also for many years before my body was born’."
  The 1939 lecture, as Baldwin quotes it: "How? By holding up my two hands, and saying, as I make a certain gesture with the right hand, ‘Here is one hand’, and adding, as I make a certain gesture with the left, ‘and here is another’ (‘Proof of an External World’ 166)."
  On dreaming, Baldwin (§6) reports that in "Certainty" Moore "accepts (with reservations) that he cannot know for certain that he is not dreaming." The skeptical-argument pages are not yet written.
- **[Moore's paradox](../problems/moores-paradox.md)** (1942, pp. 541–543; 1944, p. 204). Moore: "And this is why to say such a thing as “I went to the pictures last Tuesday, but I don’t believe that I did” is a perfectly absurd thing to say, although what is asserted is something which is perfectly possible logically" (1942, p. 543; `raw/moore-1942-reply-to-my-critics-pictures-last-tuesday.md`). His explanation, a sense of "imply" that is not entailment: "That we imply it means only, I think, something which results from the fact that people, in general, do not make a positive assertion, unless they do not believe that the opposite is true" (1944, p. 204; `raw/moore-1944-russells-theory-of-descriptions-he-has-gone-out.md`). Wittgenstein's response and later accounts are on the problem page.

## In dialogue with

- **[John Stuart Mill](mill.md)** (earlier). Moore on the proof of utility: "There, that is enough. That is my first point. Mill has made as naïve and artless a use of the naturalistic fallacy as anybody could desire." (ch. III §40). On qualities of pleasure: "I have pointed out that, if you say, as Mill does, that quality of pleasure is to be taken into account, then you are no longer holding that pleasure alone is good as an end, since you imply that something else, something which is not present in all pleasures, is also good as an end." (§48). Macleod's reply for Mill is on the Mill page (Reception).
- **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (teacher). Hurka (preamble): "In metaethics Moore’s non-naturalist realism was close to that defended by Henry Sidgwick and other late 19th-century philosophers such as Hastings Rashdall, Franz Brentano, and J.M.E. McTaggart;" and (§5): "When Sidgwick noticed Bentham or Spencer equating goodness with a natural property such as pleasure, he thought it a minor slip that ought in charity to be ignored; Moore deemed it a decisive error that vitiated the philosopher’s entire system." On "my own good", see Problems addressed.
- **Jeremy Bentham, Herbert Spencer, T. H. Green** (earlier) — reported by Hurka (§1) as the hedonist, evolutionary and metaphysical writers in whom Moore found what he called the naturalistic fallacy.
- **F. H. Bradley and J. M. E. McTaggart** (earlier). Baldwin (§1) reports that Moore led Russell "away from the idealist philosophy of McTaggart and others"; Hurka (§4) on Bradley and organic unities is quoted above.
- **Bertrand Russell** (contemporary). Baldwin (§3) reports Russell, with Keynes, criticising Moore's rule consequentialism.
- **W. D. Ross** (later). Baldwin (§3): "Later critics such as W. D. Ross argued that because Moore subjects our personal responsibilities to the impersonal test of producing the best outcome his position does not adequately capture the way in which they arise from our relationships with particular people;" Hurka (§1) reports Ross's defence of the open-question argument (1930: 92–94).
- **C. H. Langford** (later) — [the paradox of analysis](../problems/paradox-of-analysis.md), Schilpp volume (1942).
- **W. K. Frankena** (later) — "The Naturalistic Fallacy", *Mind* 48 (1939); see Reception.
- **A. J. Ayer, C. L. Stevenson, R. M. Hare** (later). Baldwin (§3): Moore's intuitionism "led directly to the development of ethical non-cognitivism by those who were influenced by Moore, such as A. J. Ayer and C. L. Stevenson." Hurka (§5) adds Hare's prescriptivism.
- **[G. E. M. Anscombe](anscombe.md)** and **[Philippa Foot](foot.md)** (later). Hurka (§5) cites "Anscombe 1958; Foot 2001" for the neo-Aristotelian naturalism that "implicitly denies the autonomy of ethics".
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein** (contemporary). Successor to the chair (Baldwin §1); named Moore's paradox and wrote to Moore about it (Stroll 2010, on the [problem page](../problems/moores-paradox.md)). Baldwin (§6) on *On Certainty*: "I myself think that Wittgenstein's writings On Certainty, which were much influenced by Moore, best indicate how this is to be achieved, but this is not the place to demonstrate this achievement." (Baldwin's view.)

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Bloomsbury, as reported.** Hurka (§5): "Keynes said the book’s publication was for them “the opening of a new heaven on a new earth” and its theory of the good became for a time their “religion” (1949: 82)". Whether they followed the whole theory is disputed in his report: "But according to Keynes (though Leonard Woolf (1960: 146–49) disagreed), they tended to ignore the impartial consequentialism within which he embedded those goods, concentrating on pursuing them just within their own lives rather than encouraging their wider spread in society."
  Baldwin (§3): "Moore's choice of values is striking: it connects with the ‘Bloomsbury’ ideal of life devoted to Art and Love, and excludes social values such as equality and freedom." "As Keynes put it, Moore's ideal was a kind of secular ‘religion’ - not much use for public policy but fine for talented individuals who could agree to differ in their detailed value judgments." Baldwin's own estimate (§1): "Through these friendships Moore exerted an indirect influence on British twentieth-century culture as profound as that of any more ‘engaged’ philosopher."
- **Frankena (1939), as two encyclopedia authors report it.** W. K. Frankena, "The Naturalistic Fallacy", *Mind* 48 (192): 464–477, DOI [10.1093/mind/XLVIII.192.464](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XLVIII.192.464) (verified by DOI content negotiation; not read here). Lutz (SEP "Moral Naturalism"): "One traditional response, from William Frankena (1939), says that the OQA begs the question." "To an analytic descriptivist who sincerely holds that, e.g., pleasure is the definition of goodness, the question “Given that X is pleasurable, is it good?” will seem closed."
  Ridge (SEP "Moral Non-Naturalism", §1): "However, to suppose that whenever someone is metaphysically or semantically confused he is guilty of a ‘fallacy’ robs the term of its more standard and useful meaning. This point was made very clearly by W.K. Frankena in a landmark article published in Mind (Frankena 1939)." and "Finally, as Frankena also nicely pointed out, it cannot be assumed at the outset that what Moore calls the naturalistic fallacy really is a mistake of any kind." (`raw/sep-naturalism-moral-non-naturalism-fall-2024-frankena-1939.md`).
- **The open-question argument, against and for.** Against: Baldwin (§3): "The merits of this argument are questionable; in many cases we can sensibly doubt the truth of a definition, especially where the definition makes use of discoveries that have not been part of our ordinary understanding, as is normally the case with definitions in the natural sciences." Hurka (§1) reports the concept/property objection: "Another objection, made from the 1970s on and very influential, says that while the open-question argument may show that the concept “good” is distinct from any non-moral concept, it cannot support a similar conclusion about the property of goodness." For: Baldwin proposes his own modification (§3), and Hurka reports replies by Ross (1930) and Parfit (2011) (§1).
- **Organic unities and intuition (Baldwin §3).** "The problem here is not that Moore's principle is incorrect, but rather that it seems irrational since it puts a block on moral reasoning." On moral knowledge: "Instead he holds that ethical knowledge rests on a capacity for an intuitive grasp of fundamental ethical truths for which we can give no reason since there is no reason to be given." Hurka (§5), on the other side, reports present-day "admiration for particular features of Moore’s own account, such as his valuing of personal love, his recursive principles (Hurka 2001) and his principle of organic unities."
- **Originality and standing (Hurka).** "G.E. Moore’s Principia Ethica of 1903 is often considered a revolutionary work that set a new agenda for 20th-century ethics. This historical view is, however, somewhat overstated." (preamble). "For this reason, Moore’s work had a disproportionate influence on 20th-century moral philosophy and remains the best-known expression of a general metaethical view also shared by later writers such as H.A. Prichard, W.D. Ross, and C.D. Broad." Over time (§5): "By the 1960s, it seems fair to say, many of Moore’s views were thought deeply problematic and his moral philosophy as a whole was not considered an especially live option." and "Fifty-plus years later the philosophical climate is more favorable to Moore."
- **The proof of an external world (Baldwin §6).** "It is commonly supposed that Moore here sets himself to refute philosophical skepticism; and that his performance, though intriguing, is unsuccessful. But this interpretation is incorrect: Moore's avowed aim is to prove the existence of an external world, not to prove his knowledge of the existence of an external world." (Baldwin's reading.) "Moore's ‘proof’ demonstrates the ‘empirical’ truth of a simple truism, that he has hands; but it leaves entirely open the question of the analysis of this truism." On "Certainty": "Most commentators agree that Moore lost his way here."
- **Overall (Baldwin §7).** "Moore was not a systematic philosopher: unlike Reid's philosophy of common sense, Moore's ‘common sense’ is not a system." "Moore had an unparalleled ability for identifying philosophical ‘phenomena’." On Moore's paradox Baldwin judges that "Wittgenstein rightly saw that this explanation was superficial" (§7; Baldwin's verdict).

## Vocabulary

- *Naturalistic fallacy*: Moore's name (1903 §10) for identifying goodness with some other property; Ridge, reporting Frankena, holds that it is not a [fallacy](../vocabulary/fallacy.md) in the term's "more standard and useful meaning". The implant's vocabulary page treats *fallacy* as always someone's classification.
- *Paradox* in "organic unities": Moore's word in §18 for the non-additivity of value, not the implant's normative [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md), an argument to an unacceptable conclusion; *Moore's paradox* is a later name (Baldwin §7; Wittgenstein, PI II.x).
- *Good*: for Moore "simple" and indefinable (1903 §10); *non-natural*: Hurka (§1) glosses as "distinct from any of the natural properties studied by science".
- *Imply*: in Moore 1942 and 1944, a relation that holds "though we neither assert this, nor does it follow from anything we do assert" (1944, p. 204) — not logical implication.
- *Ideal*: the title of *Principia Ethica* ch. VI, Moore's list of intrinsic goods; *ideal utilitarianism* is a label in the secondary literature (Skelton 2011, per Hurka's bibliography).

Related thinkers: [Hare](hare.md), [Mackie](mackie.md), [Parfit](parfit.md) (Hurka §1).

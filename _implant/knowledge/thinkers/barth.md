## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and death:** "Born: May 10, 1886, Basel, Switzerland"; "Died: December 9/10, 1968, Basel" (Torrance, fact box).
- **Education:** "He studied at the universities of Bern, Berlin, Tübingen, and Marburg. At Berlin he attended the liberal theologian Adolf von Harnack’s seminar, and at Marburg he came under the influence of Wilhelm Herrmann" (Torrance).
- **Pastorate:** "After serving a curacy in Geneva from 1909 to 1911, he was appointed to the working-class parish of Safenwil, in Aargau canton." (Torrance).
- **Break with liberal theology**, as Torrance reports it: "Deeply shocked by the disaster that had overtaken Europe in World War I and disillusioned by the collapse of the ethic of religious idealism, he questioned the liberal theology of his German teachers and its roots in the rationalist, historicist, and dualist thought that stemmed from the Enlightenment."
- **Chairs:** "On the basis of this publication, Barth in 1921 was appointed professor of Reformed theology at the University of Göttingen; he was later appointed to professorial chairs at Münster (1925) and Bonn (1930)." (Torrance; "this publication" is *Der Römerbrief*).
- **Church struggle:** "He was one of the founders of the so-called Confessing Church, which reacted vigorously and indignantly against the attempt to set up a “German Christian” church supported by the Nazi government." (Torrance; "so-called" is Torrance's word.)
- **Bonn to Basel, 1935:** "his refusal to take the oath of allegiance to Hitler—as was required of all professors—cost him his chair in Bonn in 1935. He was quickly offered the chair of theology in his native Basel, however." (Torrance).
- **Church Dogmatics unfinished:** "With the Church Dogmatics left incomplete at the time of Barth’s death in" 1968, "reconciliation ethics were never finished and redemption ethics never begun." (Edwards 2016, pp. 89–90; `raw/edwards-2016-keeping-the-command-barth-voluntarism.md`).

## Problems addressed

- **Ethics as part of dogmatics; the command of God.** Edwards (2016, p. 89) reports the plan: "The outline of Barth’s ethical corpus appeared in his summer 1928 lectures on ethics (hereafter the Münster Ethics), later published as simply Ethics." and "Barth would follow the same structure (or “Way of Theological Ethics”) throughout his Church Dogmatics—God, creation, reconciliation, redemption—placing one section on the divine command at the end of each volume."
  Barth's thesis, as Edwards quotes CD II/2, 547: "The good of human action consists in the fact that it is determined by the divine command." (p. 1, epigraph).
  Jakobsen (2023): "According to Barth, ethics is an aid to hearing God’s voice, hearing what God commands. Where do we hear God’s voice? In the Word of God, that is, in Jesus Christ (Barth 1957b, vol. II.2, p. 559)." and "the commands of God the reconciler form the material heart of Barth’s divine command theory, making Christology central to his applied ethics" (`raw/jakobsen-2023-christological-critique-divine-command-barth.md`, [doi:10.3390/rel14040558](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel14040558), CC BY 4.0).
  On particular commands, John E. Hare (2012, p. 191): "For Barth, all God’s commands are particular, and the Ten Commandments are best seen as propaedeutic, preparing us for what God is going to command us in our individual circumstances." (`raw/hare-2012-divine-command-scotus-kierkegaard-barth-euthyphro.md`, [doi:10.1501/ilhfak_0000001370](https://doi.org/10.1501/ilhfak_0000001370)).
- **[The Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md)** (divine commands and arbitrariness). Jakobsen classes him: "Barth is a divine-command theorist, holding that God’s command constitutes what is a right and wrong action", and adds (note 5): "As opposed to the other divine command theorists mentioned in this article, Barth also holds that what is good for humans is constituted by God’s command (Barth 1957b, vol. II.2, p. 547; McKenny 2021, p. 54)."
  The tie to the dilemma runs through readers, not through any text of Barth's read here. Hare (2012, p. 194), writing on divine command theory, of which he names Barth a holder (p. 191): "A second objection is that divine command theory makes morality arbitrary, since torturing babies would then be right if God were to command it. Sometimes this objection is tied to the dilemma proposed by Socrates to Euthyphro in the dialogue of that name (Euthyphro 10a1ff), whether the holy is loved by the gods because it is holy, or whether it is holy because it is loved by the gods." Hare does not apply the objection to Barth by name.
  Edwards (2016) reports the arbitrariness charge as it has been made against voluntarism, the family to which he assigns Barth: "it is claimed that voluntarism places an inordinate and dangerous emphasis upon divine power, insufficient attention is granted to human freedom, and ethics are bound to an irrational and arbitrary command." (p. 5).
  Barth's own answer, as Edwards quotes CD II/2: "What this God determines is not a whim, and what He presents to man to do or not do is not the product of the fickle caprices of a tyrant whose right consists in his might. . . . God is faithful and constant." (CD II/2, 676–77; Edwards p. 104) and "When God wills something from and for man, when man’s will is claimed by God, there can be no question of an arbitrary and purposeless control which God can exercise just because He is God and therefore superior to man." (CD II/2, 562; Edwards p. 106).
  The problem page sets out the general replies on record; whether Barth's answer meets the objection is disputed among his readers (Reception).
- **Natural theology** (the 1934 exchange with Emil Brunner; no problem page yet). Torrance: "In 1934 he published Nein! Antwort an Emil Brunner (Eng. trans. “No!” in Natural Theology [1946]), a response to Emil Brunner’s essay “Nature and Grace.” In his response, Barth traced the religious syncretism and support of anti-Semitism of the “German Christians” to natural theology and the perversion of historic Christianity."
  Chignell & Pereboom (SEP, §1): "The 20th century Reformed theologian Karl Barth opposed natural theology for much the same reason, and made his opposition to Emil Brunner’s version of the hybridist project clear in a book titled simply “Nein!” (Barth 1934)." The "same reason" is Pascal's, that unaided reason leads to the god "of philosophers and scholars" (raw file, Context).
  Hart (1993, p. 290) states the conviction he attributes to Barth: "Christian theology, Christian ethics, therefore, could in no sense be held to derive their message from two distinct sources: revelation on the one hand, and nature or reason on the other." (`raw/hart-1993-barth-brunner-debate-revisited.md`, [doi:10.53751/001c.30462](https://doi.org/10.53751/001c.30462)).
  Brunner's side, as Hart summarises it: "Brunner's insistent differentiation between a 'formal' and 'material' image of God in humans, and his affirmation of the need for a 'point of contact' for grace in human nature as created and fallen." Barth's reply, as Hart quotes *Natural Theology* p. 88: "'If we are prepared to call the fact that man is man and not a cat the 'point of contact', 'the objective possibility of divine revelation', then all objection to these concepts is nonsensical. For this truth is incontrovertible' (p. 88)." (Hart p. 300).
- **Church, state and confession: the Barmen Declaration** (Confessional Synod, Barmen, "May 29-31 1934"). Authorship: Torrance writes that the Declaration was "largely based on a draft that Barth had prepared"; Krötke (2006, p. 270) calls Barth "essentially the author of the Barmen Theses" (`raw/krotke-2006-barmen-declaration-historical-overview.md`, [doi:10.17570/ngtt.2006.v47n1.a24](https://doi.org/10.17570/ngtt.2006.v47n1.a24)).
  Thesis 1 (EKD English text, `raw/barmen-1934-theological-declaration-theses-ekd.md`): "Jesus Christ, as he is attested for us in holy scripture, is the one Word of God which we have to hear and which we have to trust and obey in life and in death." and "We reject the false doctrine, as though the church could and would have to acknowledge as a source of its proclamation, apart from and besides this one Word of God, still other events and powers, figures and truths, as God's revelation."
  Thesis 2: "We reject the false doctrine, as though there were areas of our life in which we would not belong to Jesus Christ, but to other lords - areas in which we would not need justification and sanctification through him." Thesis 5: "We reject the false doctrine, as though the state, over and beyond its special commission, should and could become the single and totalitarian order of human life, thus fulfilling the church's vocation as well."
  Krötke reports two readings of thesis 1: Barth "understood Jesus Christ himself to be the only Word of God whom we have to trust and obey. Especially in the Lutheran tradition “the one Word of God” was understood as the decisive Word of God through which God speaks to us." On the first reading the thesis excludes "natural theology" (p. 270). On thesis 5: "Karl Barth, who had to rephrase his draft of this thesis at the synod, could only allude to the concerns" for a christological grounding (p. 271).

## Works

Works pages are not yet written. Dates are as Torrance gives them unless
marked. The CD locator scheme is volume/part-volume and page of the
Bromiley–Torrance English edition (e.g. CD II/2, 547), as Edwards and Jakobsen
cite it.

- *Der Römerbrief* (1919; *The Epistle to the Romans*): Torrance writes that it "established his position as a notable theologian with a new and arresting message about the sheer Godness of God and the unlimited range of his grace."; a heavily revised edition followed in 1922. Torrance: "The critical and explosive nature of Barth’s work came to be known as “dialectical theology” or “the theology of crisis”; it initiated a trend toward neoorthodoxy in Protestant theology."
- *Ethics* (Münster lectures, summer 1928; Edwards p. 89; English: ed. Dietrich Braun, trans. Geoffrey W. Bromiley, New York: Seabury, 1981, per Edwards's note 66).
- *Fides quaerens intellectum* (1931), the study of Anselm (Torrance).
- *Die Kirchliche Dogmatik* / *Church Dogmatics*: "In the following year there appeared the first part of his massive Church Dogmatics." (Torrance, i.e. 1932). Wood's bibliography: 1932–1967, "13 books in 4 volumes"; English ed. G. W. Bromiley and T. F. Torrance, Edinburgh: T&T Clark. The general ethics is CD II/2 (prepared 1939–40, Edwards p. 90); the ethics of creation is CD III/4 (Jakobsen cites "vol. III.4, pp. 24–25").
- *Nein! Antwort an Emil Brunner* (München: C. Kaiser, 1934); English in Brunner & Barth, *Natural Theology: Comprising “Nature and Grace” by Professor Emil Brunner and the reply “No!” by Dr. Karl Barth*, trans. Peter Fraenkel, London: Geoffrey Bles, 1946 (Wood's bibliography).
- *Theological Declaration of Barmen* (1934), drafted by Barth per Torrance and Krötke; adopted by the Confessional Synod.

## In dialogue with

- **[Søren Kierkegaard](kierkegaard.md)** (earlier). Hare (2012, p. 191) on particular divine commands: "This particularism is important to the divine command theory of Søren Kierkegaard (1813-1855) and (following Kierkegaard) of Karl Barth". Edwards (p. 189) cites Barth's "adoption of Kierkegaard’s “infinite qualitative distinction”", and (p. 15, n. 34) Barth's words, via Torrance's *Karl Barth* (p. 46), on "an ancestral line which runs back through Kierkegaard to Luther and Calvin, and so to Paul and Jeremiah."
- **Emil Brunner** (contemporary; no page). The 1934 exchange on nature and grace, above; Hart (p. 289) describes "the very public disagreement between Karl Barth and Emil Brunner which reached its zenith in 1934".
- **[Paul Tillich](tillich.md)** (contemporary). Hunsinger (2018): "Karl Barth and Paul Tillich stood for two contrary ways of doing Christian theology." and "Tillich’s theology was philosophically “speculative” and “anthropocentric,” whereas Barth’s was strongly “dogmatic” and “christocentric.”" (`raw/hunsinger-2018-barth-and-tillich.md`, [doi:10.1177/0040573618785338](https://doi.org/10.1177/0040573618785338); abstract only read).
- **[Thomas Aquinas](aquinas.md)** (earlier, through readers). Edwards (abstract): "Recent scholars of Barth’s work, however, have attempted to exonerate his work, often by way of positive comparisons with the moral theology of Thomas Aquinas." He names Eugene Rogers, *Thomas Aquinas and Karl Barth* (1995), as the start of that line (p. 11).
- **Duns Scotus and William of Ockham** (earlier, as a comparison). Edwards's thesis is that "A shared “grammar” of divine command both “from above” and “from below” is demonstrated to exist in the moral theologies of John Duns Scotus, William of Ockham, and Karl Barth." (abstract). John E. Hare (2012) takes Scotus as his model divine command theorist and places Barth after Kierkegaard (p. 191).
- **Anselm** (earlier). Torrance names the Anselm study (1931); Wood (SEP) reports that "Barth had no quarrel with using philosophy in an Anselmian mode, to elucidate and clarify the implications of divine revelation".
- **Stanley Hauerwas** (later). Chignell & Pereboom: "contemporary theologian Stanley Hauerwas espouses a fideistic view in the tradition of Pascal and Barth".
- **John E. Hare** (later; not the R. M. Hare of the [thinkers](index.md) index). Jakobsen: "Both Karl Barth and John Hare take this route", that Christ gives content to the divine commands. Hare's chapter *Barth on Divine Command* in *God's Command* (2015), DOI [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199602018.003.0005](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199602018.003.0005), was verified bibliographically, not read.
- **David Novak** (later). *Karl Barth on Divine Command: A Jewish Response*, *Scottish Journal of Theology* 54(4) (2001): 463–483, DOI [10.1017/s0036930600051772](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0036930600051772) (bibliographic data only; not read).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing (Torrance, Britannica).** Barth "was one of the most influential Protestant theologians of the 20th century who radically changed Protestant thought in his emphasis of the “wholly otherness of God.”"
- **On philosophy (Wood, SEP).** Wood's reading: "Barth’s famous “No!” to philosophical reasoning about God is also best understood as a rejection of philosophical overreach rather than a rejection of philosophy per se (Brunner & Barth 1946)." and Barth, with Tertullian and Luther, seems at first "to advocate Conflict. Upon closer inspection, however, their views are closer to those in the Disjunction category." Wood reports a later use: "Sometimes, theological objections to APR simply reiterate Barthian objections to natural theology" (analytic philosophy of religion). Diller (2010), [doi:10.1111/j.1468-2265.2009.00521.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2265.2009.00521.x), is cited by Wood for this reading (not read here).
- **On the Nein! (Hart 1993).** Hart asks readers to weigh the setting: "It is important, when reading the response of Barth to Brunner in 1934 always to keep this context firmly in mind: otherwise the passion and invective may seem to be getting rather too worked up about an abstract issue of merely academic interest." (p. 292). His criticism: "My concluding contention would be that Barth, notwithstanding his strong denials, cannot, as long as he adheres to a doctrine of incarnation, or to the belief that God has revealed himself to humans, avoid positing a point of contact in this second, carefully qualified, sense." (p. 305).
- **On Barmen (Krötke 2006, p. 270).** Critical of the Declaration's scope: "The declaration is not a document of Christian resistance against a murderous ideology as such." "It contains no direct reference to the persecution of the Jews." "This is a limitation in the confession that we cannot be content with." Torrance, by contrast, reports that from 1935 "he continued to champion the cause of the Confessing Church, of the Jews, and of oppressed people generally."
- **On the command ethics — critics, as Edwards reports them.** "With the exception of Matheny, the only attention paid to Barth’s ethics prior to 1993 was critical." (p. 8). "William Schweiker, after presenting a cursory overview of the history of voluntarism, claims that many such traditional positions (such as Barth’s) are “counterintuitive” from the perspective of contemporary ethics." (pp. 8–9). "Schweiker’s concern is that an ethics of divine command cannot validate itself from within its own internal logic, but must also “assert that God is good or loving. Yet that claim, strictly speaking, cannot be formulated within the discourse of divine commands.”" (p. 104).
- **On the command ethics — defenders, as Edwards reports them.** "Biggar admits that Barth’s ethics have a strong voluntarist strain, but he goes on to demonstrate that this strain lacks consistency and that a rationalist strain appears occasionally—particularly in the later “special” ethics of creation and reconciliation." (p. 9; Nigel Biggar, *The Hastening that Waits*, 1993; "demonstrate" is Edwards's word). "McKenny’s Barth is one who struggles and goes to great length to be consistently non-voluntarist." (p. 12).
- **Edwards's own position (2016).** He holds that both camps are "inaccurate" (abstract) and that "Barth’s theological voluntarism does not rely upon an irrational good defined by the whims of an arbitrary God" (p. 104).
- **Jakobsen (2023).** Of Barth's ethics: "Although his normative and applied ethics are Christologically determined, his metaethics is not." and "Because Hare and Barth do not connect the metaethical theory to Christ, they have not shown that divine command theory qualifies as a Christian moral theory."
- **Barth and Tillich (Hunsinger 2018).** "Although the two were not without points of convergence, the “anthropocentric”/“christocentric” divergence between them was decisive. What they finally represented was the divided mind of modern theology."

## Vocabulary

- *Command of God*: in Barth's ethics as Edwards reports it, the Word of God "as a command that claims humans as created by God, reconciled by God, and redeemed by God" (Edwards p. 90, on the Münster *Ethics*); no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Divine command theory*: Hare's sense, "The central tenet of divine command theory is that what makes something right or wrong is that God commands it or forbids it." (2012, p. 187). Jakobsen and Edwards add that for Barth the good, not only the right, is constituted by the command.
- *Voluntarism*: Edwards's term for the tradition that weights "the origin of goodness on the side of the divine will (rather than reason or nature)" (p. 5); see the [Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md) page for theological voluntarism.
- *Natural theology*: what Barth rejected in 1934, in Chignell & Pereboom's account; *point of contact*: Brunner's term for a capacity in human nature for grace (Hart).
- *Dialectical theology* / *theology of crisis*: names for the work begun with *Romans*, per Torrance.

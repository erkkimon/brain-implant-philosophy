## Facts a claim depends on

- **Origin and Athens:** "Aristippus was by birth a citizen of Cyrene and, as Aeschines informs us, was drawn to Athens by the fame of Socrates." (D.L. II.65).
  Tsouna (§1): "Aristippus, son of Aritades (Suda A 3908), was a native of Cyrene and the undisputed founder of the Cyrenaic school."
- **Dates:** "On most accounts, Aristippus of Cyrene was born c. 435 BCE and joined the Socratic circle in Athens well before Socrates’ trial and execution, possibly between 415 and 410 BCE." (Tsouna §1); his activity "may have taken place between 399 BCE and c. 355 BCE (Giannantoni 1990: vol. 4, 137–139 [nota 13])." (§1).
- **State of the evidence:** "The evidence on Aristippus is second-hand and chiefly anecdotal, and it reveals relatively little about the facts of his life." (Tsouna §2).
- **Fees and Syracuse:** D.L. (II.65), citing Phanias of Eresus: "he was the first of the followers of Socrates to charge fees and to send money to his master."
  Tsouna (§2): "After Socrates’ death, he apparently remained in contact with the Socratics, taught philosophy and other subjects for a considerable fee, visited for a while the court of Dionysius in Syracuse, where he allegedly overlapped with Plato and Aeschines, and eventually founded his own school."
- **Succession:** "The disciples of Aristippus were his daughter Arete, Aethiops of Ptolemais, and Antipater of Cyrene. The pupil of Arete was Aristippus, who went by the name of mother-taught, and his pupil was Theodorus, known as the atheist, subsequently as "god."" (D.L. II.86).
  The same section names Antipater's line ending in "Hegesias, the advocate of suicide, and Anniceris, who ransomed Plato."
- **Two men of the name:** "There have been four men called Aristippus, (1) our present subject, (2) the author of a book about Arcadia, (3) the grandchild by a daughter of the first Aristippus, who was known as his mother's pupil, (4) a philosopher of the New Academy." (D.L. II.83).
  On the grandson's dates, Tsouna (§1): "There is a similar uncertainty regarding Aristippus the Younger, whose date of birth could be anywhere between c. 390 BCE and 360 BCE and whose floruit is likewise a matter of speculation."
- **Arete:** "Therefore, we know nothing about Arete’s philosophical activities or writings and cannot assess her contribution to Cyrenaic philosophy." (Tsouna §3.1).

## Problems addressed

- **Which Aristippus originated Cyrenaic doctrine** (no problem page). The
  sources divide the doctrine between founder and grandson in different ways:
  - D.L. (II.85) ascribes an end to the founder himself: "He laid down as the end the smooth motion resulting in sensation."
    He then reports separately the opinions of "Those then who adhered to the teaching of Aristippus and were known as Cyrenaics" (II.86).
  - Tsouna (§3.1): "While it is well attested that Aristippus the Younger is the author of the orthodox Cyrenaic doctrine, it is worth speculating at the outset about Arete’s own contribution to the transformation of her father’s teachings into a systematic philosophical stance."
    Her preamble: "Although Aristippus of Cyrene was not an ethical hedonist, he ascribed to pleasure far greater value than Plato or any other Socratic did."
    and "clear lines can be traced between the epistemology and ethics of Aristippus the Younger and the positions and values defended by the founder of the school."
  - The opposing ancient reading, as Tsouna (§2) reports it: "Aristippus’ focus on present pleasure, however, caused both ancient authors and modern interpreters to pigeonhole him either as an ethical hedonist positing present pleasure as the telos, moral goal, or, more rarely, as a eudaemonistic hedonist claiming that happiness understood as the collection of particular pleasures is the supreme good."
    She names Athenaeus, parts of D.L. and Lactantius for the first reading, and Xenophon, Cicero and late Epicureans for the second: Xenophon "attributes to Aristippus the goal of “living as easily and pleasantly as possible” (Mem. 2.1.9)."
    Tsouna's own assessment (§2): "In truth, these are misperceptions contradicted by the bulk of the evidence on Aristippus."
  - Carneades, as Tsouna (§4) reports, made the founder hedonism's representative: "it is Aristippus of Cyrene and not Epicurus who occupies the slot of the representative of hedonism in Carneades’ famous “division” of the possible ends of life (Cicero, De fin. 5.20), presumably because Carneades believes him to have fathered the presentism defended by his grandson."
- **The founder's view of pleasure.** Tsouna (§2) on *monochronos*: "pleasure is monochronos—a term probably coined by him and usually, if mistakenly, rendered by “momentary” or “short-lived”."
  and "whether a pleasure lasts for a longer or a shorter time, it can be enjoyed only during its actual occurrence and has no prospective or retrospective value at all (Athenaeus, Deipn. 12.544a-b)."
  D.L. (II.66): "He derived pleasure from what was present, and did not toil to procure the enjoyment of something not present".
  A saying D.L. (II.75) reports: "I have Lais, not she me; and it is not abstinence from pleasures that is best, but mastery over them without ever being worsted."
- **Hedonism of present bodily pleasure** (the Cyrenaics as D.L. reports
  them; Tsouna's "presentist hedonism", which she assigns to the grandson).
  - Pleasure and pain as motions: "They laid down that there are two states, pleasure and pain, the former a smooth, the latter a rough motion, and that pleasure does not differ from pleasure nor is one pleasure more pleasant than another." (D.L. II.86).
  - End vs happiness: "Our end is particular pleasure, whereas happiness is the sum total of all particular pleasures, in which are included both past and future pleasures." (II.87);
    "Particular pleasure is desirable for its own sake, whereas happiness is desirable not for its own sake but for the sake of particular pleasures." (II.88).
  - The argument from instinct: "That pleasure is the end is proved by the fact that from our youth up we are instinctively attracted to it, and, when we obtain it, seek for nothing more, and shun nothing so much as its opposite, pain." (II.88).
  - Bodily over mental: "However, they insist that bodily pleasures are far better than mental pleasures, and bodily pains far worse than mental pains, and that this is the reason why offenders are punished with the former." (II.90).
  - "It is sufficient even if we enjoy but each single pleasure as it comes." (II.91).
  - Tsouna (§4.1) on the grandson: "Crucially, he argues, in part on naturalistic grounds (D.L. 2.87–88), that the moral end (telos) is not happiness (eudaemonia) but the particular pleasure presently deriving from each action."
    Her reconstruction: "Ethically, only present pleasures can have intrinsic value, since only present pleasures, strictly speaking, exist."
    and "Their claim is that the value of happiness is derivative and dependent on the individual pleasures constituting a happy life (D.L. 2.87–88)."
    Against the charge that this rules out planning, Tsouna's assessment: "Considered in this light, Cyrenaic presentism cannot justifiably be accused of precluding rational planning (see Graver 2002; O’Keefe 2002; Sedley 2017; Warren 2001 & 2014: 175–210)."
- **[Paradox of hedonism](../problems/paradox-of-hedonism.md).** Martínez & Labiano (2022, *Graeco-Latina Brunensia* 27(2), DOI [10.5817/glb2022-2-5](https://doi.org/10.5817/glb2022-2-5); abstract only, `raw/crossref-paradox-of-hedonism-analysis-2005-2006-glb-2022.md`) argue that "central claims of the Cyrenaics' hedonism – specifically that only the experience of present pleasure has intrinsic value, and that we should pursue whatever brings pleasure now – make their ethical position especially vulnerable to this paradox."
- **What can be known.** "They affirm that mental affections can be known, but not the objects from which they come; and they abandoned the study of nature because of its apparent uncertainty, but fastened on logical inquiries because of their utility." (D.L. II.92).
  Tsouna (preamble): "According to their central epistemological position, we can only apprehend our own pathê (undergoings, affects) but cannot know anything about what typically causes the pathê, namely objects in the world."
  She credits this to the grandson, not the founder (§2): "There is no evidence, however, indicating that Aristippus of Cyrene was interested in epistemology in its own right or held epistemological views of his own (contra, Rowe 2015)."
- **Convention and justice.** "They also held that nothing is just or honourable or base by nature, but only by convention and custom." (D.L. II.93).

## In dialogue with

- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** — teacher. Tsouna (preamble): "Not only is it well attested that Aristippus of Cyrene (born c. 435 BCE) was a close associate of Socrates, but also the school that he founded after Socrates’ death was headed in turn by himself, his daughter Arete, and his grandson, Aristippus the Younger or the Mother-taught, who, according to the doxographical tradition, is the leader of the mainstream branch of the school."
  On ethics as his subject, Tsouna (§2) cites Aristotle: "it is attested that he followed Socrates in turning away from other subjects, such as mathematics and physics (Aristotle, Metaph. B2 996a29 ff.), in order to concentrate on ethics."
- **[Plato](plato.md)** — D.L. (II.65) reports Plato among his critics ("so does Plato in the dialogue On the Soul"). Tsouna (§1): "Aristippus of Cyrene is shown to interact with Plato, Aeschines, and Antisthenes; Aristippus the Younger had some sort of intellectual relation to Plato;"
  On the "subtler thinkers" of the *Theaetetus*, Tsouna (§2) rejects a proposal by Rowe (2015): "Nor, in contrast to what has been suggested (Rowe 2015), is there any reason to think that Plato is drawing material from Aristippus when he introduces the theory of the “subtler thinkers” in the Theaetetus."
- **Xenophon** — "Xenophon was no friend to Aristippus; and for this reason he has made Socrates direct against Aristippus the discourse in which he denounces pleasure." (D.L. II.65).
- **[Epicurus](epicurus.md)** — the contrast as the sources report it.
  - D.L. (II.87), citing Panaetius: "However, the bodily pleasure which is the end is, according to Panaetius in his work On the Sects, not the settled pleasure following the removal of pains, or the sort of freedom from discomfort which Epicurus accepts and maintains to be the end."
  - "The removal of pain, however, which is put forward in Epicurus, seems to them not to be pleasure at all, any more than the absence of pleasure is pain." (II.89);
    "Nor again do they admit that pleasure is derived from the memory or expectation of good, which was a doctrine of Epicurus." (II.89).
  - Konstan reports the other side: "The philosophical school known as the Cyrenaics advocated increasing desires and seeking ever new ways of gratifying them."
    "Epicurus objected that such pleasures are necessarily accompanied by distress, for they depend upon a lack that is painful"
    and of Epicurus' catastematic pleasure: "The Cyrenaics and others, such as Cicero, maintained, in turn, that this condition is not pleasurable but rather neutral — neither pleasurable nor painful."
  - Tsouna (§4): "Notwithstanding significant differences between the various branches of the school, interpreters of all times contrast the presentist hedonism generally attributed to the Cyrenaics with the eudaemonistic hedonism of Epicurus and his followers."
    Chronology: "Chronologically, Aristippus the Younger must have developed his presentist ethics decades before Epicurus began his professional career, while Epicurus was a close contemporary of Theodorus, Hegesias, and Anniceris."
  - Anniceris (Tsouna §4.2.1) "defended the main tenets of his school against Epicurus (PHerc. 1005 fr. 111; PHerc. 418 frs. 5.8, 6.9–13; Plutarch, adv. Col. 1120C–1121E; Strabo, Geog. 17.3.22), who deliberately opposed the presentism of the orthodox Cyrenaics on several counts (Tsouna 2016)."
    and "Moreover, Anniceris resisted Epicurus’ two-pronged position that mental pleasure is superior to bodily pleasure and, nonetheless, every mental pleasure originates in the body."
- **The later Cyrenaic sects.** Hegesias: "They denied the possibility of happiness, for the body is infected with much suffering, while the soul shares in the sufferings of the body and is a prey to disturbance, and fortune often disappoints." (D.L. II.94).
  Anniceris: "The school of Anniceris in other respects agreed with them, but admitted that friendship and gratitude and respect for parents do exist in real life, and that a good man will sometimes act out of patriotic motives." (II.96).
  Theodorus: "Theodorus was a man who utterly rejected the current belief in the gods." (II.97).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Hostile and favourable portraits.** Tsouna (preamble): "While hostile authors represent Aristippus of Cyrene as an amoral voluptuary, our most reliable sources attest that he was a man of many gifts and talents, who defended core Socratic values and integrated them successfully into his distinctive philosophical outlook."
- **On the canonical doctrine and Socrates.** Tsouna (preamble): "As for the presentist hedonism articulated by his grandson and presented as the canonical Cyrenaic doctrine, it appears prima facie incompatible with the eudaemonistic ethics of Socrates and his followers."
- **Cicero**, as Tsouna (§5) reports: "Cicero’s verdict is that, even though both doctrines ought to be rejected because they insult the dignity of human beings, nonetheless Aristippus defended his views in a clearer and more consistent way than Epicurus (De fin. 1.23)."
- **Plutarch**, as Tsouna (§5) reports: "Namely, he claims that the Cyrenaics are better philosophers than the Epicureans, first, because they realise the sceptical implications of their epistemological doctrine whereas the Epicureans do not (adv. Col. 1120d–1121b), next because they can account for the existence of aesthetic pleasures whereas the Epicureans cannot (Quaest. conv. 5.1 [674a–b]), and also because they have ways of tempering the intensity of sexual pleasures whereas the Epicureans have not (Non posse … 1089a–b)."
- **Philodemus** (author of PHerc. 1251, "probably Philodemus of Gadara" per Tsouna §5): "If we can only know our pathê, he argues, there can be no rational justification for our choices."
- **Modern.** Tsouna (§5): "Eventually, the Cyrenaics seem to disappear from the philosophical stage, though resonances of their ethical views are occasionally discernible in the Renaissance and the modern era and become vibrant in the utilitarian tradition, especially the writings of Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill, and Henry Sidgwick."
  and "Cyrenaic presentism is resurrected, reinterpreted, and developed by the nineteenth century academic and novelist Walter Pater, constitutes a point of reference for the agenda of Fred Feldman, and inspires the “embodied hedonism” of Michel Onfray (see Lampe 2015: 168–192)."
- **Paradox of hedonism.** Martínez & Labiano (2022, abstract) contrast the Cyrenaics with Epicurus, who they say can "avoid the negative conclusion of this paradox" (see Problems addressed).

## Vocabulary

- *Pathos / pathê* ("undergoings, affects", Tsouna preamble): the states the
  Cyrenaics hold knowable; no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Monochronos* ("unitemporal" in Tsouna §2, against the usual rendering
  "momentary").
- *Telos* vs *eudaimonia*: the Cyrenaics as D.L. (II.87) reports them separate
  the end (particular pleasure) from happiness (the sum of pleasures).
- *Smooth / rough motion*: pleasure and pain (D.L. II.86); *kinetic* vs
  *catastematic* pleasure is Konstan's description of Epicurus' distinction.

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "He was born in Chengdu, western China, where his parents, Jessie (nee Browne) and Norman Parfit practised preventive medicine in Christian missionary hospitals." O'Grady's dateline: "Derek Antony Parfit, philosopher, born 11 December 1942; died 1 January 2017"
- **Death, date in dispute between the sources:** O'Grady gives 1 January 2017; McMahan writes that Parfit "died unexpectedly on the second day of the new year,".
- **Education:** "He was educated at Eton, and won the top history scholarship of his year to read history at Balliol College, Oxford (1961-64)." (O'Grady). McMahan: "His undergraduate degree was in modern history and was the only academic degree he ever received."
- **Into philosophy:** "After graduating, he held a Harkness fellowship for two years at Columbia and Harvard universities, then, in 1967, gained a prize fellowship to All Souls, where he changed from history to philosophy." (O'Grady).
- **Posts:** "A senior research fellow of All Souls College, Oxford (1984-2010), Parfit was a visiting professor at Harvard, Rutgers and New York University, and elected a fellow of the British Academy (1986)." (O'Grady). McMahan: he stayed at All Souls "until he became 67, when he was required by the university’s mandatory retirement policy to leave both the college and the faculty of philosophy."
- **Prize:** "In 2014 he received philosophy’s equivalent of a Nobel prize, the Rolf Schock prize in logic and philosophy." (O'Grady's wording).
- **Marriage:** "He met Janet in 1982, and they married in 2010." — the philosopher Janet Radcliffe Richards (O'Grady).
- **Giving:** O'Grady reports him as "a member of the effective altruism movement, which enjoins everyone to give 10% of their income to charity."

## Problems addressed

- **[The non-identity problem](../problems/non-identity-problem.md)** (*Reasons and Persons* Part Four, ch. 16, §§119–127; pp. 351–379 per Shoemaker). Shoemaker: "It is the non-identity problem, first discussed in detail in Parfit 1984, 351–379." Parfit's case of the fourteen-year-old girl: "We cannot claim that this girl’s decision was worse for her child. What is the objection to her decision? This question arises because, in the different outcomes, different people would be born." (§122). On Depletion: "Some believe that what is bad must be bad for someone. On this view, there is no objection to our choice." and "Certain writers accept this conclusion.* But it is very implausible." (§123). His aim: "Call what we ought to accept Theory X." (§122); "We can predict that Theory X will not take a person-affecting form." (§127). Credit for the problem: Kavka (1982, p. 93) names "a surprising argument, discovered in-dependently by Robert M. Adams, Derek Parfit, and Thomas Schwartz,,"; Parfit's n. 10: "10 I follow ADAMS (3)." The problem page sets out the replies (rights, harm, bite the bullet) with their owners.
- **[The repugnant conclusion](../problems/repugnant-conclusion.md)** (Part Four, ch. 17, §131; p. 388 per Zuber et al. 2021). "The Repugnant Conclusion: For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better, even though its members have lives that are barely worth living." (§131).
  Arrhenius, Ryberg & Tännsjö ([SEP "The Repugnant Conclusion"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/repugnant-conclusion/), §1): "However, Parfit’s own conclusion was that he had not succeeded in developing such a theory." but "this did not seem to destroy his hopes that such a theory could be found (Parfit 1984 p. 451)." (§2.7). On lexical views (§2.2): "However, some theorists—including Parfit—have suggested that this axiological assumption is mistaken. (Parfit 1986, 2016; Griffin 1986; Rachels 2001; Crisp 1988, 1992; Glover 1977; Edwards 1979; Lemos 1993; Skorupski 1999; Klint Jensen 1996; Portmore 1999; Riley 1993, 1999, 2008, 2009)."
  Later revision, as Zuber et al. ([Utilitas 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095382082100011X), §3) report it: "Late in his career, Parfit revised his prior arguments regarding the Repugnant Conclusion, calling his prior reasoning a “mistake” on the grounds that “We cannot justifiably reject strong arguments merely by claiming that their conclusions are implausible” (Parfit 2017: 154)." ([Parfit 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/papa.12088), not read.)
- **Personal identity and what matters** (Part Three, chs. 10–13). "The rival view is that personal identity is not what matters." and "What matters is Relation R: psychological connectedness and/or continuity, with the right kind of cause." (§79). His first conclusion: "(1) We are not separately existing entities, apart from our brains and bodies, and various interrelated physical and mental events." (§79).
  Shoemaker (§2.5): "He is a “reductionist,” according to which the facts about persons and personal identity consist in more particular facts about brains, bodies, and series of interrelated mental and physical events (Parfit 1984, 210–211)." The argument is from fission: "By way of explanation, consider the case Parfit uses in support of his claim that identity is not what matters: fission (Ibid., 254–255)."; "Everything that matters in ordinary survival (or nearly everything), therefore, is preserved in fission, despite the fact that the identity relation is not." Olson (§1): "Parfit (1971, 1984: 215, 1995) and Martin (1998) argue against." the importance of identity; (§5) "Fission is death. (Shoemaker 1984: 85, Parfit 1984: 207; 2012: 6f., Unger 1990: 265)."
  No problem page on personal identity exists yet; Williams's view is on [Bernard Williams](williams.md).
- **[The ship of Theseus](../problems/ship-of-theseus.md).** Deutsch & Garbacz ([SEP "Relative Identity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/identity-relative/), §2.5), on the reply that identity is not what matters: "For the case of personal identity, Parfit (1984) develops such a response in detail. A related reaction would be to claim that if both competitors have equal claim to be the original, then neither is the original."
- **Rationality and time** (Part Two). The case of a man with Future-Tuesday-Indifference: "But he never cares about possible pains or pleasures on a future Tuesday. Thus he would choose a painful operation on the following Tuesday rather than a much less painful operation on the following Wednesday." (§46). The conclusion: "Part Two argued that we should reject the Self-interest Theory about rationality." and "We should accept the Critical Present-aim Theory, or CP." (§150). Shoemaker (§2.5) on the link to identity: "Thus, I may be justified in caring much less about my MLS than about my tomorrow's self." (MLS: Much Later Self), and "Great imprudence like this, in other words, would be immoral (Parfit 1984, 318–320)."
- **Self-defeating and self-effacing theories** (Part One). "But to be self-effacing is not to be self-defeating. It is not the aim of a theory to be believed." and "That a theory is self-effacing does not show that it is not the best theory." (§9).
- **[The prisoner's dilemma](../problems/prisoners-dilemma.md).** Kuhn ([SEP "Prisoner’s Dilemma"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/prisoner-dilemma/), §6), on single-person readings: "One such interpretation, elucidated in Quinn, derives from an example of Parfit's." and "We can view the situation here as a multi-player PD in which each “player” is the temporal stage of a single person."
- **[The demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md).** Hurka ([SEP "Moral Demands and Permissions/Prerogatives"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-demands-permissions/)) lists Parfit 1984 among the "indirect" or "two-level" consequentialists ("(e.g., Bales 1971; Hare 1981; Parfit 1984)", §2), credits him with a name: "The result is an “agent-relative permission” (Parfit 1978; Davis 1980) or “agent-centred prerogative” (Scheffler 1982) not to make certain sacrifices." (§4), and cites a one-stage weighing view to "(Parfit 2011: 137–41)." (§4).
- **[The experience machine](../problems/experience-machine.md)** (theories of well-being). Crisp ([SEP "Well-Being"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/well-being/), §4.3): "The threefold distinction I am using between different theories of well-being has become standard in contemporary ethics (Parfit 1984: app. I)."
- **[The trolley problem](../problems/trolley-problem.md).** Woollard ([SEP "Doing vs. Allowing Harm"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/doing-allowing/), §3) reports that "Derek Parfit (2017) uses the bystander trolley case in this way, arguing that considering such cases shows that the fact that a threat is heading towards one group of people rather than another gives no reason not to redirect the threat." and Kamm's (2020) reply (on the problem page).

## In dialogue with

- **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (earlier). Schultz ([SEP "Henry Sidgwick"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/sidgwick/), §3): "Parfit, too, argued that Sidgwick’s two standpoints approach failed to capture how egoistic reasons can be weaker than omnipersonal ones (Parfit 2011a,b), though Smith (2009) has defended Sidgwick’s construction of the problem." and "Indeed, Parfit called his deeply Sidgwickian view a form of non-naturalistic, non-metaphysical moral rationalism." On population, the repugnant-conclusion entry (§1) says "Henry Sidgwick was close to acknowledging the implication" (see the [problem page](../problems/repugnant-conclusion.md)).
- **[John Rawls](rawls.md)** (contemporary). Shoemaker: "The objection he is concerned to refute is Rawls' famous “separateness of persons” charge, the contention that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons, because it controversially jettisons interpersonal distributive principles in exactly the way we uncontroversially jettison them intrapersonally (Rawls 1971, 22–27)." Shoemaker's assessment of Parfit's reply: "The success of arguments for this conclusion actually depends on the specific version of reductionism being advanced." (citing Jeske 1993, Brink 1997a). On moral progress Parfit writes: "Like Rawls and Nagel’, I believe the opposite." (§154; see [Thomas Nagel](nagel.md)).
- **[Immanuel Kant](kant.md)** and **T. M. Scanlon** (the convergence argument). Ashford & Mulgan (§5): "In On What Matters, Derek Parfit argues that rule consequentialism, contractualism, and the best interpretation of Kant’s moral theory all coincide." Its formula: "Kantian rule consequentialism: Everyone ought to follow the optimific principles, because these are the only principles that everyone could rationally will to be universal laws. (Parfit 2011, volume 1, p. 411)" Scanlon's reply: "Scanlon observes that “the degree to which Parfit’s conclusion should seem surprising depends to a certain extent on how close the theories he is discussing are to Kant’s” (Scanlon 2011, p. 117)."; and (§13) that "any impersonal reformulation of contractualism is much more demanding than the original formulation of contractualism (Scanlon 2011)."
- **[Bernard Williams](williams.md)** (contemporary). O'Grady: "Another moral philosopher, his friend Bernard Williams, said that their plausibility relied on the external, third-person way in which they were presented." ("their": Parfit's theories).
- **David Lewis, Theodore Sider, Marya Schechtman** (identity). Shoemaker (§2.5): "While there are plausible alternative reactions to fission that maintain the importance of the identity relation (see, e.g., Lewis 1976, Sider 2001a)"; Olson (§5): "(Lewis 1976 and Parfit 1976 debate whether the multiple-occupancy view can preserve the conviction that identity is what matters practically.)" Shoemaker contrasts methods: "Parfit asks the first-person question, “What matters to me in fission?”" while "Schechtman, on the other hand, asks the third-person question, about how such fission products would be treated, which allows for their being creatures who are indeed different from us."
- **Thomas Reid** (earlier). Shoemaker (§1): "(And Reid's objection in particular anticipates Derek Parfit's “Extreme Claim,” to be discussed later.)"
- **Robert M. Adams, Gregory Kavka, Thomas Schwartz** (non-identity; see the problem page). Parfit's n. 9: "This problem has been called by Kavka The Paradox of Future Individuals. See KAVKA (4)."
- **Michael Huemer** and the 2021 joint statement (repugnant conclusion). Huemer ([Mind 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzn079), abstract): "I defend the ‘Repugnant’ Conclusion that for any possible population of happy people, a population containing a sufficient number of people with lives barely worth living would be better." Zuber et al. (§3): "Parfit might never have agreed with our claims 1, 2, and 3, but we agree with him that conclusions that appear implausible are sometimes true."
- **[Peter Singer](singer.md)** (contemporary), on the books' standing (Reception); **[Jonathan Dancy](dancy.md)** edited *Reading Parfit* (Blackwell, 1997; Shoemaker's bibliography).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing.** McMahan: "Derek Parfit, who died unexpectedly on the second day of the new year, was one of the most important philosophers of the past half century and, in the view of many, the single best moral philosopher in more than a century." McMahan reports Singer: "Peter Singer has described Reasons and Persons as the best work of moral philosophy since Sidgwick’s Methods of Ethics, which was published in 1874." Schultz (preamble) lists Parfit among those who "have acknowledged Sidgwick’s Methods as a vital source for their arguments."
- **Population ethics.** McMahan: "While each of the four parts has been enormously influential, Part Four effectively created a new, difficult, highly important, and now flourishing area of philosophy known as ‘population ethics’." Roberts ([SEP "The Nonidentity Problem"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/nonidentity-problem/), §1): "Still, Parfit’s early assessment of the conclusion as “repugnant” continues to be the dominant view in moral philosophy writ large, and the repugnant conclusion, together with the nonidentity problem, continues to define the basic contours of the contemporary controversy surrounding the person affecting intuition." Against treating it as decisive, the 29 authors of Zuber et al. (claim 1): "The fact that an approach to population ethics (an axiology or a social ordering) entails the Repugnant Conclusion is not sufficient to conclude that the approach is inadequate."
- **Identity: revisionary or not.** Shoemaker (§2.5): "Parfit's theory has often been called “revisionary,” in part because of moves like this one (see, e.g., Rovane 1998, 11; Martin 1998, 15)." His own suggestion: "This suggests, then, that Parfit's view is less revisionary than revelatory:". Parfit, on believing it: "Is the truth depressing? Some may find it so. But I find it liberating, and consoling." (§95). O'Grady reports a concession: "An obvious objection to his theories, Parfit admitted, is the psychological impossibility of viscerally feeling as selfless as they assert we are, or ought to be, which renders them effectively unbelievable."
- **Self-effacing theories.** Schultz (SEP "Henry Sidgwick", §2.2), on the de Lazari-Radek and Singer defence of esoteric morality: "Their argument owes much to Parfit’s demonstration that an ethical theory could be self-effacing without being self-defeating, incoherent or untrue (Parfit 1984)." ("demonstration" is Schultz's word).
- **On What Matters, for and against.** Chappell: "Derek Parfit’s On What Matters is arguably the most important work in moral philosophy since Reasons and Persons." and, critically, "He says much less about why he thinks consequentialists should be moved towards his (rule-consequentialist) triple theory." and on the metaethics: "Parfit’s criticisms are by and large “external” ones, depending on assumptions that his opponents will not share." Ashford & Mulgan (§5): "Proponents of individualist formulations of contractualism might reply that, in some key cases, Parfit’s intuitions are not neutral benchmarks, but rather reflect the considered moral judgements of a consequentialist mind." and "Parfit’s convergence argument has not attracted as much attention as other aspects of either his work or Scanlon’s. (One exception is Kumar 2011.)" O'Grady: "Philosophers are divided as to how successful he was in either task, but a huge literature on the book anticipated its publication, and volume 2 contains objections by four eminent philosophers, with Parfit’s rejoinders."

## Vocabulary

- *Relation R*: "psychological connectedness and/or continuity, with the right kind of cause." (§79); used where the implant would otherwise say "survival". No vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Reductionist View*: about persons, not about ethics — the facts about persons consist in facts about brains, bodies and events (Shoemaker §2.5). *Moderate* and *Extreme Claim*: Parfit's names for whether R grounds our concerns or nothing does (Shoemaker §2.5).
- *Self-effacing* vs. *self-defeating*: a theory that tells us to believe another theory is self-effacing; this "is not to be self-defeating" (§9).
- *Repugnant* in "the Repugnant Conclusion": Parfit's name for a conclusion he found unacceptable (Arrhenius et al., preamble: "As the name indicates, Parfit finds the Repugnant Conclusion unacceptable and many philosophers agree."), not an implant verdict; on whether it is a [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md), see the problem page.
- *Triple theory*: the conjunction "optimific, uniquely universally willable, and not reasonably rejectable" (Ashford & Mulgan §5); *optimific*: in O'Grady's paraphrase of the related Kantian rule consequentialism, the principles "whose universal acceptance would make things go best".

## Facts a claim depends on

All from the MIT obituary written by Alex Byrne with colleagues
([MIT News, 4 Dec 2020](https://news.mit.edu/2020/professor-emerita-judith-jarvis-thomson-influential-philosopher-dies-1204);
excerpt: `raw/mit-news-2020-judith-jarvis-thomson-obituary.md`).

- **Death.** "died on Nov. 20 at her home in Cambridge, Massachusetts. She was 91."
  The obituary gives no birth date. Arithmetic only: age 91 on 20 November
  2020 places the birth between 21 November 1928 and 20 November 1929.
- **Education.** BA Barnard College 1950; then Newnham College, Cambridge, on a
  Fulbright Scholarship, where John Wisdom, "a follower of Wittgenstein", was
  her supervisor; PhD Columbia, "graduating in 1959". The obituary reports
  that she "later came to reject completely Wittgenstein’s view of philosophy".
- **Posts.** MIT associate professor 1964, full professor 1969, retired 2004;
  president of the APA Eastern Division 1992–93; Tanner Lectures (Princeton,
  1999) and Carus Lectures (APA, 2003), the latter the basis of *Normativity*
  (2010).

## Problems addressed

- **[The ethics of abortion](../problems/abortion.md).** Thomson grants the
  fetus personhood for the argument's sake — "I propose, then, that we grant that the fetus is a person from the moment of conception"
  (1971, p. 48) — and answers with the violinist: "You wake up in the morning and find yourself back to back in bed with an unconscious violinist."
  (pp. 48–49). Her thesis: "the right to life consists not in the right not to be killed, but rather in the right not to be killed unjustly."
  (p. 57). She limits her conclusion: "I am inclined to think it a merit of my account precisely that it does not give a general yes or a general no."
  (p. 65), and "A very early abortion is surely not the killing of a person, and so is not dealt with by anything I have said here."
  (p. 66). Excerpt: `raw/thomson-1971-defense-of-abortion-violinist.md`.
  Duties to aid are split between "the Good Samaritan and what we might call the Minimally Decent Samaritan" (p. 62).
- **[The trolley problem](../problems/trolley-problem.md), 1976–1985.**
  Starting from Foot's driver case ("Some years ago, Philippa Foot drew attention to an extraordinarily interesting problem.", 1985, p. 1395),
  she introduces "a case I will call Bystander at the Switch" (p. 1397) and
  holds that "an ordinary person, a mere bystander, may intervene in such a case" (p. 1397).
  The name is hers: "Since I find it particularly puzzling that the bystander may turn his trolley, I am inclined to call this The Trolley Problem."
  (p. 1401). She adds the Fat Man case — "Everybody to whom I have put this case says it would not be. But why?"
  (p. 1409) — and proposes a "distributive exemption" (p. 1408). Excerpt:
  `raw/thomson-1985-the-trolley-problem-yale-law-journal.md`.
- **The trolley problem, 2008 reversal.** In "Turning the Trolley" she adds a
  third option, "(iii) throw the switch to the left, killing himself." (p. 364),
  argues that the bystander who turns the trolley makes "the one workman pay the cost of his good deed because he doesn’t feel like paying it himself"
  (p. 365), and concludes that the bystander "is no more free to turn the trolley than I am to shove the fat man off the footbridge into the path of the trolley"
  (p. 368). She endorses Foot's weighting of negative over positive duties
  ("I find myself strongly inclined to think they were right.", p. 372) while stating: "I know of no thoroughly convincing account of its source, and regard the need for one as among the most pressing in all of moral theory."
  (p. 372). Excerpt: `raw/thomson-2008-turning-the-trolley.md`. Where this
  places her among readings of [double effect](../arguments/double-effect-conditions.md):
  McIntyre (SEP 2024, §4.5) puts "Thomson, 2008, contradicting Thomson 1976 and 1985"
  in the group arguing "that it would be wrong for a bystander to switch the trolley"
  (excerpt: `raw/sep-double-effect-and-doing-allowing-fall-2024-trolley.md`).
- **Privacy (no page yet).** Roessler & DeCew (SEP 2024, §2.1) report that
  "Ultimately the right to privacy, on Thomson’s view, is merely a cluster of rights"
  (Thomson, "The Right to Privacy", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 4(4), 1975,
  pp. 295–314, as their bibliography gives it).
- **Self-defense (no page yet).** Frowe & Parry (SEP 2024, §1.2): "The causal account of liability is most closely associated with Judith Jarvis Thomson."
  (Thomson, "Self-Defense", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 20(4), 1991,
  pp. 283–310, as their bibliography gives it).
- **[Moral luck](../problems/moral-luck.md).** Nelkin (SEP 2024, §4.1.1) lists
  Thomson among those who hold that "What is not appropriate is to offer different moral assessments of their behavior (e.g., Rosebury, Richards, Wolf, Thomson)."
  (excerpt: `raw/sep-moral-luck-fall-2024-nelkin-map.md`).

## In dialogue with

- **[Philippa Foot](foot.md)** (trolley). Thomson's bystander case answers
  Foot's driver case; Woollard (SEP 2024, §3) reports that it "undermines Foot’s claim that the difference between doing and allowing explains our intuitions about these cases".
  In 2008 Thomson returns to Foot's duty asymmetry (p. 372, above).
- **Foot** (abortion). Hacker-Wright (SEP 2024, §4) reports: "Foot argues that the analogy has a devastating fault."
- **Alexander Friedman** (trolley). Thomson (2008, p. 363) reports his
  unpublished MIT thesis: "He said: it just isn’t true that the bystander may choose option (ii) in Bystander’s Two Options!"
- **William J. Fitzpatrick** (trolley). Woollard (§3): "Fitzpatrick (2009) has responded to Thomson by casting doubt on the analogy between stealing money to give to charity and turning the trolley on a stranger rather than oneself."
  ("Thomson's turnabout on the trolley", *Analysis* 69(4), 636–643,
  [doi 10.1093/analys/anp091](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/anp091); not read).
- **[G. E. M. Anscombe](anscombe.md)** (trolley). McIntyre (§4.5) places
  "Anscombe, 1982" in the same group as Thomson 2008.
- **Mary Anne Warren** (abortion). Warren (1973, ¶13) holds that "it is only in the case of pregnancy due to rape that the woman’s situation is adequately analogous to the violinist case for our intuitions about the latter to transfer convincingly."
  (excerpt: `raw/warren-1973-moral-and-legal-status-of-abortion-personhood.md`).
- **Thomas Scanlon and Raymond Geuss** (privacy). Roessler & DeCew (§2.1):
  "This reductionist view was quickly discarded by Thomas Scanlon"; "Raymond Geuss (2001), on the other hand, seconds Thomson’s reductionist line of criticism but adds to it a more fundamental consideration".
- **McMahan, Otsuka, Kamm and others** (self-defense). Frowe & Parry (§1.2): "Several commentators argue that Thomson’s view entails, implausibly, that falling stones can violate rights (McMahan 1994: 276, 2005a: 388; Otsuka 1994: 80; Zohar 1993: 608; Rodin 2002: 85–87)."
  and "There seems to be at least some scope for Thomson to resist this move (Kamm 1992: 47)."

## Reception

Assessments are listed as published, for and against, not weighed.

- **Of the violinist, for.** Brown & Fehige (SEP 2024, §2): "Judith Thomson provided one of the most striking and effective thought experiments in the moral realm (see Thomson 1971)."
  and it "is effective in distinguishing two concepts that had previously been run together: “right to life” and “right to what is needed to sustain life.”"
  Gordon (IEP, "Abortion", §3g) calls it "her landmark article" (excerpt:
  `raw/iep-abortion-gordon-three-views-and-arguments.md`).
- **Of the violinist, against.** Warren's rape-only transfer (above); Foot's
  "devastating fault" (as Hacker-Wright reports it); Brown & Fehige note the
  option to "dismiss her thought experiment as a useless fiction", citing Dancy 1985 as a critic of thought experiments in ethics.
- **Of the trolley work.** The MIT obituary (Byrne et al.) states that she
  "devised the standard form of the “trolley problem.”" and that "Thomson’s examples have generated a vast “trolleyology” literature, spanning disciplines including psychology and neuroscience."
  McIntyre (§4.5) and Woollard (§3) record her own 2008 reversal of the
  1976/1985 verdict; Fitzpatrick 2009 replies to the reversal (above).
- **Of the privacy and self-defense work.** Roessler & DeCew call hers "Probably the most famous fundamental critique of a right to privacy"
  (§2.1); Scanlon's and Geuss's responses and the falling-stones objection
  are listed above.
- **Of her work overall.** The obituary says she "transformed the philosophical discussion of abortion"
  and describes "six highly regarded books". Richard Holton, as quoted there: "she has had an enormous influence on fellow faculty and on generations of graduate students who now populate the philosophy departments of the English-speaking world."
  Alex Byrne, as quoted there: "But the idea that Judy might change her mind was completely unexceptional, not noteworthy at all, because she was so intellectually honest and would follow the argument wherever it led."
  No published assessment against her work overall was found in the sources read.

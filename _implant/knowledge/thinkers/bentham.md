## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and death:** "Jeremy Bentham was born on 15 February 1748 and died on 6 June 1832 in London." (Crimmins §1).
- **Family:** "He was the elder son of an attorney, Jeremiah Bentham (1712–92) and his first wife, Alicia Whitehorn (d. 1759), and brother to Samuel (1757–1831), a naval architect and diplomat." (§1).
- **Education:** "Westminster School (1755–60) and Queen’s College, Oxford (BA 1763, MA 1766)." (§1). "He returned briefly to Oxford in 1763–64 to attend lectures given by William Blackstone, the first Vinerian Professor of English Law," (§1).
- **Law and the utility principle, 1769:** "Bentham was called to the Bar in 1769, but his legal career lasted only one brief. In that year he discovered the utility principle and related ideas in the writings of Hume, Helvétius and Beccaria and chose instead a career dedicated to analytic jurisprudence, law reform, and social and political improvement." (§1).
- **"Utilitarian", 1781:** Crimmins reports that Bentham "coined the name “utilitarian” in recording a dream he had while a guest at the country estate of his patron, the Whig politician William Petty, 2nd Earl of Shelburne (1737–1805)." (§1). "The term “international” was coined by Bentham." (§1).
- **France:** "Bentham’s efforts to stimulate reform in France were recognised in the granting of honorary citizenship in September 1792." (§1).
- **James Mill:** "association with James Mill, whom he met in late 1808, and who for many years thereafter acted as his philosophical and political aide-de-camp." (§1). J. S. Mill edited Bentham's evidence manuscripts, "published by J.S. Mill as Rationale of Judicial Evidence (1827)." (§1).
- **Circle:** "In December 1823 he provided funds to start the Westminster Review, a periodical dedicated to radical views." His associates "later came to be known as the “philosophic radicals” (a coinage of J.S. Mill’s), though in reality the group was far less cohesive than is sometimes thought (Thomas 1979)." (§1).
- **Death and remains:** "Bentham never married, and died in the company of friends on the eve of the signing of the Great Reform Act." "He requested that his own mummified head and skeleton, dressed in his habitual garments, be displayed, and it can still be viewed today at University College London." (§1).

## Problems addressed

- **The principle of utility** (IPML ch. I). The book opens: "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do." (ch. I, para. I). "The principle of utility recognises this subjection, and assumes it for the foundation of that system, the object of which is to rear the fabric of felicity by the hands of reason and of law." (para. I). The definition: "By the principle of utility is meant that principle which approves or disapproves of every action whatsoever, according to the tendency which it appears to have to augment or diminish the happiness of the party whose interest is in question" — "not only of every action of a private individual, but of every measure of government." (para. II). A note of July 1822 records the later name: "To this denomination has of late been added, or substituted, the greatest happiness or greatest felicity principle".
  Crimmins (§3) reads the opening passage as expressing "two forms of hedonism": "(1) psychological hedonism, which states that all motives of action are grounded in the apprehension of pain or the desire for pleasure; and (2) ethical hedonism, which holds that pleasure is the only good and actions are right in so far as they tend to produce pleasure or avoid pain." Earlier, in the *Fragment on Government* (1776), Crimmins (§1) reports the "fundamental axiom" that "it is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong",
- **Measuring pleasure and pain: the felicific calculus** (IPML ch. IV). "Pleasures and pains are the instruments he has to work with: it behoves him therefore to understand their force, which is again, in other words, their value." (ch. IV, para. I; "he" is the legislator). The circumstances are intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, propinquity or remoteness (para. II), then "5. Its fecundity, or the chance it has of being followed by sensations of the same kind: that is, pleasures, if it be a pleasure: pains, if it be a pain." and "6. Its purity, or the chance it has of not being followed by sensations of the opposite kind: that is, pains, if it be a pleasure: pleasures, if it be a pain." (para. III), and for several people "7. Its extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it." (para. IV). A later note gives memory verses: "Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure— Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure."
  Crimmins (§3.2): "Though Bentham did not use this terminology, the calculus he devised—commonly known as the “felicific calculus”—describes the elements or dimensions of the value of a pain or pleasure." On practicability: "it has often been said that applying the felicific calculus is impractical. Bentham recognised that neither the individual nor the legislator could strictly follow the process he described. Rather, he presented it as a model of the ideal calculation," (§3.2).
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md)** (IPML ch. XVII §1, para. IV and note). Animals "stand degraded into the class of things." The note: "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" Bentham separates killing from tormenting: "If the being eaten were all, there is very good reason why we should be suffered to eat such of them as we like to eat" but "is there any reason why we should be suffered to torment them? Not any that I can see." Crimmins (§3): "the proposed penal code was to include a section on cruelty to animals", and the remark is "a proposition that lies at the beginnings of utilitarian arguments for the ethical treatment of animals (see Singer 1975)." Gruen and Monsó (SEP "The Moral Status of Animals", §1.4) call him "one of the more forceful defenders of this sentientist view of moral considerability" (`raw/sep-moral-animal-fall-2024-moral-status.md`). See [sentience](../vocabulary/sentience.md) and [moral patient](../vocabulary/moral-patient.md).
- **[The justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md)** (IPML ch. XIII). "But all punishment is mischief : all punishment in itself is evil. Upon the principle of utility, if it ought at all to be admitted, it ought only to be admitted in as far as it promises to exclude some greater evil" (ch. XIII, para. II; 1823 spacing). Punishment is not to be inflicted "Where it is groundless ; where there is no mischief for it to prevent" (para. III.1), nor where it is inefficacious, unprofitable or needless (III.2–4). Crimmins (§7): "utilitarian penal law is framed in terms of the principal objective of deterrence, but it also embraces the secondary ends of disablement, moral reformation, and compensation (see Crimmins 2011)." On the death penalty: "By 1809, however, he abandoned the exceptions and argued that no offence warranted capital punishment (UC cvii. 201)." (§7).
- **[The ticking time bomb](../problems/ticking-bomb.md)** (MS 1804). In "Means of extraction for extraordinary occasions", as transcribed by [Davies (2012)](https://doi.org/10.16995/ntn.643), Bentham asks: "For the purpose of rescuing from torture these hundred innocents, should any scruple be made of applying equal or superior torture, to extract the requisite information from the mouth of one criminal, who having it in his power to make known the place where at this time the enormity was practicing or about to be practiced, should refuse to do so?" (UC 74b/428–30; `raw/davies-2012-bentham-and-torture-means-of-extraction.md`). Miller (SEP "Torture") writes that "the classic utilitarian justification remains that of Bentham (1804)." (`raw/sep-torture-fall-2024-ticking-bomb.md`).
- **Natural rights** (*Anarchical Fallacies*, on Art. II of the 1789 Declaration). "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, -nonsense upon stilts." and "But reasons for wishing there were such things as rights, are not rights; — a reason for wishing that a certain right were established, is not that right-want is not supply - hunger is not bread." Crimmins (§1) dates the text 1795 and connects it to the Terror: "The bloody excesses of the Terror reinforced his criticism of the abstract and slippery nature of natural and imprescriptible rights, which he famously dismissed as “nonsense on stilts” (1795 [2002, 330])—a disposition formed while listening to Blackstone’s lectures" (the 2002 Collected Works text reads "on", the Bowring text "upon"). No problem page on rights exists yet.
- **Prison design: the panopticon.** Crimmins (§1): "It was in Russia, too, that Bentham took an idea of Samuel’s and developed it into the “panopticon”, the full dimensions of which he explained in Panopticon; Or, The Inspection House (1791)." Bentham's statement of the principle: "The essence of it consists, then, in the centrality of the inspector’s situation, combined with the wellknown and most effectual contrivances for seeing without being seen." (Letter V); the inmate "should conceive himself to be so" watched at every instant (Letter I). The Preface: "A new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind, in a quantity hitherto without example". The project ended: "In 1802, however, he admitted defeat, and in 1812 the government officially closed the books on the whole sorry affair, paying Bentham £23,000 in compensation." (Crimmins §1).

## In dialogue with

- **[David Hume](hume.md)**, Helvétius and Beccaria (earlier). In Hume's *Treatise* "Bentham found virtue equated with utility, at which he “felt as if scales had fallen from my eyes” (1776 [1977, 440n])." (Crimmins §1). Crimmins (preamble) lists "John Gay, Francis Hutcheson, David Hume, Claude-Adrien Helvétius and Cesare Beccaria" as earlier moralists who had "enunciated several of the core ideas".
- **William Blackstone** (earlier). Bentham attended his Oxford lectures (above); Crimmins (§1) traces the rights critique to "a disposition formed while listening to Blackstone’s lectures".
- **[John Stuart Mill](mill.md)** (later). Mill's 1838 essay "Bentham" is quoted under Reception. In *Utilitarianism*, per Crimmins (§3.2), "J. S. Mill introduced the concept of“higher pleasures”, by which he meant aesthetic and spiritual gratification and the pleasures of the intellect, and claimed these were intrinsically more desirable than other pleasures." Crisp (SEP "Well-Being" §4.1): "Mill added a third property to the two determinants of value identified by Bentham, duration and intensity." (`raw/sep-well-being-and-hedonism-fall-2024-mill-higher-pleasures.md`).
- **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (later). Schultz (SEP "Henry Sidgwick") places the *Methods* as "the culmination of the classical utilitarian tradition—the tradition of Jeremy Bentham and James and John Stuart Mill—" (`raw/sep-sidgwick-fall-2024-life-methods-dualism-reception.md`). Crimmins (§10) groups him with "the more amenable forms of utilitarian moral and legal theory offered by John Austin, J. S. Mill, and Henry Sidgwick."
- **Peter Singer** (later). Crimmins (§3) cites Singer 1975 for the animal-ethics lineage; Singer's "All Animals Are Equal" (1974) builds on the Bentham note (`raw/singer-1974-all-animals-are-equal.md`; see the problem page).
- **Michel Foucault** (later). Crimmins (§8): "Michel Foucault took the perspective that “panopticism” defined a “new physics of power”, an experimental “laboratory of power” in which behaviour could be modified, and he viewed the panopticon—that “cruel, ingenious cage”—as a symbol of the repressive, disciplinary society, the modern “society of surveillance” (Foucault 208)."
- **[William James](william-james.md)** and **[John Dewey](dewey.md)** (later). Crimmins (§11): "the Pragmatists, notably William James and John Dewey, may have shared Bentham’s ontological misgivings about the meaning and misrepresentation of fictions and fictitious entities (Quinn 2012)" "but they rejected the claim that all motivation may be reduced to a drive to seek pleasure and avoid pain, which they viewed as a self-defeating commitment to materialist individualism (Dewey and Tufts 1908, 271–74; see also Crimmins 2022, Epilogue)."
- **[G. E. M. Anscombe](anscombe.md)** (later). Wiland and Driver (SEP "Anscombe", §2) report that in "Modern Moral Philosophy" "she brusquely and perhaps uncharitably attacks Butler, Hume, Kant, Bentham, and Mill" (`raw/sep-anscombe-fall-2024-life-ethics-intention.md`; their wording).
- **[Aristippus](aristippus.md)** and the Cyrenaics (earlier, as a resemblance). Tsouna (SEP "Cyrenaics", §5): their ethical views "become vibrant in the utilitarian tradition, especially the writings of Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill, and Henry Sidgwick." (`raw/sep-cyrenaics-fall-2026-aristippus-presentism-epicurus.md`).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's. Praise and
criticism are set side by side.

- **Crimmins (SEP, preamble).** "it was Bentham who rendered the theory in its recognisably secular and systematic form and made it a critical tool of moral and legal philosophy and political and social improvement." "Upon this rests Bentham’s reputation as one of the great thinkers in modern philosophy." (Crimmins's assessment).
- **J. S. Mill, "Bentham" (1838), for.** "Bentham has been in this age and country the great questioner of things established." (Dissertations and Discussions I, 1882, p. 357). "Bentham: he is the great subversive, or, in the language of Continental philosophers, the great critical, thinker of his age and country." (p. 359). "he was not a great philosopher; but he was a great reformer in philosophy." "It was not his doctrines which did this: it was his mode of arriving at them." (p. 364). (`raw/mill-1838-bentham-essay-great-questioner-half-thinker.md`; [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/DissertationsAndDiscussionsPoliticalPhilosophicalHistoricalV1)).
- **J. S. Mill (1838), against.** "Man is never recognized by him as a being capable of pursuing spiritual perfection as an end; of desiring, for its own sake, the conformity of his own character to his standard of excellence, without hope of good, or fear of evil, from other source than his own inward consciousness." "Nothing is more curious than the absence of recognition, in any of his writings, of the existence of conscience, as a thing distinct from philanthropy, from affection for God or man, and from self-interest in this world or in the next." (p. 384). Mill's verdict combines the two: "For our own part, we have a large tolerance for one-eyed men, provided their one eye is a penetrating one" and "Almost all rich veins of original and striking speculation have been opened by systematic half-thinkers" (p. 382).
- **Mill's higher pleasures, two readings.** Crimmins (§3.2): "This tended to undermine the aggregative dimension of the theory laid down by Bentham, since the depth of feeling associated with such pleasures resist quantification." Against: "Recent commentators, however, have questioned whether the distance between Bentham and Mill is as large as commonly supposed, arguing for example that “intensity” and “purity” are qualities of pains and pleasures that, in principle, are still subject to measurement, at least when comparing alternative actions on the same index (Warke 2000; Rosen 2003, Ch. 10)."
- **Nineteenth-century critics (Crimmins §11).** Constant "questioned the vagueness of the concept of utility, which he thought susceptible to many different definitions and pregnant with danger as a principle of policy." Hazlitt called it a philosophy "fit neither for man nor beast" (1826, 184). "Thomas Carlyle summed up the general perception of the crassness of “Benthamee utility” as the “pig-philosophy”, nothing more than “virtue by Profit and Loss” (1840, 65)." "Marx considered Bentham an “arch-philistine” and utilitarianism a superficial and ephemeral bourgeois ideology (Capital I, Ch. XXIV, sect. 5)."
- **Admirers (Crimmins §10).** "Graham Wallas, another of the Fabians, was deeply impressed by Bentham’s legal, political and administrative innovations and hailed him as “Britain’s greatest political inventor” (Wallas 1926)." Against the formula: "F. Y. Edgeworth (1881) thought Bentham’s formula of the “greatest happiness of the greatest number” a mathematical absurdity, and reformulated the goal in terms of the greatest total happiness (or greatest average happiness for a fixed number of people)."
- **Separateness of persons (Crimmins §4.3).** "This aspect of Bentham’s theory has often been ignored or dismissed as irrelevant by critics who, from T. H. Green forward, argue that calculations of total utility fail to respect the distinctiveness of persons and thereby place their interests at perpetual risk (Rawls 1971, 22–27; Nozick 1974, 28–35;" — Crimmins goes on to argue that Bentham's security principle answers the innocent-bystander case (his reading).
- **The panopticon.** Foucault's reading is quoted above; Crimmins (§8) replies: "However, as a critique of Bentham’s proposals it hardly does justice to the intricacies of the project, as Semple (1993) has shown." (Crimmins's assessment).
- **Torture.** Davies (2012, abstract): "Jeremy Bentham has frequently been regarded as the father of the ‘ticking bomb’ argument in defence of interrogational torture." Davies argues that the 1804 text differs from Bentham's 1770s writings; the [problem page](../problems/ticking-bomb.md) sets out the responses.

## Vocabulary

- *Utility*: for Bentham, the "property" of producing pleasure or preventing pain; Crimmins (§1) quotes "tends to produce benefit, advantage, good or happiness" and calls these "all commensurate terms in Bentham’s moral vocabulary". No vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Felicific calculus*: not Bentham's term (Crimmins §3.2: "Though Bentham did not use this terminology"); IPML ch. IV is headed "Value of a Lot of Pleasure or Pain, How to be Measured", and Crimmins describes the calculus as giving "the elements or dimensions of the value of a pain or pleasure".
- *Mischief*: the 1823 text uses it for whatever "tends to subtract from" the community's happiness (ch. XIII, para. I).
- *Rights*: in *Anarchical Fallacies* only legal rights exist; "natural rights" is "simple nonsense", not the sense of rights used on problem pages that report rights theories.
- *Suffer* / *sensitive being*: Bentham's ch. XVII note speaks of "a sensitive being"; the implant's [sentience](../vocabulary/sentience.md) page notes that Bentham does not use "sentience".

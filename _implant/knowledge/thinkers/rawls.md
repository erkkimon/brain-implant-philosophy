## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates and field:** "John Rawls (b. 1921, d. 2002) was an American political philosopher in the liberal tradition." (Wenar, preamble).
- **Birthplace:** "Rawls was born and raised in Baltimore, Maryland." (§1).
- **Education and teachers:** "Rawls studied at Princeton and Cornell, where he was influenced by Wittgenstein’s student Norman Malcolm; and at Oxford, where he worked with H. L. A. Hart, Isaiah Berlin, and Stuart Hampshire." (§1).
- **Appointments:** "His first professorial appointments were at Cornell and MIT. In 1962 Rawls joined the faculty at Harvard, where he taught for more than thirty years." (§1).
- **Religion** (as Wenar reports it): "As a college student, Rawls wrote an intensely religious senior thesis (BI) and had considered studying for the priesthood." "Yet Rawls lost his Christian faith as an infantryman in World War II on seeing the capriciousness of death in combat and learning of the horrors of the Holocaust." (§1). The thesis was published posthumously in 2009 (Works).
- **Vietnam:** "Then in the 1960s, Rawls spoke out against the draft for the Vietnam war because it discriminated against black and poor Americans." (§1).

## Problems addressed

The theory itself has no problem page yet; its parts are set out first, then the
problem pages in this implant that a read source ties to Rawls.

- **Justice as fairness** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971). Wenar: "His theory of justice as fairness describes a society of free citizens holding equal basic rights and cooperating within an egalitarian economic system." (preamble). Freeman: "The original position is a central feature of John Rawls’s social contract account of justice, “justice as fairness,” set forth in A Theory of Justice (TJ)." (preamble). Wenar (§4.6): "The move to agreement among citizens is what places Rawls’s justice as fairness within the social contract tradition of Locke, Rousseau, and Kant."
- **The original position and the veil of ignorance.** Wenar (§4.6): "The original position is a thought experiment: an imaginary situation in which each real citizen has a representative, and all of these representatives come to an agreement on which principles of justice should order the political institutions of the real citizens." and "The most striking feature of the original position is the veil of ignorance, which prevents arbitrary facts about citizens from influencing the agreement among their representatives." Freeman (preamble): "The main distinguishing feature of the original position is “the veil of ignorance”: To ensure complete impartiality of judgment, the parties are deprived of all knowledge of their personal characteristics and conceptions of the good, and of social and historical circumstances." On its force, Freeman (§2): "Hypothetical agreement in the original position does not then bind anyone to duties or commitments they do not already have."
- **The two principles** (Wenar §4.3, quoting *Justice as Fairness* 42–43):
  "First Principle: Each person has the same indefeasible claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties, which scheme is compatible with the same scheme of liberties for all;"
  "Second Principle: Social and economic inequalities are to satisfy two conditions:" "They are to be attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity;" "They are to be to the greatest benefit of the least-advantaged members of society (the difference principle) (JF, 42–43)."
  Priority: "Fulfillment of the first principle takes priority over fulfillment of the second principle, and within the second principle fair equality of opportunity takes priority over the difference principle."
- **The difference principle.** Wenar's illustration (§4.3) gives four economies, incomes for the least-advantaged / middle / most-advantaged groups: A 10,000 / 10,000 / 10,000; B 12,000 / 30,000 / 80,000; C 30,000 / 90,000 / 150,000; D 20,000 / 100,000 / 500,000. "Here the difference principle selects Economy C, because it contains the distribution where the least-advantaged group does best."
  *Arithmetic check (structural note, [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)):* the least-advantaged column is 10,000, 12,000, 30,000, 20,000; its maximum, 30,000, is C's. D has the largest total (620,000 against C's 270,000), so a rule that maximised the sum would pick D, not C.
  Its basis, per Wenar: "The difference principle is partly based on the negative thesis that the distribution of natural assets is undeserved."
- **The argument from the original position: maximin.** Wenar (§4.7): "Rawls only attempts to show that his two principles of justice as fairness would be favored over utilitarian principles, since he sees utilitarianism as the main competing tradition of reasoning about justice." "In this first comparison, Rawls argues that it is rational for the parties to use maximin reasoning: to maximize the minimum level of primary goods that the citizens they represent might find themselves with." Freeman (§6.2) defines the rule: "One rule of choice—called “maximin”—directs that we play it as safe as possible by choosing the alternative whose worst outcome leaves us better off than the worst outcome of all other alternatives." Harsanyi's objection is under In dialogue with.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (method; see [reflective equilibrium](../methods/reflective-equilibrium.md)). Wenar (§2.4): "For Rawls, how justified one is in one’s political convictions depends on how close one is to achieving reflective equilibrium." "One treats these considered judgments as provisional fixed points, and then starts the process of bringing one’s beliefs into relations of mutual support and explanation as described above." The end-point is wide equilibrium, "aiming for the end-point of wide reflective equilibrium, in which coherence is maintained after many alternatives have been considered." Lamont and Favor (SEP "Distributive Justice", §9): "The most notable exception is John Rawls (1971, 1974) who explicitly brought the method of wide reflective equilibrium to political philosophy." (their assessment; `raw/sep-justice-distributive-fall-2024-rawls-difference-principle-critics.md`).
- **Political liberalism** (*Political Liberalism*, 1993). Wenar: "Rawls holds that the need to impose a unified law on a diverse citizenry raises two fundamental challenges." (§3) — legitimacy and stability. The liberal principle of legitimacy, as Wenar quotes it (§3.1): "Our exercise of political power is fully proper only when it is exercised in accordance with a constitution the essentials of which all citizens as free and equal may reasonably be expected to endorse in the light of principles and ideals acceptable to their common human reason (PL, 137)." Stability: "In an overlapping consensus, citizens all endorse a core set of laws for different reasons." (§3.5). Public reason: "In essence, public reason requires citizens to be able to justify their political decisions to one another using publicly available values and standards." (§3.6).
- **Ideal and non-ideal theory.** "Within each sub-domain of the political Rawls also follows a sequence: ideal theory before non-ideal theory." (Wenar §2.3).
- **[Justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md)** ("Two Concepts of Rules", 1955, §I; `raw/rawls-1955-two-concepts-of-rules-punishment-telishment.md`). Against the objection that utilitarianism licenses punishing the innocent, Rawls imagines "telishment": "Try to imagine, then, an institution (which we may call "telishment") which is such that the officials set up by it have authority to arrange a trial for the condemnation of an innocent man whenever they are of the opinion that doing so would be in the best interests of society." and concludes "A utilitarian justification for this institution is most unlikely." The problem page sets out the replies on record.
- **[Just war](../problems/just-war.md)** (TJ 382). Fiala (SEP "Pacifism", §2.2): "As John Rawls says of what he calls “contingent pacifism,” “the possibility of a just war is conceded but not under present circumstances” (Rawls 1971, 382)." (`raw/sep-pacifism-fall-2024-fiala-absolute-contingent-just-war-pacifism.md`).
- **[Moral luck](../problems/moral-luck.md).** Nelkin (SEP "Moral Luck", §2.2): "Inspired by the work of John Rawls, some egalitarians have invoked the idea that our constitution and circumstances are out of our control in the justification of their view." (`raw/sep-moral-luck-fall-2024-nelkin-map.md`).
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md)** (an extension by others). Wilson (IEP "Animals and Ethics", §1d): "Although Rawls argues for this conception as a conception of justice, others have tried to extend it to cover all of morality. For example, in The Animals Issue, Peter Carruthers argues for a conception of morality that is based largely on Rawls’s work. Carruthers notes that if we do so extend Rawls’s conception, animals will have no direct moral standing." (`raw/iep-animals-and-ethics-wilson-indirect-theories-marginal-cases.md`).
- **[The Heinz dilemma](../problems/heinz-dilemma.md)** (as used by Kohlberg). LeBar (SEP, §2): "In fact, Kohlberg was impressed by the work of Rawls, and thought that the nature of Rawls’ “original position of equality” exemplified the kind of reversibility that is paradigmatic of the highest form of moral thought (Kohlberg 1981, p. 204)." (`raw/sep-kohlberg-gilligan-fall-2024-heinz-stages-and-care.md`).
- **[Lying to the murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md)** (as a model). Korsgaard (1986) proposes "special principles to use when dealing with evil" (p. 349), modelled, per the excerpt notes, on Rawls's ideal and non-ideal theory (pp. 342–349; `raw/korsgaard-1986-right-to-lie-kant-dealing-with-evil.md`).
- **[Moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)** (as a target). Ballarin (SEP "Ruth Barcan Marcus", §8): "Marcus criticizes the dominant view of moral dilemmas which is based on an excessively demanding notion of consistency. She attributes this view in some form or other to W.D. Ross, John Lemmon, Hare, Rawls and Davidson." (`raw/sep-ruth-barcan-marcus-fall-2024-life-modal-logic-names-dilemmas.md`).

## In dialogue with

- **[Immanuel Kant](kant.md)** (earlier; the Kantian interpretation). Freeman (§1) places the original position among versions of "the moral point of view": "Kant’s categorical imperative procedure, Adam Smith’s “impartial spectator,”, and Sidgwick’s “point of view of the universe” are all different versions of the moral point of view." and "Rawls’s idea of the original position, as initially conceived, is his account of the moral point of view regarding matters of justice." The difference: "Unlike Kant’s categorical imperative procedure, the original position is designed to represent the predominantly social bases of justice." (§2). Kantian readings Freeman reports: "In this regard the veil of ignorance interprets the Kantian idea of equality as equal respect for moral persons (cf. CP 255)." (§3); "Rawls resembles Kant in this regard (PL 25n); his distinction between the reasonable and rational parallels Kant’s distinction between categorical and hypothetical imperatives." (§4); and Rawls, as Freeman quotes TJ 157 rev. (§6.4): "“[T]he difference principle interprets the distinction between treating men as means only and treating them as ends in themselves." On Rawls 1980 and constructivism, see the [Kant](kant.md) page (In dialogue with), quoting Bagnoli.
- **[Henry Sidgwick](sidgwick.md)** (earlier). Schultz (SEP "Henry Sidgwick", §2.1; `raw/sep-sidgwick-fall-2024-life-methods-dualism-reception.md`): "In A Theory of Justice, Rawls famously explained that Sidgwick characterizes a person’s future good on the whole as what he would now desire and seek if the consequences of all the various courses of conduct open to him were, at the present point of time, accurately foreseen by him and adequately realized in imagination." On method (§3): "Rawls himself shifted his ground somewhat in later work, playing up Sidgwick’s “rational intuitionism” (Rawls 1993, 2007)." Freeman (§5.2) contrasts Rawls's publicity condition with Sidgwick: "So Sidgwick argues that the aims of utilitarianism might better be achieved if it remains an “esoteric morality,” knowledge of which is confined to “an enlightened few” (Sidgwick 1907 [1981], 489–90)."
- **[Jeremy Bentham](bentham.md)** and the utilitarians (earlier). Lamont and Favor (§5): "The first, which was famously articulated by John Rawls (1971), is that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinctness of persons." Crimmins (SEP "Jeremy Bentham", §4.3) lists Rawls among such critics: "This aspect of Bentham’s theory has often been ignored or dismissed as irrelevant by critics who, from T. H. Green forward, argue that calculations of total utility fail to respect the distinctiveness of persons and thereby place their interests at perpetual risk (Rawls 1971, 22–27; Nozick 1974, 28–35;" (`raw/sep-bentham-fall-2024-life-calculus-panopticon-reception.md`; Crimmins replies for Bentham, see that page).
- **Critics, side by side** (later). Each objection is the critic's; each reply is as the encyclopedia author reports it.
  - *Robert Nozick* (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974). Freeman (§6): "(Nozick agrees and says the OP is incapable of yielding historical entitlement principles, but only patterned end-state principles instead. Nozick 1974, 198–204. Rawls replies that the difference principle does not conform to any observable pattern but grounds fair distributions in a fair social process that must actually be carried out. PL, 282–83)" Lamont and Favor (§7) on his alternative: "For libertarians, just outcomes are those arrived at by the separate just actions of individuals; a particular distributive pattern is not required for justice." Rawls's side, per Wenar (§3.4): "By Rawls’s criteria, a libertarian conception of justice (such as Nozick’s in Anarchy, State, and Utopia) is not a liberal political conception of justice."
  - *John Harsanyi* (*Can the Maximin Principle Serve as a Basis for Morality?*, title per Crossref, *American Political Science Review* 69(2) (1975): 594–606, DOI [10.2307/1959090](https://doi.org/10.2307/1959090), verified; not read). Freeman (§6.2): "Thus, John Harsanyi contends that it is more rational under conditions of complete uncertainty always to choose according to the principle of insufficient reason and assume an equal probability of occupying any position in society." "When the equiprobability assumption is made, the parties in the original position would choose the principle of average utility instead of the principles of justice (Harsanyi 1975)." Rawls's reply: "Rawls denies that the parties have a psychological disposition to risk-aversion." "He argues however that it is rational to choose as if one were risk averse under the highly exceptional circumstances of the original position." Freeman's own assessment of the Bayesian counterexamples: "But such examples do not suffice here; simply because maximin is under most circumstances irrational does not mean that it is never rational."
  - *Michael Sandel* (*Liberalism and the Limits of Justice*, 1982) and the communitarians. Bell (SEP "Communitarianism", §2; `raw/sep-communitarianism-fall-2024-sandel-taylor-against-rawls.md`): "Communitarian thinkers in the 1980s such as Michael Sandel and Charles Taylor argued that Rawlsian liberalism rests on an overly individualistic conception of the self." Freeman (§3) states the objection to the veil this way: "Or they are incapable of making a rational choice, since we cannot decide upon ethical principles without knowing our primary purposes in life, the values of community, or certain other final ends and commitments. (MacIntyre, 1981; Sandel 1982)" and goes on to give the answer that the parties need not know their final ends to choose background conditions (§3).
  - *Amartya Sen* (*The Idea of Justice*, 2009) and *Lara Buchak* (2017). Freeman (§3): "Among the more common criticisms are that the parties’ choice in the original position is indeterminate (Sen, 2009, 11–12, 56–58), or would result in choice of the principle of (average) utility (Harsanyi, 1975), or a principle of relative prioritarianism that gives greater weight to but does not maximize the least advantaged position (Buchak, 2017)"
  - *G. A. Cohen* ("Incentives, Inequality, and Community", Tanner Lectures, 1992; *Rescuing Justice and Equality*, 2008; neither read). Lamont and Favor (§3): "Cohen (1992) also provides a critique of Rawls’ Difference Principle that is inspired by what he calls an “egalitarian ethos”." "However, Cohen rejects applications of the Difference Principle in the context of greater incomes to induce those who are particularly talented to undertake work which will benefit the least advantaged, particularly when that work, as is often the case, is already more fulfilling than other employment options."
- **Lawrence Kohlberg** (later) and **Ruth Barcan Marcus** (later): see Problems addressed (Heinz dilemma; moral dilemmas).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Wenar (§1):** "Rawls’s most discussed work is his theory of a just liberal society, called justice as fairness." On the literature (§6): "Historically, the most influential volume of essays on justice as fairness has been Daniels (1975)."
- **Freeman (§3), on the volume of criticism:** "Many criticisms have been leveled against Rawls’s veil of ignorance." (the critics are under In dialogue with).
- **Lamont and Favor (§6), on desert:** "John Rawls has made one of the most widely discussed arguments to this effect (Rawls 1971), and while a strong form of this argument has been clearly refuted (Zaitchik, Sher), it remains a problem for desert-based principles." ("this effect": that desert-based principles make benefits depend on factors beyond people's control; "clearly refuted" is their assessment.)
  On Cohen: "Cohen’s critique effectively urges a stricter reading of the requirements of the Difference Principle than Cohen believes Rawls and many of his supporters have held."
- **Communitarians (Bell, preamble):** "Drawing primarily upon the insights of Aristotle and Hegel, political philosophers such as Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Sandel, Charles Taylor and Michael Walzer disputed Rawls’ assumption that the principal task of government is to secure and distribute fairly the liberties and economic resources individuals need to lead freely chosen lives."
- **On Sidgwick's legacy (Schultz, preamble):** "Even such influential critics of utilitarianism as William Frankena, Marcus Singer, and John Rawls looked to Sidgwick’s work for guidance."
- **On punishment (Duff and Hoskins, as the [problem page](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md) quotes them):** "Rawls’s account (1955) has also been characterised as a hybrid view of this sort, but in fact it is a version of rule utilitarianism"

## Vocabulary

- *Justice as fairness*: Rawls's name for his theory; *fairness* qualifies the choice situation, since "His aim in designing the original position is to describe an agreement situation that is fair among all the parties to the hypothetical social contract." (Freeman §3).
- *Original position*, *veil of ignorance*, *maximin*, *difference principle*: technical terms defined above; no vocabulary pages yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Reasonable* vs *rational*: per Freeman (§4) Rawls's distinction "parallels Kant’s distinction between categorical and hypothetical imperatives".
- *Political* (as in "political conception", "political liberalism"): Wenar (§3.5): "Recall that the content of a political conception is freestanding:"; each citizen supports it "for reasons internal to her own comprehensive doctrine." Not the everyday sense of partisan politics.
- *Reflective equilibrium*: the implant's [method page](../methods/reflective-equilibrium.md) traces the term to Goodman (1955); Rawls's narrow/wide distinction is set out there.

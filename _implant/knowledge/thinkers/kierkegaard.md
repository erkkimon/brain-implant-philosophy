## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates:** "Søren Aabye Kierkegaard (1813–1855) was an astonishingly prolific writer whose work—almost all of which was written in the 1840s—is difficult to categorize, spanning philosophy, theology, religious and devotional writing, literary criticism, psychology and social critique." (Lippitt & Evans, preamble; the evaluative words are theirs).
- **Birth:** "Søren Kierkegaard was born to Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard and Anne (Lund) Kierkegaard in Copenhagen on 5 May 1813, the youngest of seven children." (§1).
- **Education:** "After a prolonged period of study at the University of Copenhagen, Søren received a first degree in theology and a Magister degree in philosophy, with a dissertation dealing with irony as practiced by Socrates (On the Concept of Irony with Continual Reference to Socrates)." "However, Kierkegaard was never ordained and never became a pastor, though he preached on occasions in various Copenhagen churches." (§1).
- **Engagement:** "In 1840 Kierkegaard became engaged to the eighteen-year-old Regine Olsen." "Finally, in 1841 he broke the engagement for good and fled to Berlin for a brief time, perhaps in part to escape the public scandal of the whole affair." (§1).
- **The Corsair:** "First, in 1845–6 Kierkegaard became embroiled in a controversy with The Corsair, a satirical literary magazine that included cartoons mocking many of Denmark’s most prominent public figures." (§1).
- **Attack on the church:** "Second, in the last two years of his life, Kierkegaard, known as a defender of Christian faith, embarked on a controversial attack on the Danish state church, a target that symbolized all that he thought was wrong with “Christendom” as a whole." (§1).
- **Death:** "He died there a few weeks later, on 11 November 1855. A tentative diagnosis of the cause was tuberculosis of the spine marrow." (§1).
- **Tradition:** Hare (§4) calls him "a religious thinker who started, like Hegel and Kant, from Lutheranism."
- **Biographies differ:** "Walter Lowrie’s early biography (Lowrie 1938) is a work of loving devotion, as Lowrie spent a great deal of his later life translating Kierkegaard into English. Joakim Garff’s portrait (2000, English translation 2005) is far more critical and provides unflattering interpretations of many of the key events in Kierkegaard’s life." (Lippitt & Evans §1).

## Problems addressed

- **Who is speaking: pseudonymity.** Lippitt & Evans (§1): "the authorship divides broadly into two distinct kinds of text: those written under a variety of pseudonyms (the “pseudonymous” authorship) and those written under his own name (the “signed” or “veronymous” authorship)." Their reported reasons: "Kierkegaard consistently maintains that ethical and religious truth cannot be directly or “immediately” communicated to others." "The use of the pseudonyms is in part an attempt to do this." Hollander (1923, Introduction I) gave a different reason: "The writer's own personality was to be entirely eliminated by a system of pseudonyms; for the effect of his teaching was not to be jeopardized by a distracting knowledge of his personality."
  *Readings side by side* (as Lippitt & Evans §1 report them, unranked here):
  (a) *the pseudonyms as characters* — "One way of characterizing this pseudonymous authorship is to think of it as one vast novel, with the various pseudonymous authors who figure in the works being characters therein."
  (b) *"S. Kierkegaard" as one more mask* — "Although some (e.g., Mackey 1986) have claimed that “S. Kierkegaard” is ultimately just another pseudonym, this remains a minority view." (Lippitt & Evans's count).
  (c) *shared themes* — "One is to look for common themes and conceptual perspectives that are shared by most or (in some cases) nearly all the pseudonyms, such as the view that there are distinct “stages” or “spheres” of existence (to be discussed below)."
  (d) *the signed works as baseline* — "Another strategy is to compare the pseudonymous works to the signed works, on the assumption that the latter provide a baseline because they embody what Kierkegaard wanted to put forward under his own name."
  (e) *Anti-Climacus as Kierkegaard's own view* — "Many scholars have treated Anti-Climacus as only “weakly” pseudonymous, taking him to articulate views Kierkegaard himself holds, but which he considers himself to lack the authority to pronounce in his own name."
  Kierkegaard's own request: "Kierkegaard himself explicitly requested that anyone citing these works should make the attribution to the pseudonyms rather than himself (CUP I 627/SKS 7, 571)." This page follows that request in naming the pseudonym for each claim.
  *The religious-author claim, both sides.* "In The Point of View for My Work as an Author (written 1848, published posthumously) Kierkegaard—in his own voice—claims that he was from first to last a religious author," Against: "This claim has been hotly contested, with some critics arguing that The Point of View represents what Kierkegaard later wanted his readers to think rather than being an honest account of his intentions—and that other elements of his writings, such as the journals, are similarly unreliable (see, e.g., Fenger 1976 [1980], Mackey 1986, Garff 2000 [2005])." (§1).
- **The aesthetic and the ethical (*Either/Or*, 1843, edited by "Victor Eremita").** Volume I: "The first volume of Either/Or consists of the papers of a nameless young man, known only as “A”, who in a series of sometimes witty, sometimes rambling, sometimes astute and sometimes despairing writings, presents a view of what it means to live “aesthetically”." "As is clearest in his essay “Crop Rotation”, he sets himself the task of living to avoid boredom at all costs." (§3.1). Volume II: "Kierkegaard’s most famous spokesman for the ethical life is known as Judge (or “Assessor”) William." "His key theme is to try to persuade the aesthete that choosing “aesthetically” is not really choosing at all; that he needs to “choose choice itself:” take responsibility for the person he has become, as the first step in aiming to change it." (§3.2). Lippitt & Evans's summary: "a key contrast is between a life viewed in terms of mere possibilities (the aesthetic) and in terms of “tasks” (the ethical)."
  Hare (§4) reads Judge William as a caricature: "In Either/Or he caricatured Kant's ethical thought (as well as Hegel's) in the person of Judge William, who is stuck within the ethical life and has not been able to reach the life of faith." Hollander (1923) reads the ethical as a step: "The fact is that Kierkegaard, when considering the ethical sphere, in order to carry out his plan of contrasting it with the æsthetic sphere, was already envisaging the higher sphere of religion, to which the ethical sphere is but a transition, and which is the only true alternative to the æsthetic life." Lippitt & Evans (§3) caution that "The idea that there are precisely three such spheres is an over-simplification,".
- **Abraham and the ethical (*Fear and Trembling*, 1843, by "Johannes de silentio").** "Johannes’ focus is on the events of Genesis 22, wherein Abraham receives the shocking command to sacrifice his long-awaited son Isaac on Mount Moriah." (§3.3.1). In de silentio's words (Hollander tr.): "The fact is, the ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he wanted to murder Isaac; the religious, that he wanted to sacrifice him. But precisely in this contradiction is contained the fear which may well rob one of one's sleep." Abraham's faith: "He believed this on the strength of the absurd; for there was no question of human calculation any longer." and "Now the absurd is not one of the categories which belong to the understanding proper. It is not identical with the improbable, the unforeseen, the unexpected." ("Preliminary Expectoration").
  *The Problems.* "Each of the three Problems begins with the idea that “the ethical as such is the universal”, a different dimension of which is brought out in each Problem." Problem I asks "whether the story of Abraham contains a “teleological suspension of the ethical”." Abraham's offence, as Lippitt & Evans state it: "First, he makes of himself an exception to what universality demands (one ought not to kill one’s innocent offspring). This—secondly—amounts to the “paradox” that the “single individual” stands higher than the universal." Against the tragic hero: "The stories of Agamemnon, Jepthah and Brutus (FT 50–2/SKS 4, 151–3) each offers another case of a father feeling the obligation to sacrifice his own offspring for a cause conceived of as “higher”." "These explanations are within the realm of the ethical conceived of as the universal, whereas—according to Johannes—Abraham has no such explanation available to him." Problem II "focuses on whether our duty to God is absolute"; Problem III, "focusing on Abraham’s silence—considers whether concealing his purpose from others (such as his son, wife and servant) was ethically defensible." (§3.3.1).
  *Readings side by side.* (a) A conflict of law and command — Hollander (1923): "In other words, Abraham chooses to be "the exception" and set aside the general law, as well as does the æsthetic individual; but, note well: "in fear and trembling," and at the express command of God!" (b) Not obedience alone — Lippitt & Evans, on the four "sub-Abrahams" of the opening "Attunement": "This should warn us off readings which suggest that the central message of the text—and of faith—is simply the importance of being obedient to God." and "The true measure of Abraham’s faith is less his willingness to sacrifice Isaac than his trust and hope that he will “receive him back”—in this life, not just in eternity." (c) A clash of loves — "Fear and Trembling, often approached as being about a clash of duties (between Abraham’s duty to his God and ethical duties conceived of in terms of “the universal”), may just as well be approached as an apparent clash of loves (for God; for Isaac)." (§3.3.2). (d) Whose "ethical" — "there has been considerable discussion about whether the conception of the ethical in Fear and Trembling is Kantian (e.g., Pojman 1984, Hampson 2013), Fichtean (Kosch 2006) or Hegelian (e.g., Evans 2004, Walsh 2009, Westphal 2014))." (§3.2). On attribution, Lippitt & Evans (§3.3.1): "But since Johannes de silentio claims to be an outsider to “faith”, and repeatedly tells us he cannot understand Abraham, this is a dangerous move." (taking de silentio's views as Kierkegaard's; their assessment).
  *An earlier reading of the same story.* Hare (§3): "God also gives dispensation from the commands, according to Scotus, for example the command to Abraham to kill Isaac (Ord III, suppl. Dist. 37)."
- **[The Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md)** (divine commands and obligation). The tie is through a reader: Hare (§5) reports that "C. Stephen Evans, in Kierkegaard's Ethics of Love: Divine Commands and Moral Obligations (2004) and God and Moral Obligation(2013) articulates both in Kierkegaard and in its own right a divine command theory that is argued to be superior to all the main alternative non-theist accounts of the nature and basis of moral obligation." In *Works of Love* (1847, signed), per Lippitt & Evans (§3.3.2): "Furthermore, the duty to love must be grounded in divine authority; no human authority is sufficient." Neither source read here states that Kierkegaard addressed Plato's question itself.
- **Reflection and choice (*Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, 1846, by "Johannes Climacus").** "In Concluding Unscientific Postscript Johannes Climacus argues that reflection, though a crucial and necessary element in human life, by itself cannot lead to choices (see Evans 2006: 311–326)." "In an argument that resembles David Hume’s claim that “reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions…”, (Hume 1739–40 [1888: 415]) Climacus says that reflection itself has a kind of inner infinity." (§2). The ethical and the religious: "Concluding Unscientific Postscript offers an account of the ethical life that sees it as the beginning of a kind of “immanent” religious existence (“Religiousness A”) that is possible for humans apart from any transcendent revelation." "Religiousness A, which is distinct from Christianity (what Climacus calls “Religiousness B”) is seen a providing the “pathos” or inwardness that is necessary for a person to become a Christian," (§3.2; wording as in the source). Against system: "Kierkegaard’s pseudonym Johannes Climacus insists that a system of existence is impossible for anyone but God (CUP I, 118–125/SKS 7, 114 -120)." (§4).
- **Faith and reason, both sides.** "Calling the incarnation a “contradiction” has suggested to some that Kierkegaard is a radical fideist who sees Christian faith as requiring a rejection of reason by believing what is logically contradictory, a view applauded by some (e.g., Shestov 1936 [1969]), and decried by others (e.g., Pojman 1984). However, it is far from obvious that Kierkegaard saw Christian faith this way." (§3.3.1; the last sentence is Lippitt & Evans's assessment).
- **Neighbour-love (*Works of Love*, 1847, signed).** "A central issue in this text is the love that is at stake in the command to love our neighbors as ourselves." "“The neighbor” includes everyone, and we are to strive to love all—including our enemies. This is so counter-intuitive to the “natural” human, Kierkegaard claims, that it needs to be commanded (WL 24–5, SKS 9, 32–3)." (§3.3.2); see [Jesus of Nazareth](jesus.md).

## In dialogue with

- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** (earlier). The dissertation was "dealing with irony as practiced by Socrates" (§1). Of *Philosophical Fragments*, Lippitt & Evans (§3.3.1): "He begins with an account of the Socratic view (understood very much in terms of Plato’s Socrates) of “the Truth” and how it is obtained, and then seeks an alternative to the Socratic view that the Truth can be found through “recollection”."
- **[G. W. F. Hegel](hegel.md)** (earlier). Lippitt & Evans (preamble): "scholarship in recent decades has challenged and complicated this view, suggesting both that some of Kierkegaard’s central ideas are creative developments of Hegel’s ideas, and that the main target of his critique is certain Danish Hegelians influential in his day, rather than Hegel himself (see especially Stewart 2003 and section 4 below)." Hare (§4): "Kierkegaard mocked Hegel constantly for presuming to understand the whole system in which human history is embedded, while still being located in a particular small part of it." and "On the other hand, he used Hegelian categories of thought himself, especially in his idea of the aesthetic life, the ethical life and the religious life as stages through which human beings develop by means of first internal contradiction and then radical transition." Thulstrup (1967) and Stewart (2003) disagree; see Reception. Stewart, *Kierkegaard’s Relations to Hegel Reconsidered*, DOI [10.1017/CBO9780511498367](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511498367).
- **[Immanuel Kant](kant.md)** (earlier). Hare (§4): "Kierkegaard's relation with Kant was problematic as well." and "On the other hand, his own description of the religious life is full of echoes of Kant's Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason." Kosch, *Freedom and Reason in Kant, Schelling, and Kierkegaard* (2006), DOI [10.1093/0199289115.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199289115.001.0001), reads the ethical of *Fear and Trembling* as "Fichtean (Kosch 2006)" (§3.2).
- **[David Hume](hume.md)** (earlier, as a resemblance Lippitt & Evans draw): Climacus's argument on reflection and choice "resembles David Hume’s claim that “reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions…”" (§2).
- **[Jesus of Nazareth](jesus.md)** — the love commandment as read in *Works of Love* (above).
- **John Duns Scotus** (earlier, on the same story): the command to Abraham as a divine dispensation (Hare §3, above).
- **Heidegger, Sartre and others** (later). Preamble: "Kierkegaard’s thought has certainly influenced thinkers in the phenomenological and existential traditions (including Heidegger, Sartre, Jaspers, Marcel, and Lévinas), but also thinkers in very different philosophical traditions, such as Wittgenstein (who famously described him as a “saint” and “by far the most profound thinker” of the nineteenth century)." Hare (§5): "Heidegger drew here from Kierkegaard," "On the other hand he is unlike Kierkegaard in thinking of traditional Christianity as just one more convention making authentic existence more difficult."
- **Alasdair MacIntyre** (later). See Reception.
- **[Bernard Williams](williams.md)** and others (later, as parallels drawn by scholars): "Besides MacIntyre, these would include Harry Frankfurt (see, e.g., Mooney 1996, esp. 65–76 and the essays in Rudd and Davenport 2015); Iris Murdoch (e.g., Martens 2012); Charles Taylor (e.g., Khan 2012) and Bernard Williams (e.g., Mooney 1996, esp. 65–76, Compaijen 2018)." (§4).
- **C. Stephen Evans** (later). *Kierkegaard’s Ethic of Love: Divine Commands and Moral Obligations* (2004), DOI [10.1093/0199272174.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199272174.001.0001) (not read here; as Hare reports it, above).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **The label.** "Also often dubbed the “father of existentialism”, this label obscures at least as much as it reveals, especially to those who associate existentialism with atheistic figures such as Sartre." (Lippitt & Evans, preamble).
- **Kierkegaard and Hegel.** "For many years, Niels Thulstrup’s Kierkegaard’s Relation to Hegel (1967 [1980]) was thought to have decisively shown that Kierkegaard took little from Hegel. However, Stewart’s Kierkegaard’s Relations to Hegel Reconsidered (2003)—note the plural in the title—argues convincingly that many of the targets Kierkegaard had in mind in his attacks on Hegelianism were Danish followers of Hegel who may have misunderstood or misapplied Hegel in significant ways." ("argues convincingly" is Lippitt & Evans's assessment). Against: "Critics have disagreed as to whether Stewart’s analysis closes the gap between Kierkegaard and Hegel excessively, for instance regarding the two figures’ different views of the relationship between philosophy and religion." (§4).
- **MacIntyre's charge and the reply.** "A related debate has arisen from Alasdair MacIntyre’s claim, in his A Short History of Ethics and After Virtue, that the choice between the aesthetic and the ethical is arbitrary or “criterionless”, (MacIntyre 1981 [1984]). MacIntyre’s view has been much discussed since, though Kierkegaard scholars have been almost unanimous in their rejection of it." "MacIntyre subsequently amended his position, while nevertheless re-stating the “criterionless choice” charge in a different form (see MacIntyre 2001, esp. 344)." (§3.2; the "almost unanimous" count is Lippitt & Evans's).
- **Two fault lines.** "Another major fault line in Kierkegaard scholarship concerns the divide between scholars who take Kierkegaard’s Christian commitments seriously and those who find them something of an embarrassment, and whose approach to the texts seems to be to ask what can be salvaged despite these commitments." "The latter type of work has sometimes taken the form of reading Kierkegaard “against Kierkegaard” (e.g., Adorno 1933 [1989], Theunissen 1993 [2005])." (§4).
- **Interpretation in general.** "He has thus been interpreted in many—often incompatible—ways, and this is likely to continue to be so." (§5).
- **Politics, from two sides.** "His perspective on religion and society is likely to elicit objections from opposite sides of the contemporary political spectrum, since he sharply criticizes “Christendom”, including contemporary versions of Christian nationalism (see, e.g., Backhouse 2011), while also rejecting the claim of some liberals that religion should be excluded from our common life, which requires a supposedly neutral, disengaged stance that Kierkegaard sees as neither possible nor desirable." (§5).
- **An early English reading.** Hollander (1923) quotes Kierkegaard on how *Either/Or* was received: "As he said himself: "With my left hand I held out to the world 'Either-Or,' with my right, 'Two Edifying Discourses'; but they all--or practically all--seized with their right hands what I held in my left.""

## Vocabulary

- *The ethical*: not one fixed sense. Lippitt & Evans (§3.2): "In both Either/Or and Fear and Trembling (in the judgment of most scholars), the ethical is seen primarily as Sittlichkeit: grounded—as for Hegel—in social requirements and expectations." In *Fear and Trembling* "the ethical as such is the universal"; in the *Postscript* it opens onto Religiousness A (above).
- *Teleological suspension of the ethical*: the question of Problem I of *Fear and Trembling* (§3.3.1); no vocabulary page yet.
- *Paradox*: in *Fear and Trembling*, "the “paradox” that the “single individual” stands higher than the universal" (§3.3.1); in *Fragments*, "The incarnation is claimed to be “the Absolute Paradox”," (§3.3.1). Neither is the implant's normative [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md), an argument to an unacceptable conclusion.
- *The absurd*: for de silentio, "not one of the categories which belong to the understanding proper" (Hollander tr.).
- *Spheres* / *stages* of existence: "A common way to interpret Kierkegaard’s thought is in terms of three “existence-spheres”: the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious." (§3); not a [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md) in the implant's sense, though the book is titled *Either/Or*.

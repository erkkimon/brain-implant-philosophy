## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth:** "Arthur Schopenhauer came into the world on February 22, 1788 in Danzig [Gdansk, Poland]" (Wicks §1).
- **Study:** "In 1809, Schopenhauer began studies at the University of Göttingen, where he remained for two years, first majoring in medicine, and then, philosophy. In Göttingen, he absorbed the views of the skeptical philosopher, Gottlob Ernst Schulze (1761–1833), who introduced him to Plato and Kant." Then: "Schopenhauer next enrolled at the University of Berlin (1811–13), where his lecturers included Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762–1814) and Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834)." (§1).
- **Doctorate, 1813:** for *The Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason*, "Schopenhauer submitted his dissertation to the nearby University of Jena and was awarded a doctorate in philosophy in absentia." (§1).
- **Main work:** in Dresden "he developed ideas from The Fourfold Root into his most famous book, The World as Will and Representation, that was completed in March of 1818 and published in December of that same year (with the date, 1819)." (§1). The second volume "was published in 1844 along with the first volume in a combined second edition"; the third edition appeared in 1859 (§1).
- **Berlin, 1820:** "in March of 1820, Schopenhauer self-assuredly scheduled his class at a time that was simultaneous with Hegel’s popular lectures, and few students chose to hear Schopenhauer." (§1).
- **Frankfurt:** "Shortly thereafter, in June of 1833, he settled permanently in Frankfurt, where he remained for the next twenty-seven years" (§1).
- **The two prize essays:** *On the Freedom of Human Will* (1839) won the Norwegian Society's prize; *On the Basis of Morality* "was not honored with an award by The Royal Danish Society of the Sciences in Copenhagen, even though it was the sole submission in their essay competition." "In 1841, Schopenhauer defiantly published both essays together as Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics (Die Beiden Grundprobleme der Ethik)." (§1; "defiantly" is Wicks's word).
- **Recognition:** after *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851), "within a couple of years, he began to receive the philosophical recognition for which he had long hoped." (§1).
- **Death:** "Schopenhauer died peacefully on September 21, 1860, in his apartment in Frankfurt at Schöne Aussicht 16." (§1).
- **Indian sources (as Wicks reports them, §4):** "Only a few months after completing his dissertation, Schopenhauer was exposed to classical Indian thought in late 1813 by the orientalist Friedrich Majer (1771–1818), who visited Johanna Schopenhauer’s salon in Weimar." "As the records of his library book withdrawals indicate, Schopenhauer began reading the Bhagavadgita in December 1813 or very soon thereafter, and the Upanishads in March 1814, coincident with the time when Schopenhauer’s thought assumed an explicitly atheistic quality." The Upanishads he read were the Latin *Oupnekhat*: "in 1801, a Persian version of the Upanishads (the Oupnekhat) was rendered into Latin by the French Orientalist, Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron (1731-1805)". Schopenhauer's own statement (Preface to the First Edition), of the Vedas: "the access to which, opened to us through the Upanishads, is in my eyes the greatest advantage which this still young century enjoys over previous ones,"

## Problems addressed

- **The basis of morality** (*On the Basis of Morality*, 1840). The Danish Society asked: "Is the fountain and basis of Morals to be sought for in an idea of morality which lies directly in the consciousness (or conscience), and in the analysis of the other leading ethical conceptions which arise from it? or is it to be found an some other source of knowledge?" (The Question, Bullock's English, "an" sic).
  His answer: "The absence of all egoistic motives is thus the Criterion of an action of moral value." (III.iv). The incentive meeting it is "the phaenomenon of Compassion; in other words, the direct participation, independent of all ulterior considerations, in the sufferings of another, leading to sympathetic assistance in the effort to prevent or remove them;" (III.v).
  "It is this Compassion alone which is the real basis of all voluntary justice and all genuine loving-kindness. Only so far as an action springs therefrom, has it moral value; and all conduct that proceeds from any other motive whatever has none." (III.v). He calls it "the great mystery of Ethics, its original phaenomenon," (III.v).
  The three springs: "For as the fundamental springs of all human conduct (_v_. Chapter V. of this Part), are three, namely, Egoism, Malice, Compassion;" (III.vii). The principle: "_Neminem laede; immo omnes, quantum potes, juva._ (Do harm to no one; but rather help all people, as far as lies in your power.)" (III.v), whose two clauses ground the virtues of justice (III.vi) and loving-kindness (III.vii).
  Wicks (§5.2) contrasts this with Kant: "This compassionate way of apprehending another person is not merely understanding abstractly the proposition that “each person is a human being,” or understanding abstractly (as would Kant) that, in principle, the same regulations of rationality operate equally in each of us and oblige us accordingly as equals." In the main work the same thesis reads: "It is this: “All love (αγαπη, _caritas_) is sympathy.”" (WWR I §66).
- **The critique of Kant's ethics** (*Basis*, Part II, "Critique of Kant's Basis of Ethics"). On duty and command: "This conception, equally with the kindred notions of Law, Command, Obligation, etc., taken thus unconditionally, has its source in theological morals, and it will remain a stranger to philosophical morals, so long as it fails to furnish sufficient credentials drawn either from man's nature, or from the objective world." (II.ii).
  On the categorical imperative: "From this analysis it is abundantly clear that Kant's famous leading principle is not--as he maintains with tireless repetition--a categorical, but in reality a hypothetical Imperative;" with "its concealed hypothetical nature, whereby its basis turns out to be nothing else than Egoism, the latter being the secret interpreter of the direction which it contains." (II.vi). Summing up: "the imperative form of Ethics, is valid only in theological morals, outside of which it loses all sense and meaning." (III.ii). These are Schopenhauer's charges; Kant's positions are on the [Kant](kant.md) page.
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).** Against Kant's view (*Tugendlehre* §§16–17) that cruelty to animals is wrong as a duty to oneself: "So one is only to have compassion on animals for the sake of practice, and they are as it were the pathological phantom on which to train one's sympathy with men!" (II.vi). He offers inclusion of animals as evidence for his own principle: "There is another proof that the moral incentive disclosed by me is the true one. I mean the fact that animals also are included under its protecting aegis." (III.viii); "Boundless compassion for all living beings is the surest and most certain guarantee of pure moral conduct, and needs no casuistry." (III.viii); "Compassion for animals is intimately connected with goodness of character, and it may be confidently asserted that he, who is cruel to living creatures, cannot be a good man." (III.viii). The problem page sets out Kant's indirect-duty view and its critics.
- **Lying** (III.vi, *The Virtue of Justice*). "Every mendacious word is a wrong, excepting only when the occasion arises of defending oneself against violence or cunning." The chapter's cases are a traveller asked about his money and a suitor; it does not discuss the case on the [murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md) page (structural note: the link is the implant's, by topic).
- **Pessimism** (WWR I, Book IV). "Thus its life swings like a pendulum backwards and forwards between pain and ennui." (§57). Wicks (§4): "This marks the origin of Schopenhauer’s renowned pessimism: he claims that as individuals, we are the anguished products of our own epistemological making, and that within the world of appearances that we structure, we are fated to fight with other individuals, and to want more than we can ever have." Shapshay (SEP "Schopenhauer's Aesthetics", §1) defines it as "the view that sentient beings, with few exceptions, are bound to strive and suffer greatly, all without any ultimate purpose or justification and thus life is not really worth living." Wicks (preamble) qualifies the label: "Often considered to be a thoroughgoing pessimist, Schopenhauer in fact advocated ways – via artistic, moral and ascetic forms of awareness – to overcome a frustration-filled and fundamentally painful human condition."
- **The denial of the will to live** (WWR I §§66–71). Beyond justice lies "actual renunciation, denial of the will to live,—asceticism, of which we shall speak last." (§66). Wicks (§5.3): "The result is an attitude of denial towards our will-to-live that Schopenhauer identifies with an ascetic attitude of renunciation, resignation, and will-lessness, but also with composure and tranquillity." and "St. Francis of Assisi (WWR, Section 68) and Jesus (WWR, Section 70) subsequently emerge as Schopenhauer’s prototypes for the most enlightened lifestyle, in conjunction with the ascetics from every religious tradition." Volume I ends: "Rather do we freely acknowledge that what remains after the entire abolition of will is for all those who are still full of will certainly nothing; but, conversely, to those in whom the will has turned and has denied itself, this our world, which is so real, with all its suns and milky-ways—is nothing." (§71). Shapshay (§6.2) reads the denial as admitting degrees: "Schopenhauer recommends resignation (extreme denial of the will to life) but his ethical theory admits of degrees of negation—by acting in a less egoistic fashion or further in a truly compassionate manner."
- **Will and representation** (WWR I §1). "“The world is my idea:”—this is a truth which holds good for everything that lives and knows, though man alone can bring it into reflective and abstract consciousness." and "a man can also say and must say, “the world is my will.”" On space and time as *principium individuationis* see [individuation](../vocabulary/individuation.md).
- **Freedom of the will.** Wicks (§5.2): "Schopenhauer maintains similarly in his “Essay on the Freedom of the Will” (1839) that everything that happens, happens necessarily." No problem page in the implant treats this essay.

## In dialogue with

- **[Kant](kant.md)** (earlier). Schulze introduced him to Kant (Wicks §1, quoted above). In ethics he attacks Kant's imperative form and his indirect duties regarding animals (*Basis* Part II; see Problems addressed). Wicks (§5.2) nonetheless places him beside Kant: "Far from being immoralistic, his moral theory is written in the same vein as those of Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) and John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), that advocate principles that are in general accord with Christian precepts." The SEP entry's §3, *Schopenhauer’s Critique of Kant* (on the thing-in-itself), was not read in detail here.
- **[Plato](plato.md)** (earlier). Read through Schulze (Wicks §1); Wicks (§4) reports that "Schopenhauer refers to the Platonic Ideas as the direct objectifications of Will and as the immediate objectivity of Will."
- **[Spinoza](spinoza.md)** (earlier). Wicks (§4): "A primary inspiration for Schopenhauer’s double-aspect view of the universe is Baruch (Benedict) de Spinoza (1632–1677), who developed a similarly-structured metaphysics, and who Schopenhauer had studied in his early years before writing his dissertation."
- **Fichte, Schelling and [Hegel](hegel.md)** (contemporaries). Wicks (§2) reports "his uncompromising ad hominem attacks on Fichte, Schelling and Hegel, who he described repeatedly as “humbugs” and “charlatans.”" The Danish Society's judgment (Bullock's English) objected that "he mentions several recent philosophers of the highest standing in an unseemly manner, such as to justly occasion serions offence." ("serions" sic); Wicks (§1) names them: "(viz., Fichte and Hegel)".
- **Indian and Buddhist texts** (earlier). Besides the reading record above, Wicks (§5.2) reports "he states explicitly that his views on morality are entirely in the spirit of Christianity, as well as being consistent with the doctrines and ethical precepts of the sacred books of India (WWR, Section 68)." In §66 Schopenhauer gives the ethical sense of the Vedic formula: "by the formula of the Vedas already quoted: “Tat twam asi!” (This thou art!) Whoever is able to say this to himself, with regard to every being with whom he comes in contact, with clear knowledge and firm inward conviction, is certain of all virtue and blessedness, and is on the direct road to salvation." Wicks (§5.3): "In a manner reminiscent of traditional Buddhism, he recognizes that life is filled with unavoidable frustration and acknowledges that the suffering caused by this frustration can itself be reduced by minimizing one’s desires." Wicks (§4) calls the Upanishads "A subsequent, but often highlighted inspiration" after Spinoza.
- **[Nietzsche](nietzsche.md)** (later). Anderson (SEP "Friedrich Nietzsche", §1) reports that Nietzsche published essays "on Schopenhauer, and on Wagner"; §2: "To highlight just one example, Nietzsche attacks the value of compassion (another central element in Schopenhauer’s moral theory)." and "The Genealogy misses no chance to criticize religious versions of asceticism, but its target is broader—including, specifically, the more rational form asceticism takes in Schopenhauer’s ethics." On Reginster's reading (Anderson §3.2.1): "By replacing Schopenhauer’s will to life with his will to power (understood as a drive to overcome resistance, which wills the world’s resistance along with its overcoming; KSA 9[151] 12: 424), Nietzsche can argue that our basic condition as desiring, striving creatures can lead to a mode of existence worthy of endorsement, rather than to inevitable frustration (as Schopenhauer had it)." *The Birth of Tragedy* §1 quotes Schopenhauer's sailor and his *principium individuationis* (`raw/nietzsche-1872-birth-of-tragedy-1-principium-individuationis-haussmann.md`).
- **Wittgenstein** (later). Biletzki and Matar (SEP "Ludwig Wittgenstein", §2.1) say the *Tractatus*, coming out of the notes and *Notebooks* of 1913–16, was "showing Schopenhauerian and other cultural influences". Shapshay (§1) links Schopenhauer's "intuitive knowledge" to "as Wittgenstein would later put it, of “seeing the world aright.”"
- **[Jung](jung.md)** (later). Fordham, as the Jung page quotes him: "He also studied the Greek philosophers, Hegel, and Schopenhauer, whose relation to Kant drew his attention;" (`raw/encyclopedia-com-jung-reference-entries.md`).
- **[William James](william-james.md)** (later) names him with Kant among "schoolmasters deciding what all must think," (`raw/james-1897-will-to-believe-moral-philosopher-dilemma-of-determinism.md`, p. 204).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **The Danish Royal Society (1840),** in Bullock's English: "The writer attempts to show that compassion is the ultimate source of morality; but neither does his mode of discussion appear satisfactory to us, nor has he, in point of fact, succeeded in proving that such a foundation is adequate." Wicks (§1) summarises: "The Society claimed that Schopenhauer did not answer the assigned question and that he gravely disrespected philosophers with outstanding reputations (viz., Fichte and Hegel)."
- **Nietzsche,** as Shapshay (§1) reports him: "By the 1870s, Arthur Schopenhauer’s philosophy had gained, in Nietzsche’s words “ascendency in Europe” (GM III, §5)." His objections to compassion and asceticism are under In dialogue with.
- **Wicks on standing (preamble, §8).** "Arthur Schopenhauer was among the first 19th century philosophers to contend that at its core, the universe is not a rational place." "Schopenhauer’s philosophy has been widely influential, partly because his outlook acknowledges traditional moral values without the need to postulate the existence of God." Philosophers he names: "Among philosophers, one can cite Henri Bergson, Julius Bahnsen, Eduard von Hartmann, Suzanne Langer, Philipp Mainländer, Hans Vaihinger, and Friedrich Nietzsche, where each tended to focus on selected aspects of Schopenhauer’s philosophy, such as his views on the meaning of life, his theory of the non-rational will, his theory of music, or his Kantianism." On Freud: "Schopenhauer’s ideas about the importance of instinctual urges at the core of daily life also reappeared in Freud’s surrealism-inspiring psychoanalytic thought" (§8).
- **Shapshay's list (§1):** "Indeed, late-19th and early-20th century philosophers, writers, composers and artists such as Nietzsche, Wagner, Brahms, Freud, Wittgenstein, Horkheimer, Hardy, Mann, Rilke, Proust, Tolstoy, Borges, Mahler, Langer and Schönberg were influenced by Schopenhauer’s thought."
- **Wicks's critical points (§§5.3, 6, 7).** On asceticism: "This emphasis upon the ascetic consciousness and its associated detachment and tranquillity introduces some paradox into Schopenhauer’s outlook, for he admits that the denial of our will-to-live entails a terrible struggle with instinctual energies, as we avoid the temptations of bodily pleasures and resist the mere animal force to endure, reproduce, and flourish." On the mystical state: "This advocacy of mystical experience creates a puzzle: if everything is Will without qualification, then it is unclear where to locate the will-less mystical state of mind." On readings of the thing-in-itself: "It also implies that interpretations that portray him as a traditional metaphysician who claims that the thing-in-itself is straightforwardly, wholly and unconditionally Will, also stand in need of qualification." And of his own tempered reading: "This tempered approach, though, does leave us with the decisive question of why the world would appear to be so violent, if the universe’s core is not thoroughly “Will,” but is also something mysterious beyond this."

## Vocabulary

- *Will* (*Wille*): the inner side of the world, "the world is my will" (WWR I §1); Wicks (§4): "an aimless, blind striving"; Shapshay (§1): "a blind, purposeless urge or striving akin to energy". Not the implant's sense of a person's faculty of choice; no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Representation* / *Idea* (*Vorstellung*): Haldane & Kemp's "idea", Wicks's "representation" — the world as object for a subject (WWR I §1).
- *Compassion* / *sympathy* (*Mitleid*): participation in another's suffering as a motive (*Basis* III.v); translators differ ("compassion" in Bullock and Wicks, "sympathy" in Haldane & Kemp).
- *Principium individuationis*: space and time as what makes many individuals; a separate sense on the [individuation](../vocabulary/individuation.md) page.
- *Denial of the will to live*: his name for ascetic renunciation (WWR I §§66–71); "will to life" in Shapshay and Anderson.
- *Pessimism*: the label others apply (Shapshay's definition above); Wicks (preamble) reports it as how he is "Often considered".

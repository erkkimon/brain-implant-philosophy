## Facts a claim depends on

- **Name:** "“Mencius” is a Latinization (coined by Jesuit missionaries in the 17th century) of the Chinese “Mengzi,” meaning Master Meng. His full name was “Meng Ke.”" (Van Norden §1).
- **Dates:** Van Norden (preamble): "Mencius (fourth century BCE) was a Confucian philosopher." His note 6: "Traditionally, Mencius is said to have lived from 372–289 BCE (Legge 1970, 16). However, many recent scholars put his lifetime about twenty years earlier."
  and "Mencius is treated as a respected older man in the Mengzi, so it is likely that he was born around 390 BCE." Richey's title gives the traditional dates: "Mencius (c. 372—289 B.C.E.)".
- **Birthplace and teacher:** "Details of Mencius’s early life are sketchy and uncertain, but he is said to have been born in the state of Zou, in what is now Shandong Province." "Mencius is said to have studied under Confucius’s grandson Zisi" (Van Norden §1).
- **Relation to Confucius:** "Mencius was born about a century after the death of Confucius." (§1). He quotes Mencius at 2A2: "Ever since man came into this world, there has never been one greater than Confucius" (Lau tr.). See [Confucius](confucius.md).
- **Office:** "Mencius held office in the state of Qi (2B6–7)." "Mencius eventually resigned his position, when it became clear to him that the ruler was unwilling to enact any of the reforms that he proposed (2B10–13)." (§1).
- **The text:** "This work was probably compiled by his disciples or disciples of his disciples. It was subsequently edited and shortened by Zhao Qi in the second century C.E., who also wrote a commentary on the text." (§1).

## Problems addressed

- **Human nature: "the nature is good"** (6A6; 3A1). No problem page yet.
  Van Norden (§3) on the term: "As A.C. Graham (1967) demonstrated in a classic essay, Mencius and his contemporaries regarded the nature of X as the characteristics that X will develop if given a healthy environment for the kind of thing X is."
  He counts that "the specific phrase “[human] nature is good” only occurs in two of these (3A1, 6A6)". Side by side, 6A6:

  | Legge (1861) | Van Norden (2008, as SEP §3 quotes it) |
  |---|---|
  | "From the feelings proper to it, it is constituted for the practice of what is good. This is what I mean in saying that the nature is good. If men do what is not good, the blame cannot be imputed to their natural powers." | "As for what they are inherently [qíng], they can become good." |
  | "Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge are not infused into us from without. We are certainly furnished with them. And a different view is simply owing to want of reflection." | (the same passage continues in SEP §3) |

  Van Norden cites Kwong-loi Shun's summary: "Mencius was saying that human beings have a constitution comprising certain emotional predispositions that already point in the direction of goodness and by virtue of which people are capable of becoming good" (Shun 1997, 219–20).
- **The four sprouts** (2A6). Legge: "The feeling of commiseration is the principle of benevolence. The feeling of shame and dislike is the principle of righteousness. The feeling of modesty and complaisance is the principle of propriety. The feeling of approving and disapproving is the principle of knowledge. Men have these four principles just as they have their four limbs."
  Van Norden (§2): "Mencius holds that all humans have innate but incipient tendencies toward benevolence, righteousness, wisdom, and propriety. Employing an agricultural metaphor, he refers to these tendencies as “sprouts” (2A6)."
  2A6 on development: "Let them have their complete development, and they will suffice to love and protect all within the four seas. Let them be denied that development, and they will not suffice for a man to serve his parents with." (Legge).
- **The child at the well** (2A6). Van Norden (§4) calls it "His most famous argument for this conclusion is the thought experiment of the child at the well:" — the conclusion being that there is more to human nature than self-interest, against Yang Zhu. Side by side:

  | Legge (1861) | Van Norden (2008, as SEP §4 quotes it) |
  |---|---|
  | "All men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others." | — |
  | "even now-a-days, if men suddenly see a child about to fall into a well, they will without exception experience a feeling of alarm and distress. They will feel so, not as a ground on which they may gain the favour of the child's parents, nor as a ground on which they may seek the praise of their neighbours and friends, nor from a dislike to the reputation of having been unmoved by such a thing." | "Suppose someone suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well: anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion—not because one sought to get in good with the child’s parents, not because one wanted fame among one’s neighbors and friends, and not because one would dislike the sound of the child’s cries." |

  Structural note (manifest G4): the two translations differ on the third excluded motive — Legge's "reputation of having been unmoved", Van Norden's "the sound of the child’s cries".
- **The Niu mountain** (6A8, Legge): "The trees of the Niu mountain were once beautiful. Being situated, however, in the borders of a large State, they were hewn down with axes and bills - and could they retain their beauty?"
  "but then came the cattle and goats and browsed upon them. To these things is owing the bare and stripped appearance of the mountain, and when people now see it, they think it was never finely wooded. But is this the nature of the mountain?"
  and "Therefore, if it receive its proper nourishment, there is nothing which will not grow. If it lose its proper nourishment, there is nothing which will not decay away."
  The SEP entry lists 6A8 among the 17 passages where "nature" occurs (§3) but does not discuss it further.
- **Extension, and the ox** (1A7) — bearing on [the moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).
  The king, in Legge: "The king said, Let it go. I cannot bear its frightened appearance, as if it were an innocent person going to the place of death."
  Mencius: "Now here is kindness sufficient to reach to animals, and no benefits are extended from it to the people. How is this?" (1A7).
  Van Norden (§2): "In a much–discussed example (1A7), Mencius draws a ruler’s attention to the fact that he had shown compassion for an ox being led to slaughter by sparing it."
  "In order to become genuinely virtuous, a person must “extend” or “fill out” his or her nascent virtuous inclinations." On animals and graded concern: "Benevolence is manifested in the affection one has for his or her own kin, as well as compassion for the suffering of other humans, and even concern for non–human animals."
  and "However, according to Confucians, one should always have greater concern for, and has greater obligations toward, relatives, teachers, and friends than one does for strangers and animals (7A45)." 7A45 in Legge: "In regard to inferior creatures, the superior man is kind to them, but not loving. In regard to people generally, he is loving to them, but not affectionate. He is affectionate to his parents, and lovingly disposed to people generally."
- **Ritual overridden: the drowning sister-in-law** (4A17) — bearing on [moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md) as a case of one requirement overriding another.
  Legge: "He who would not so rescue the drowning woman is a wolf. For males and females not to allow their hands to touch in giving and receiving is the general rule; when a sister-in-law is drowning, to rescue her with the hand is a peculiar exigency."
  Van Norden (§2): "Mencius thinks that matters of ritual place legitimate ethical demands on us, but he stresses that they are not categorical, and can be overridden by more exigent obligations."
  "For example, he notes that rescuing one’s sister–in–law if she were drowning would justify violation of the ritual prohibition on physical contact between unmarried men and women (4A17; cf. 5A2, 6B2). Knowing when to violate ritual is a matter of wisdom."
  Structural note (G4): neither source uses the word "dilemma" for 4A17; the link records the overriding relation Van Norden names.
- **[Just war](../problems/just-war.md)** — Van Norden (§1): "Mencius was asked, unofficially, whether it might be legitimate to invade Yan to restore order. He replied that it was. However, after Qi successfully invaded and annexed Yan, Mencius complained that he had not encouraged the specific actions that Qi took, which apparently included widespread killing of noncombatants and taking spoils of war (see 2B8, 1B10, 1B11, and 2B9, in that order)."
  (excerpt: `raw/sep-mohism-fraser-and-mencius-van-norden-fall-2024-war.md`). Van Norden (§1): conquest by war is like "climbing a tree in search of a fish" [1A7].
- **Profit against benevolence** (1A1, Legge): "Why must your Majesty use that word "profit?" What I am provided with, are counsels to benevolence and righteousness, and these are my only topics."

## Works

- The *Mengzi* (*Mencius*), the one work: "The received text of the Mengzi is divided into seven “books,” each of which is subdivided into two parts (labeled “A” and “B” in English), and then further subdivided into “chapters.”" (Van Norden §1).
  Locator scheme: book–part–passage (1A1 … 7B38). No page in [works](../works/index.md) yet.

## In dialogue with

- **Gaozi** (Legge: "The philosopher Gao said"; Richey: "Gaozi (Kao-tzu, 300s BCE)", "known only from the Mencius"), 6A1–6. Gaozi and Mencius side by side, in Legge:

  | Gaozi | Mencius |
  |---|---|
  | "Man's nature is like the qi-willow , and righteousness is like a cup or a bowl." (6A1) | "You must do violence and injury to the willow, before you can make cups and bowls with it." (6A1) |
  | "Man's nature is indifferent to good and evil, just as the water is indifferent to the east and west." (6A2) | "The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards." (6A2) |
  | "Life is what we call nature!" (6A3) | "Is the nature of a dog like the nature of an ox, and the nature of an ox like the nature of a man?" (6A3) |
  | "Benevolence is internal and not external; righteousness is external and not internal." (6A4) | (the exchange continues in 6A4; not excerpted) |

  Van Norden on 6A3: "Mencius performs a swift reductio ad absurdum, pointing out that—since dogs, oxen, and humans are the same in being alive—the proposed definition entails that dogs, oxen, and humans have the same nature (6A3)."
  His note 17 adds the reply open to Gaozi: "It is open to the rival philosopher to bite the bullet and accept this implication. However, Mencius has at the least raised a strong, prima facie objection."
  Richey: "Gaozi – whom later Confucians identified, probably anachronistically, as a Daoist — offers multiple hypotheses about human nature, each of which Mencius refutes in Socratic fashion."
- **Xunzi** (Richey: "Xunzi (Hsun-tzu, 310-220 BCE)"; "Xunzi knew him only retrospectively"). Van Norden (§5): "Xunzi attacks Mencius by name in his essay, “Human Nature Is Bad”:" — quoting it (Ivanhoe & Van Norden tr.):
  "Mengzi says: When people engage in learning, this manifests the goodness of their nature. I say: This is not so."
  "Those things in people that cannot be learned and cannot be worked at are called their “nature.” Those things in people that they become capable of through learning and that they achieve through working at them are called their “deliberate efforts.”"
  Xunzi's image for cultivation: "crooked wood must await steaming and straightening on the shaping frame, and only then does it become straight. Blunt metal must await honing and grinding, and only then does it become sharp".
  Richey: "Against Mencius, Xunzi defines human nature as what is inborn and unlearned, and then asks why education and ritual are necessary for Mencius if people really are good by nature."
  "Whereas Mencius claims that human beings are originally good but argues for the necessity of self-cultivation, Xunzi claims that human beings are originally bad but argues that they can be reformed, even perfected, through self-cultivation."
  Two readings of the disagreement, both Van Norden's report and his own view: "It is common to observe that Mencius and Xunzi are speaking at cross–purposes, because they assume different conceptions of the key term “nature” (Graham 1967)."
  and "However, it is possible that Mencius and Xunzi still have a substantive disagreement, even if Xunzi’s way of framing that disagreement is misleading."
  Richey sees a shared ground: "the two thinkers share many assumptions, including one that links each to Confucius: the assumption that human beings can be transformed by participation in traditional aesthetic, moral, and social disciplines."
- **Yang Zhu and Mozi.** Van Norden (§4): "Mencius viewed himself as a defender of the Confucian tradition, and saw the two major contemporary threats to that tradition as the doctrines of Mozi and Yang Zhu".
  "Mencius’s doctrine that human nature is good was part of his strategy to present a Confucian response to both the Mohists and Yang Zhu."
  On Yang Zhu (whose views, Van Norden notes, are disputed: "There is controversy about what the views of Yang Zhu (fifth to fourth century BCE) were."): "However, he argued that Yang Zhu had an implausibly impoverished conception of what human nature is like."
  On the Mohists, Van Norden's summary of Mencius's view: "In summary, the impartial caring advocated by the Mohists is both perverse and impractical."
- **Zhuangzi.** "In his own era, Mencius’s views were criticized by the Daoist Zhuangzi and by his fellow Confucian Xunzi." Van Norden reads Zhuangzi 2 as aimed at the sprouts: "From where I see it, the sprouts of benevolence and righteousness and the pathways of right and wrong are all snarled and jumbled" (§5).
- **[Confucius](confucius.md)** — see Facts above; Zhu Xi's reconciliation of the two below.
- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** — compared, not in dialogue: "Mencian wisdom (somewhat like Aristotelian phronēsis) seems to be only partially constrained by rules." (Van Norden §2).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing.** Van Norden (preamble): "Mencius (fourth century BCE) was a Confucian philosopher. Often referred to as the “Second Sage” of Confucianism (meaning second in importance only to Confucius himself), Mencius is best known for his claim that “human nature is good.”"
  Han Yu: "In addition to denouncing Buddhist practices as unnatural and antithetical to the well being of the state, Han Yu singles out Mencius as the “most pure” of the followers of Confucius." (§5).
- **Zhu Xi and the "fundamentally good" gloss.** "The statement that “[human] nature is good [xìng shàn]” is often misquoted as “[human] nature is fundamentally good [xìng bĕn shàn].” The latter phrase actually derives from a gloss on Mencius’s view popularized by the seminal commentator Zhu Xi (1130–1200)."
  "The most influential Neo–Confucian philosopher was Zhu Xi, who grouped the Mengzi along with three other Confucian classics (the Great Learning, Analects, and Mean) as the Four Books, and wrote clear, detailed commentaries on each."
  Van Norden's assessment of the gloss: "While ingenious, this interpretation of Mencius is unmotivated without the assumption that the Analects and Mengzi must be expressing the same view." (§3).
- **The circularity objection.** Van Norden states it: "This thesis runs the danger of becoming viciously circular: we might characterize goodness as the result of growing up in a healthy environment, and a healthy environment as one that results in humans being good."
  Graham's side, per note 11: "Graham (1967, 14–15) takes this to be a decisive objection to Mencius’s view."
  Van Norden's side: "Mencius’s views might be mistaken, but they cannot be casually dismissed as naively circular; rather, they must be evaluated as a complex and subtle account of ethics and philosophical psychology."
- **Wang Yangming, Dai Zhen, Mou Zongsan.** "Wang Yangming (1472–1529) famously argued for the “unity of knowing and acting,” as the most plausible interpretation of Mencius’s view" (§2).
  Dai Zhen, per Van Norden (§5): "This criticism was made within the Chinese tradition by later Confucians such as Dai Zhen (1724–1777), who argued that, due to Buddhist influence, the Neo–Confucians understood terms like “Pattern” in anachronistic ways, and were led to an ethics that unnaturally devalued physical human desires (see Dai 1777)."
  Richey: "During the Qing (Ch’ing) dynasty (1644-1911 CE), late Confucian thinkers such as Dai Zhen (Tai Chen, 1724-1777 CE) developed critiques of Xunzi that aimed at the vindication of Mencius’ position on human nature."
  Mou: "many New Confucians are influenced by the views of Mou Zongsan (1909–1995), who argued that Mencius and the Neo–Confucian Wang Yangming accurately explicated what is implicit in the views of Confucius."
- **Contemporary debates, both sides as reported.** On 5A3 and 7A35: "Some argue that this illustrates that Mencius (and Confucianism in general) is committed to a form of nepotism that encourages corruption (see, e.g., Liu 2007). Others have argued for alternative interpretations of the significance of these passages (e.g., Guo 2007)."
  On democracy: "Mencius has been invoked as providing a potential foundation for democracy (Bai 2008), but others have cautioned about overestimating the extent to which he champions the will of the people as the source of political authority (Tiwald 2008)."
- **Virtue-ethics readings.** "Another recent trend in the study of Mencius and other Confucian philosophers has been the application of the framework of virtue ethics, beginning with Yearley (1990)."
  Against: "New Confucian and virtue ethics interpreters of the Mengzi have both been criticized for projecting an alien vocabulary onto the text." (§5). Van Norden adds: "However, as advocates of virtue ethics interpretations acknowledge, there are also substantial differences between Mencius and any Western virtue ethics."

## Vocabulary

- *Nature* (xìng): in Graham's reading as Van Norden reports it, what a thing develops in a healthy environment (§3); Xunzi uses it for what "cannot be learned and cannot be worked at". The two senses differ; no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Sprouts* (duān): Legge translates "principles" in 2A6; Van Norden "sprouts".
- *Benevolence* (rén), *righteousness* (yì), *wisdom* (zhì), *propriety* (lĭ): the four, per Van Norden §2; Legge renders zhì "knowledge".
- *Extension* (tui and related terms) and *reflection* (sī): Van Norden §2.

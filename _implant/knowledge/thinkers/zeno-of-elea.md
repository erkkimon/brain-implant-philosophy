## Facts a claim depends on

- **No book survives; the evidence is second-hand.** Huggett (preamble): "Sadly this book has not survived, and what we know of his arguments is second-hand, principally through Aristotle and his commentators", and "almost none of these paradoxes are quoted in Zeno’s original words by their various commentators, but in paraphrase."
- **Sources.** Huggett: "Almost everything that we know about Zeno of Elea is to be found in the opening pages of Plato’s Parmenides." Palmer (§2.1.1) names the Neoplatonist "Simplicius (6th c. C.E.)" as quoting the plurality arguments verbatim; Huggett draws "particularly on Simplicius, who, though writing a thousand years after Zeno, apparently possessed at least some of his book".
- **Birth, estimated.** Plato makes Zeno "nearly 40 years of age" when "Socrates, then a very young man, came to see them" (*Parmenides* 127b–c, Jowett). Palmer (§1): "Given that Socrates was a little past seventy when executed by the Athenians in 399 B.C.E., this description suggests that Zeno was born about 490 B.C.E." Huggett: "Since Socrates was born in 469 BC we can estimate a birth date for Zeno around 490 BC."
- **Place and activity.** Palmer (§1): "He would appear to have been active in Magna Graecia, that is, the Greek-speaking regions of southern Italy, during the mid-fifth century B.C.E." and "There is otherwise little credible information about the circumstances of his life."
- **The tyrant story.** Palmer (§1) reports that Diogenes Laertius' life (IX.25–9) "is largely taken up with stitching together conflicting reports of his involvement in a brave plot to overthrow one of the local tyrants, but how much truth these reports contain cannot be determined."
- **Relation to Parmenides.** Plato: "in the days of his youth he was reported to have been beloved by Parmenides" (127b, Jowett). Diogenes Laertius (IX.25, Hicks): "Zeno, then, was all through a pupil of Parmenides and his bosom friend." Palmer calls Parmenides "his Eleatic mentor" (preamble).
- **The book.** Palmer (§1) reports that the *Parmenides* passage "is nonetheless normally taken as indicating that Zeno composed a single treatise comprising numerous arguments, cast in the form of antinomies, all purporting to demonstrate the untenability of the commonsense presumption that there are many things." Proclus, per Palmer (§1), knew "a work transmitted under Zeno’s name containing forty arguments or logoi (Procl. in Prm. 694, 17–18 Steel)"; Palmer judges such a work "unlikely to have been a fair replica of any original treatise of Zeno’s".
- **Earliest trace.** Palmer (§1) describes a mid-fifth-century red-figure cup from Falerii (Rome, Villa Giulia inv. 3591): "It depicts a heroic figure racing nimbly ahead of a large tortoise and has every appearance of being the first known “response” to the Achilles paradox."

## Problems addressed

- **[Zeno's paradoxes](../problems/zenos-paradoxes.md) — motion.** Aristotle (*Physics* VI.9, 239b10–14): "Zeno's arguments about motion, which cause so much disquietude to those who try to solve the problems that they present, are four in number." The four — Dichotomy, Achilles, Arrow, Stadium (Palmer calls the last "The Moving Rows", §2.2.4) — are stated there from Aristotle. Palmer (§2.2): "Thus reconstruction of these famous arguments rests almost exclusively on Aristotle’s incomplete presentation." Diogenes Laertius (IX.72) gives a one-line form: "A moving body moves neither where it is nor where it is not".
- **Plurality (on the same problem page; no separate page yet).** Simplicius quotes, in Palmer's translation (§2.1.1): "“If there are many things, it is necessary that they be just so many as they are and neither greater than themselves nor fewer. But if they are just as many as they are, they will be limited. If there are many things, the things that are are unlimited; for there are always others between these entities, and again others between those. And thus the things that are are unlimited” (Zeno fr. 3 DK, i.e., Simp. in Ph. 140.29–33 Diels)." Palmer: "This is the only Zenonian antinomy that has the appearance of being preserved in its entirety." A second (fr. 1–2 DK) ends: "Thus if there are many things, they must be both small and large, so small as to have no magnitude, and so large as to be unlimited’” (Zeno fr. 1 DK, = Simp. in Ph. 140.34–141.8)." Huggett (§2.1–2.3) treats these as the arguments from denseness, finite size and complete divisibility; for the third, "These words are Aristotle’s not Zeno’s", included on "Simplicius’ opinion (On Aristotle’s Physics, 139.24) that it originates with Zeno".
- **The antinomy form (structural note, this implant).** Palmer (§1) gives the pattern Plato's description suggests: "if there are many things, these must be both F and not-F; but things cannot be both F and not-F; therefore, it cannot be the case that there are many things." With M = "there are many things", `python3 _implant/skills/tools/logic.py check --premises "M -> F" "M -> ~F" --conclusion "~M"` reports `VALID`: the form is a valid reductio; whether the premises hold is the dispute ([validity](../vocabulary/validity.md)).
- **[Sorites paradox](../problems/sorites-paradox.md) — the millet seed.** Aristotle (*Physics* VII.5, 250a20–1) and Simplicius (in Ph. 1108.18–28) report it; Palmer (§2.3.1): "The evidence nonetheless suggests that Zeno anticipated reasoning related to that of the sorites paradox, apparently invented more than a century later."
- **Place (no page).** Palmer (§2.3.2), from Aristotle: "“Zeno raises the problem that, if place is something, it will be in something” (Arist. Ph. 4.3, 210b22–3".

## Works

No works page; nothing Zeno wrote survives except the fragments Simplicius
quotes (Huggett, preamble; Palmer §2.1). Locators: Diels–Kranz fragment
numbers (fr. 1–3 DK) with Simplicius' *On Aristotle's Physics* by Diels page
and line (e.g. 140.29–33); Aristotle by Bekker number.

- **The book** read at Athens in Plato's *Parmenides* (127c–128e). Zeno, in Plato's telling: "Zeal for my master led me to write the book in the days of my youth, but some one stole the copy; and therefore I had no choice whether it should be published or not; the motive, however, of writing, was not the ambition of an elder man, but the pugnacity of a young one." (128d–e, Jowett). Palmer (§3) calls the claim that the book "was stolen and circulated" before Zeno chose to publish "historically unverifiable".
- ***The Forty Arguments of Zeno*** — the work Proclus knew under Zeno's name (Palmer §1), above.

## In dialogue with

- **Parmenides** (earlier; no page). Huggett (preamble): "he wrote a book of paradoxes defending Parmenides’ philosophy". In Plato, Zeno says: "The truth is, that these writings of mine were meant to protect the arguments of Parmenides against those who make fun of him and seek to show the many ridiculous and contradictory results which they suppose to follow from the affirmation of the one." (128c–d). The reading of this is disputed — see Reception.
- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** (later, as Plato portrays him). In the *Parmenides* Socrates asks: "What is your meaning, Zeno? Do you maintain that if being is many, it must be both like and unlike, and that this is impossible, for neither can the like be unlike, nor the unlike like-is that your position? Just so, said Zeno." (127e) and tells Parmenides "You affirm unity, he denies plurality." (128a–b). Zeno replies that Socrates does "not fully apprehend the true motive of the composition" (128c).
- **[Plato](plato.md)** (later). Beyond the *Parmenides*, Palmer (§3) reports the *Alcibiades* (119a) — "Pythodorus and Callias each paid Zeno a hundred minae to become clever and skilled in argument" — and the *Phaedrus*' "Eleatic Palamedes" (261d6–8).
- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** (later). States the four motion arguments and answers them (*Physics* VI.2, VI.9, VIII.8); see the side-by-side table below. Palmer (§3) reports that Aristotle, in his lost *Sophist*, "spoke of Zeno as the inventor of dialectic (D.L. 8.57; cf. 9.25; S.E. M. 7.7)", while he "also attributes the invention of dialectic to Socrates (Arist. Metaph. M.4, 1078b25–30) and to Plato (Metaph. A.6, 987b31–3)".
- **Anaxagoras, Leucippus and Democritus** (later; no pages). Palmer (§3): "Zeno’s powerful principle that any spatially extended entity must be limitlessly divisible would profoundly impact the development of the subtle and powerful physical theories of both Anaxagoras, who accepts the principle, and the early atomists, Leucippus and Democritus, who reject it." Huggett (§5): "Aristotle (On Generation and Corruption 316b34) claims that our third argument—the one concerning complete divisibility—was what convinced the atomists that there must be smallest, indivisible parts of matter."
- **Protagoras, Gorgias and the sophists** (later; no pages). Palmer (§3) reports Kerferd (1981): "Zeno’s paradoxes were a profound influence on the development of the sophistic method of antilogic". Plutarch (*Pericles* 4.5), as Palmer quotes him: "“Pericles heard Zeno of Elea discoursing on nature in the manner of Parmenides, and practicing a kind of skill in cross-examination and in driving one’s opponent into a corner by means of contradictory argument” (Plu. Per. 4.5)."
- **Diogenes the Cynic** (later). Simplicius reports him answering by "silently standing and walking" (Huggett §3.1, citing Simplicius 1012.22).
- **[Bertrand Russell](russell.md)** (1914) and the received view; **[William James](william-james.md)** (1911), Bergson and Whitehead on temporal becoming — in the table below.
- **Lewis Carroll** (1895): his dialogue opens on Zeno's race-course (`raw/carroll-1895-what-the-tortoise-said-to-achilles.md`); see [What the Tortoise Said to Achilles](../problems/tortoise-and-achilles.md).

### Ancient and modern replies, side by side

The full list, with every owner, is on [Zeno's paradoxes](../problems/zenos-paradoxes.md). Nothing here is ranked.

| Target | Ancient reply (owner, locator) | Modern reply (owner, locator) | Criticism on record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dichotomy, Achilles | Aristotle: divide the time as well; "Hence Zeno's argument makes a false assumption in asserting that it is impossible for a thing to pass over or severally to come in contact with infinite things in a finite time." (*Phys.* VI.2, 233a21–23) | Russell (1914, Lecture VI): "If half the course takes half a minute, and the next quarter takes a quarter of a minute, and so on, the whole course will take a minute." | Aristotle himself later: "adequate as a reply to the questioner" but "as an account of the fact and explanation of its true nature it is inadequate" (VIII.8, 263a13–18) |
| Dichotomy | Aristotle: "If the units are actual, it is not possible: if they are potential, it is possible." (VIII.8, 263b3–6) | Huggett (§3.2) on Achilles' points: "we shall take it that the series is mathematically legitimate" (Cantor's ordinals) | Huggett (§3.1) on Aristotle: it "assumes that a clear distinction can be drawn between potential and actual infinities, something that was never fully achieved" |
| Arrow | Aristotle: "time is not composed of indivisible moments any more than any other magnitude is composed of indivisibles." (VI.9, 239b8–9) | At-at theory; Russell: "there is no next position and no next moment" (1914, Lecture VI) | Huggett (§3.3): "it carries the counter-intuitive implication that motion is not something that happens at any instant, but rather only over finite periods of time" |
| Stadium | Aristotle: the fallacy lies in assuming "a body occupies an equal time in passing with equal velocity a body that is in motion and a body of equal size that is at rest" (240a1–4) | Davey (2007), Sattler (2015) reconstructions (Huggett §3.4) | Huggett (§3.4): "The general verdict is that Zeno was hopelessly confused about relative velocities in this paradox." |
| Plurality (size from sizeless points) | Aristotle's divisibility argument, which Simplicius attributes to Zeno (Huggett §2.3) | Grünbaum (1967), measure theory: "a geometric line segment is an uncountable infinity of points plus a distance function" (Huggett §2.2) | Huggett (§2.3) reports "Sherry’s (1988) concern that refusing to extend the definition would be ad hoc" ([doi:10.1086/289416](https://doi.org/10.1086/289416)), addressed by Ehrlich (2014, [doi:10.1086/677978](https://doi.org/10.1086/677978)) |

Huggett's own measure-theoretic statement (§2.3): "In a strict sense in modern measure theory (which generalizes Grünbaum’s framework), the points in a line are incommensurable with it, and the very set-up given by Aristotle in which the length of the whole is analyzed in terms of its points is illegitimate." Against the received view's sufficiency, Bergson (1911, ch. IV, n. 99): "we do not consider the sophism of Zeno refuted by the fact that the geometrical progression" of the distances "has a finite sum" (`raw/bergson-1911-creative-evolution-arrow-and-achilles.md`); Huggett (§5) replies that "the tools of standard modern mathematics are up to the job of resolving the paradoxes, so no such conclusion seems warranted". Huggett also allows (§1.2): "arguably Aristotle and other ancients had some replies that would—or should—have satisfied Zeno."

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Did Zeno defend Parmenides? Yes (the conventional reading).** Huggett (preamble): "he wrote a book of paradoxes defending Parmenides’ philosophy". Russell (1914, Lecture VI): "Zeno is anxious to prove that motion is really impossible, and that he desires to prove this because he follows Parmenides in denying plurality". Palmer (preamble) records that this "is typically said".
- **No, or not straightforwardly (Palmer, Barnes).** Palmer (preamble): "While it is typically said that he aimed to defend the paradoxical monism of his Eleatic mentor, Parmenides, the Platonic evidence on which this view has resided ultimately fails to support it." (§3): "The evidence of Plato’s Parmenides, then, does not license the conventional view that Zeno’s arguments against plurality and motion were intended to support the strict monism of Parmenides." He quotes Barnes (1982, 236): "Many men had mocked Parmenides: Zeno mocked the mockers." Palmer adds that whether the historical Zeno was in that context "must remain uncertain".
- **Whose view was the target?** Huggett (§1.1): "some say that the target was a technical doctrine of the Pythagoreans, but most today see Zeno as opposing common-sense notions of plurality and motion." He reports Tannery (1885) for the first, Vlastos (1967) for the second, and Matson (2001) as a later defender of Tannery (§5). Palmer (preamble) proposes the paradoxes "may have originated in reflection upon Pythagorean efforts to apply mathematical notions to the natural world."
- **Sophist, dialectician, eristic?** Palmer (§3) reports Isocrates, who would "lump Gorgias, Zeno, and Melissus together as among the other “sophists” flourishing in the era of Protagoras"; and Aristotle's view as Palmer sums it: "For Aristotle, then, Zeno was a controversialist and paradox-monger, whose arguments were nevertheless both sophisticated enough to qualify him as the inventor of dialectic and were important for forcing clarification of concepts fundamental to natural science."
- **Seriousness.** Russell (1914, Lecture VI): "They are not, however, on any view, mere foolish quibbles: they are serious arguments, raising difficulties which it has taken two thousand years to answer". Aristotle on their "disquietude" (VI.9), above.
- **Method.** Palmer (§3) credits Zeno with forms of argument, "most notably, reductio ad absurdum by means of antinomical and/or regress arguments—that have ever since been fundamental to philosophical probing of conceptual adequacy." Palmer (§3) also writes: "Zeno’s arguments also had a formative influence on Aristotle’s own theory of the continuum and of continuous motion."

## Vocabulary

- *Paradox*: Palmer (§1) glosses Zeno's arguments as "quite literally “para-doxes”—from the Greek para (“contrary to” or “against”) and doxa (“belief” or “opinion”)—arguments for conclusions contrary to what people ordinarily believe"; Huggett (§1.1) defines a paradox as "a demonstration that a contradiction or absurd consequence follows from apparently reasonable assumptions". The implant's entry: [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md).
- *Dialectic*: in Huggett (§1.1) the arguments are "‘reductio ad absurdum’ arguments (or ‘dialectic’ in the sense of the period)" — not Hegel's or Plato's later sense; see [persuasion, rhetoric, dialectic](../vocabulary/persuasion-rhetoric-dialectic.md).
- *Limited / unlimited* (peras / apeiron): in fr. 3 DK, "limited" means finitely many; no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Many* / *one*: "If there are many things" is the hypothesis each plurality argument reduces (Palmer §2.1); not the implant's set-theoretic sense.

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates:** "Immanuel Kant was born April 22, 1724 in Königsberg, near the southeastern shore of the Baltic Sea." "Kant died February 12, 1804, just short of his eightieth birthday." (Rohlf §1).
- **Schooling:** "Kant’s parents were Pietist and he attended a Pietist school, the Collegium Fridericianum, from ages eight through fifteen." (§1).
- **Teacher:** "Kant’s favorite teacher was Martin Knutzen (1713–1751), a Pietist who was heavily influenced by both Wolff and the English philosopher John Locke (1632–1704)." (§1).
- **Posts:** "He finally returned to Königsberg in 1754 and began teaching at the Albertina the following year." "In 1770, at the age of forty-six, Kant was appointed to the chair in logic and metaphysics at the Albertina, after teaching for fifteen years as an unsalaried lecturer and working since 1766 as a sublibrarian to supplement his income." "Kant retired from teaching in 1796." (§1).
- **Influences Rohlf names for the early ethics:** "These ideas often stemmed from British sentimentalist philosophers such as David Hume (1711–1776) and Francis Hutcheson (1694–1747), some of whose texts were translated into German in the mid-1750s; and from the Swiss philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778), who published a flurry of works in the early 1760s." (§1).
- **Censure:** *Religion Within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793) "drew a censure from the Prussian King when Kant published the book after its second essay was rejected by the censor;" (§1).
- **Fichte:** Fichte's first book was "published anonymously and initially mistaken for a work by Kant himself."; Fichte later developed "an original position quite at odds with Kant’s, which Kant finally repudiated publicly in 1799 (12:370–371)." (§1).

## Problems addressed

- **The supreme principle of morality.** Johnson & Cureton (preamble): "Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) argued that the supreme principle of morality is a principle of practical rationality that he dubbed the “Categorical Imperative” (CI)." It is contrasted with the hypothetical imperative (see Vocabulary). The formulas, in Abbott's translation unless marked:
  - *Universal Law* (4:421): "Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law." Earlier, in the First Section (4:402): "I am never to act otherwise than so that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law."
  - *Law of Nature* (4:421): "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature."
  - *Humanity* (4:429): "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." Johnson & Cureton (§6): "First, the Humanity Formula does not rule out using people as means to our ends."
  - *Autonomy* (Johnson & Cureton §7): "The third formulation of the CI is “the Idea of the will of every rational being as a will that legislates universal law.” (G 4:432)."
  - *Kingdom of Ends* (§8): "The formulation of the CI states that we must “act in accordance with the maxims of a member giving universal laws for a merely possible kingdom of ends” (G 4:439)."
  - *Their unity* (§9): "Kant claimed that all of these CI formulas were equivalent. Unfortunately, he does not say in what sense." Kant's own words, as the authors quote them in Gregor's translation: "What he says is that these “are basically only so many formulations of precisely the same law, each one of them by itself uniting the other two within it,” and that the differences between them are “more subjectively than objectively practical” in the sense that each aims “to bring an Idea of reason closer to intuition (by means of a certain analogy) and thus nearer to feeling” (G 4:435)."
- **The lying promise** (4:422), Kant's example under the Universal Law formula: "When I think myself in want of money, I will borrow money and promise to repay it, although I know that I never can do so." Universalised, "the promise itself would become impossible, as well as the end that one might have in view in it, since no one would consider that anything was promised to him, but would ridicule all such statements as vain pretences." Johnson & Cureton's reconstruction (§5): "The maxim of lying whenever it gets you what you want generates a contradiction once you try to combine it with the universalized version that all rational agents must, by a law of nature, lie when doing so gets them what they want." They add: "Again, Kant’s interpreters differ over exactly how to reconstruct the derivation of these duties."
- **The good will.** Johnson & Cureton (§2): "Kant’s analysis of commonsense ideas begins with the thought that the only thing good without qualification is a “good will”." Kant (*Groundwork* I, 4:394): "A good will is good not because of what it performs or effects, not by its aptness for the attainment of some proposed end, but simply by virtue of the volition; that is, it is good in itself" — even without success, "like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself." On acting from duty: "Kant’s views in this regard have understandably been the subject of much controversy." (§2).
- **Autonomy.** Rohlf (preamble): "The fundamental idea of Kant’s “critical philosophy” – especially in his three Critiques: the Critique of Pure Reason (1781, 1787), the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), and the Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) – is human autonomy." Johnson & Cureton (§10): "At the heart of Kant’s moral theory is the idea of autonomy." and "As with Rousseau, whose views influenced Kant, freedom does not consist in being bound by no law, but by laws that are in some sense of one’s own making."
- **God and immortality as postulates** (reported as Kant's argument, not as a finding). Rohlf (§6.2): "Kant argues that we can comply with our duty to promote the highest good only if we believe in the immortality of the soul and the existence of God."
- **[Lying to the murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md)** ("On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives", 1797, Ak. 8:425 ff.; Abbott tr. 1898, pp. 361–365, excerpt `raw/kant-1797-supposed-right-to-lie-constant-abbott.md`). Constant, as Kant quotes him: "To tell the truth then is a duty, but only towards him who has a right to the truth. But no man has a right to a truth that injures others." Kant, on being named: "I hereby admit that I have really said this in some place which I cannot now recollect.—I. Kant." His reply: "To be truthful (honest) in all declarations is therefore a sacred unconditional command of reason, and not to be limited by any expediency." (p. 363). He limits the essay's scope: "For this principle belongs to Ethics, and here we are speaking only of a duty of justice." (p. 362, n. 1). The readings — Korsgaard (1986), Varden (2010), Mahon's grouping — are set out on the problem page and summarised under Reception below.
- **[The justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md)** (*Metaphysics of Morals*, Doctrine of Right, 6:331–333; Hastie tr. 1887, excerpt `raw/kant-1797-metaphysics-of-morals-6-331-333-punishment-island-hastie.md`): "Juridical Punishment can never be administered merely as a means for promoting another Good either with regard to the Criminal himself or to Civil Society, but must in all cases be imposed only because the individual on whom it is inflicted has committed a Crime." (6:331); "The Penal Law is a Categorical Imperative" (6:331); the island case is quoted on the problem page.
- **[Moral luck](../problems/moral-luck.md).** The good-will passage above is the Kantian source Nelkin (SEP "Moral Luck" §1) and Nagel (1979) cite; Nelkin (§4.2, `raw/sep-moral-luck-fall-2024-nelkin-map.md`): "All of those who accept the existence of some type of moral luck reject the Control Principle and the Kantian conception of morality that embraces it."
- **[The moral status of animals](../problems/moral-status-of-animals.md).** Kant, as Gruen & Monsó (SEP) quote the *Lectures on Ethics* (Ak 27:459; `raw/sep-moral-animal-fall-2024-moral-status.md`): "If a man shoots his dog because the animal is no longer capable of service, he does not fail in his duty to the dog, for the dog cannot judge, but his act is inhuman and damages in himself that humanity which it is his duty to show towards mankind."
- **[Dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md).** *Perpetual Peace* (1795), Appendix I (Campbell Smith tr. 1903, `raw/kant-1795-perpetual-peace-appendix-1-morals-and-politics-smith.md`): "Hence there can be no quarrel between politics, as the practical science of right, and morals, which is also a science of right, but theoretical." Coady (SEP "Dirty Hands" §3, `raw/sep-dirty-hands-fall-2024-coady-map.md`): "Absolutist positions have been explicitly held by many philosophers, notably Augustine, Aquinas, and Kant, and implicitly perhaps by many more."

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing.** Rohlf (preamble): "Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) is the central figure in modern philosophy." Nagel, as Wilson and Denis quote him, says that moral philosophy "continues to be dominated by the disagreement between these two giants" (Kant and Hume; Nagel 2012).
- **Hegel's emptiness charge**, as Brooks reports it: "Perhaps no area of the PR has attracted more controversy than Hegel’s so-called “emptiness charge” against Kant: Kant is accused of offering no more than an empty formula of “duty for duty’s sake” where our task is to avoid contradicting a set formula (PR §135, see Hoy 1989, Freyenhagen 2012, Stern 2012)." Brooks grants: "Undoubtedly, Kant does offer Hegel some ammunition for this criticism." Brooks's own assessment, citing Wood: "But, of course, as Kant’s defenders are correct to point out, Hegel’s criticism is unfair and misses its mark (Wood 1989)." and "In fact, Kant purposefully offers multiple versions of his formula to help draw our considerations of morality away from a purely rationalist exercise and bring it “nearer to feeling” (note 15)." Wood, "The Emptiness of the Moral Will", *Monist* 72 (1989): 454–483, DOI [10.5840/monist198972318](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist198972318); Freyenhagen, "The Empty Formalism Objection Revisited" (2012), DOI [10.1002/9781444354256.ch2](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444354256.ch2) (neither read here).
- **Schiller on duty and inclination**, per Moland's statement of his view: "Once we understand the underlying unity that grace suggests, however, we can correct the excesses brought about by Kant’s rigorous separation of reason and inclination." Moland reports the essay's later use: "“On Grace and Dignity” has thus been much discussed in Kant scholarship both as a possible contribution to Kantian theories of moral psychology and as supporting the possibility of a Kant-based ethics of character (Baxley 2010; Deligiorgi 2011; Winegar 2013)." A related objection, in Johnson & Cureton (§2): "Many object that we do not think better of actions done for the sake of duty than actions performed out of emotional concern or sympathy for others, especially those things we do for friends and family."
- **Mill and the teleological readings.** Johnson & Cureton (§14): "Perhaps the first philosopher to suggest a teleological reading of Kant was John Stuart Mill." "In the first chapter of his Utilitarianism, Mill implies that the Universal Law formulation of the Categorical Imperative could only sensibly be interpreted as a test of the consequences of universal adoption of a maxim." "Several 20th century theorists have followed Mill’s suggestion, most notably, R. M. Hare." For Hare, "Thus, in his view, the CI is “simply utilitarianism put into other words” (1993, p. 103)." Against: "But a powerful argument for the deontological reading is Kant’s own apparent insistence that the authority of moral demands must come simply from their being the demands of a rational will, quite apart from the value that will may have (see Schneewind 1996; Johnson 2007, 2008; and Reath 1994)."
- **Constructivist and realist readings, side by side** (Johnson & Cureton §15: "A second issue that has received considerable attention is whether Kant is a metaethical constructivist or realist.").
  - *Constructivist:* "Constructivism in metaethics is the view that moral truths are, or are determined by, the outcomes of actual or hypothetical procedures of deliberation or choice." "Many who interpret Kant as a constructivist claim that his analysis of “duty” and “good will” reveals that if there are moral requirements then the agents who are bound to them have autonomy of the will (Rawls 1980; Korsgaard 1996; O’Neil 1989; Reath 2006; Hill 1989a, 1989b, 2001; Cureton 2013, 2014; Engstrom 2009)." "Kantians in this camp, however, disagree about how this rational procedure should be characterized."
  - *Realist:* "Other commentators interpret Kant as a robust moral realist (Ameriks 2003; Wood 1999; Langton 2007; Kain 2004)." "According to these philosophers, Kant’s theory, properly presented, begins with the claim that rational nature is an objective, agent-neutral and intrinsic value." And on value: "And Wood argues that humanity itself is the grounding value for Kant." (§14).
  - *Quietist:* "Some interpreters of Kant, most notably Korsgaard (1996), seem to affirm a kind of quietism about metaethics by rejecting many of the assumptions that contemporary metaethical debates rest on." Reply: "Others have raised doubts, however, about whether Kantians can so easily avoid engaging in metaethical debates (Hussain & Shaw 2013)."
  - Shared ground on the Humanity formula (§6): "One such strategy, favored by Korsgaard (1996) and Wood (1999) relies on the apparent argument Kant gives that humanity is an end in itself." Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity* (1996), DOI [10.1017/CBO9780511554476](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511554476); Wood, *Kant's Ethical Thought* (1999), DOI [10.1017/CBO9781139173254](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139173254) (both cited as the SEP cites them; not read here).
- **On the lie to the murderer.** Korsgaard (1986, p. 327; `raw/korsgaard-1986-right-to-lie-kant-dealing-with-evil.md`): "Kant's readers differ about whether Kant's moral philosophy commits him to the claims he makes in these passages." She reports both camps: "Unsympathetic readers are inclined to take them as evidence of the horrifying conclusions to which Kant was led by his notion that the necessity in duty is rational necessity" and "Sympathetic readers are likely to argue that Kant here mistook the implications of his own theory, and to try to show that, by careful construction and accurate testing of the maxim on which this liar acts, Kant's conclusions can be blocked by his own procedures." Her own result: "It is permissible to lie to deceivers in order to counteract the intended results of their deceptions, for the maxim of lying to a deceiver is universalizable." (p. 330), and "This result impugns Kant's belief that the formulas are equivalent." (p. 337). Varden (2010, p. 403; `raw/varden-2010-kant-lying-murderer-door-legal-philosophy.md`): "In this paper, I argue that Kant’s discussion of lying to the murderer at the door has been seriously misinterpreted." and (p. 406) "Kant never discusses first-personal ethics (universalizable maxims and actions from duty) in this paper." Timmermann, *Kant and the Supposed Right to Lie* (2025), DOI [10.1017/9781108992435](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108992435), is reported on the problem page from its abstract.

## Vocabulary

- *Categorical* and *hypothetical imperative*: Johnson & Cureton (§1): "Kant characterized the CI as an objective, rationally necessary and unconditional principle that we must follow despite any natural desires we may have to the contrary." The hypothetical imperative is "a command in a conditional form" (§4). No vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Maxim*: what the Universal Law formula tests (4:402, 4:421). Johnson & Cureton (§5): "The form of a maxim is “I will A in C in order to realize or produce E” where “A” is some act type, “C” is some type of circumstance, and “E” is some type of end to be realized or achieved by A in C."
- *Autonomy*: for Kant, being bound by "laws that are in some sense of one’s own making" (Johnson & Cureton §10).
- *Lie* in the 1797 essay: Kant calls the untruthful statement a lie "though not in the jurist's sense" (Abbott p. 362); Mahon's survey of definitions is on the [problem page](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md).
- *Right* and *duty of justice*: the 1797 essay speaks "only of a duty of justice" (p. 362, n. 1), the domain of the Doctrine of Right, as distinct from ethics; Varden (2010) builds her reading on this.

Related concepts: [liberty, positive and negative](../vocabulary/liberty-positive-and-negative.md), [categorical imperative](../positions/categorical-imperative.md), [autonomy](../vocabulary/autonomy.md).

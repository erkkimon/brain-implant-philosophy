## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates:** "David Hume (1711–1776)" (Morris & Brown, preamble).
- **Birth and childhood:** "Born in Edinburgh, Hume spent his childhood at Ninewells, his family’s modest estate in the border lowlands." (§1).
- **The Treatise:** "In 1734, when he was only 23, he began writing A Treatise of Human Nature." "Book I, “Of the Understanding”, and Book II, “Of the Passions”, appeared anonymously in 1739. The next year saw the publication of Book III, “Of Morals”, as well as his anonymous “Abstract” of Books I and II." (§1).
- **No academic post:** "When he applied for the Chair of Ethics and Pneumatical (“Mental”) Philosophy at Edinburgh in 1745, his reputation provoked vocal and ultimately successful opposition. Six years later, he stood for the Chair of Logic at Glasgow, only to be turned down again. Hume never held an academic post." (§1).
- **The History:** "Published in six volumes between 1754 and 1762, his History was a bestseller well into the next century, giving him the financial independence he had long sought." (§1).
- **Death and the Dialogues:** "In 1775, Hume was diagnosed with intestinal cancer. Before his death in 1776, he arranged for the posthumous publication of his most controversial work, the Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, and composed a brief autobiography, “My Own Life”." (§1; "most controversial" is the authors' description).
- **Predecessor and opponents in ethics:** "Hume follows his sentimentalist predecessor, Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746), in building his moral theory around the idea of a spectator who approves or disapproves of people’s character traits and motives." "Hume has two sets of opponents: the self-love theorists and the moral rationalists." (§7).
- **Friend:** Morris & Brown (preamble) call Adam Smith "his close friend".

## Problems addressed

- **The problem of induction** (T 1.3.6; E 4–5). No problem page yet; the [new riddle of induction](../problems/new-riddle-of-induction.md) page sets out the problem Goodman built on it. Henderson (preamble): "The original source of what has become known as the “problem of induction” is in Book 1, part iii, section 6 of A Treatise of Human Nature by David Hume, published in 1739 (Hume 1739)." and "In 1748, Hume gave a shorter version of the argument in Section iv of An enquiry concerning human understanding (Hume 1748)."
  Hume: "If reason determin'd us, it wou'd proceed upon that principle, that instances, of which we have had no experience, must resemble those, of which we have had experience, and that the course of nature continues always uniformly the same." (T 1.3.6.4, SBN 88–9).
  "To say it is experimental, is begging the question. For all inferences from experience suppose, as their foundation, that the future will resemble the past, and that similar powers will be conjoined with similar sensible qualities." "It is impossible, therefore, that any arguments from experience can prove this resemblance of the past to the future; since all these arguments are founded on the supposition of that resemblance." (E 4.21, SBN 37–8).
  Morris & Brown's reconstruction (§5.1): "Any attempt to infer (2) from (1) by a probable inference will be viciously circular—it will involve supposing what we are trying to prove."
  The constructive part: "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone, which renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past." (E 5.6, SBN 44–5). "Hume calls his constructive account of causal inference a “sceptical solution” to the “sceptical doubts” he raised in the critical phase of his argument." (Morris & Brown §5.2).
- **Reason and the passions** (T 2.3.3). "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them." (T 2.3.3.4, SBN 414–5); "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger." (T 2.3.3.6, SBN 416).
  Readings, side by side (Cohon §3): "Interpreters disagree as to whether Hume is an instrumentalist or a skeptic about practical reason."
  - *Instrumentalist:* "Instrumentalists understand the claim that reason is the slave of the passions to allow that reason not only discovers the causally efficacious means to our ends (a task of theoretical causal reasoning) but also requires us to take them."
  - *Sceptical:* "Skeptical interpreters read Hume, instead, as denying that reason imposes any requirements on action, even the requirement to take the known, available means to one’s end."
  - *Common ground, per Cohon:* "Either way, Hume denies that reason can evaluate the ends people set themselves; only passions can select ends, and reason cannot evaluate passions."
- **Is and ought** (T 3.1.1.27, SBN 469–70; the last paragraph of "Moral distinctions not deriv'd from reason"). Hume: "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not."
  "For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, 'tis necessary that it shou'd be observ'd and explain'd; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it." (the full paragraph is in the raw file).
  Cohon (§5): "Few passages in Hume’s work have generated more interpretive controversy." The readings she reports:
  - *"Hume's Law" (the reading she calls dominant in the twentieth century):* "According to the dominant twentieth-century interpretation, Hume says here that no ought-judgment may be correctly inferred from a set of premises expressed only in terms of ‘is,’ and the vulgar systems of morality commit this logical fallacy." "A number of present-day philosophers, including R. M. Hare, endorse this putative thesis of logic, calling it “Hume’s Law.”" Against it, as Cohon reports: "(As Francis Snare observes, on this reading Hume must simply assume that no purely factual propositions are themselves evaluative, as he does not argue for this.)"
  - *Noncognitivist:* "Some interpreters think Hume commits himself here to a non-propositional or noncognitivist view of moral judgment — the view that moral judgments do not state facts and are not truth-evaluable."
  - *Cognitivist:* "Other interpreters — the more cognitivist ones — see the paragraph about ‘is’ and ‘ought’ as doing none of the above." "Some read it as simply providing further support for Hume’s extensive argument that moral properties are not discernible by demonstrative reason, leaving open whether ethical evaluations may be conclusions of cogent probable arguments." "They point out that Hume himself makes such inferences frequently in his writings."
- **The source of moral distinctions: sentimentalism** (T 3.1.2; M App 1). "Morality, therefore, is more properly felt than judg'd of;" (T 3.1.2.1, SBN 470). "The hypothesis which we embrace is plain. It maintains that morality is determined by sentiment. It defines virtue to be whatever mental action or quality gives to a spectator the pleasing sentiment of approbation; and vice the contrary." (M App 1.10, SBN 289).
  Cohon's four theses (preamble): "(1) Reason alone cannot be a motive to the will, but rather is the “slave of the passions”" "(2) Moral distinctions are not derived from reason" "(3) Moral distinctions are derived from the moral sentiments: feelings of approval (esteem, praise) and disapproval (blame) felt by spectators who contemplate a character trait or action" "(4) While some virtues and vices are natural (see Section 13), others, including justice, are artificial (see Section 9)." She adds: "There is heated debate about what Hume intends by each of these theses and how he argues for them."
  What a moral evaluation is, per Cohon (§6): "Four main interpretations have significant textual support."
  - *Nonpropositional:* "either it gives vent to a feeling, or it is itself a feeling (Flew, Blackburn, Snare, Bricke)."
  - *Subjective description:* "The subjective description view, by contrast, says that for Hume moral evaluations describe the feelings of the spectator, or the feelings a spectator would have were she to contemplate the trait or action from the common point of view."
  - *Dispositional:* "Often grouped with the latter view is the third, dispositional interpretation, which understands moral evaluations as factual judgments to the effect that the evaluated trait or action is so constituted as to cause feelings of approval or disapproval in a (suitably characterized) spectator (Mackie, in one of his proposals)."
  - *Two states:* "One version says that the moral judgments, as distinct from the moral feelings, are factual judgments about the moral sentiments (Capaldi)." "A distinct version, the moral sensing view, treats the moral beliefs as ideas copied from the impressions of approval or disapproval that represent a trait of character or an action as having whatever quality it is that one experiences in feeling the moral sentiment (Cohon)." (Cohon's own reading.)
- **Justice and the artificial virtues** (T 3.2). Morris & Brown (§7.4): "The artificial virtues—respecting people’s property rights, fidelity in keeping promises and contracts, and allegiance to government—are dispositions based on social practices and institutions that arise from conventions."
- **[The prisoner's dilemma](../problems/prisoners-dilemma.md)** (T 3.2.5). The two farmers: "Your corn is ripe to-day; mine will be so tomorrow." ending "both of us lose our harvests for want of mutual confidence and security." (`raw/hume-1739-40-treatise-3-2-5-farmers-dilemma.md`); Kuhn (SEP §9) reports Skyrms (1998) and Vanderschraaf reading it as an asynchronous prisoner's dilemma.
- **[Dirty hands](../problems/dirty-hands.md)** (T 3.2.11). Hume reports the maxim "that there is a system of morals calculated for princes, much more free than that which ought to govern private persons." and reads it so: "though the morality of princes has the same extent, yet it has not the same force as that of private persons, and may lawfully be trangressed from a more trivial motive." (`raw/hume-1739-treatise-3-2-11-morals-calculated-for-princes.md`).
- **[The Epicurean trilemma](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md)** (D X–XI, 1779). The words are Philo's: "EPICURUS's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?" (D X). Philo then concedes: "A mere possible compatibility is not sufficient." (D X), and of four hypotheses about the first causes: "The fourth, therefore, seems by far the most probable." (D XI). Tooley (SEP "The Problem of Evil" §3.3) reads Part XI thus: "Hume advanced, then, an evidential argument from evil that has a distinctly different logical form from that involved in direct inductive arguments" (`raw/sep-evil-fall-2024-logical-evidential-and-responses.md`). The problem page sets out the responses and the dispute over the attribution to Epicurus.
- **Psychological egoism and hedonism.** Cohon (§1): "Hume roundly criticizes Hobbes for his insistence on psychological egoism or something close to it, and for his dismal, violent picture of a state of nature." Andrew Moore (SEP "Hedonism" §1.2): "By appeal to such cases Butler and Hume arguably refuted the strong motivational hedonist thesis that one's every desire has one's own pleasure and that alone as its object." (`raw/sep-hedonism-fall-2024-paradox-of-hedonism-and-butler.md`; Moore's assessment).

- *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion* (1779, posthumous); speakers Cleanthes, Demea and Philo. Morris & Brown (§8.1): "Although all three characters say very Humean things at one time or another, Philo’s views are consistently the closest to Hume’s." (their reading). - *My Own Life*, a brief autobiography composed before his death in 1776 (§1; cited as "MOL").
- **Treatise and Enquiries, read side by side.** In 1775 Hume sent his publisher an "Advertisement": "In it, he complains that his critics focused “all their batteries” on the Treatise, “that juvenile work”, which he published anonymously and never acknowledged. He urges his readers to regard the Enquiries “as containing his philosophical sentiments and principles”, assuring his publisher that they provide “a compleat answer” to his critics." (Morris & Brown §2). They state the question: "Hume’s apparent disavowal of the Treatise and his regard for the Enquiries raise a question about how we should read his work." and their own view: "It is not unreasonable to conclude that Hume’s recasting of the Treatise was designed to address this issue, which suggests that we might understand him best by reading both works, despite their differences, together." (§2).

## In dialogue with

- **Francis Hutcheson** (earlier). "One way of understanding Hume’s project is to see it as an attempt to naturalize Hutcheson’s moral sense theory." (Morris & Brown §7.1; their reading).
- **Samuel Clarke** (earlier). "Clarke, Hume’s central rationalist opponent, appeals to reason to explain almost every aspect of morality." (§7.1).
- **Thomas Hobbes** (earlier). Hume's criticism of psychological egoism (Cohon §1, quoted above).
- **[Epicurus](epicurus.md)** (earlier). Philo's "old questions" (D X); Russell (SEP "Hume on Religion" §5) cites the passage as "D, 10.25/198". Wikipedia's claim that Hume took the passage from Bayle is recorded, with its sourcing, on the [problem page](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md).
- **Adam Smith** (contemporary). Morris & Brown (preamble): "his influence is evident in the moral philosophy and economic writings of his close friend Adam Smith."
- **[Immanuel Kant](kant.md)** (later). "Kant reported that Hume’s work woke him from his “dogmatic slumbers” (Prolegomena, Introduction)" (Morris & Brown, preamble). On induction, Henderson (§3.1): "Kant famously argued in response to Hume that such synthetic a priori knowledge is possible (Kant 1781, 1783)." Rohlf (SEP "Immanuel Kant" §1) names Hume among the "British sentimentalist philosophers" from whom ideas reached Kant's early ethics: "These ideas often stemmed from British sentimentalist philosophers such as David Hume (1711–1776) and Francis Hutcheson (1694–1747), some of whose texts were translated into German in the mid-1750s;" (`raw/sep-kant-fall-2024-life-works-autonomy.md`).
- **[Jeremy Bentham](bentham.md)** (later). "Jeremy Bentham remarked that reading Hume “caused the scales to fall” from his eyes (“A Fragment on Government”, chapter 1, paragraph 36, footnote 2)." (Morris & Brown, preamble).
- **[Friedrich Nietzsche](nietzsche.md)** (later, as a classification). Leiter (SEP "Nietzsche's Moral and Political Philosophy" §3.3): "Nietzsche is part of a tradition of moral anti-realists (and moral relativists) who are also sentimentalists, like Hume and, in the German tradition, Herder (see Forster 2017)" (`raw/sep-nietzsche-moral-political-fall-2024-leiter-critique-antirealism-politics.md`).
- **[William James](william-james.md)** (later). As the SEP "William James" entry reports him: "Spinoza’s unity of all things in one substance is “barren,” as is Hume’s “‘looseness and separateness’ of everything…” (WB 60)." (§2; `raw/sep-james-fall-2024-life-ethics-pragmatism-religion.md`).
- **R. M. Hare** (later). Endorses "Hume's Law" (Cohon §5, quoted above).
- **Hans Reichenbach, Karl Popper** (later, on induction). Henderson: "Reichenbach did think Hume’s argument unassailable, but nonetheless he attempted to provide a weaker kind of justification for induction." (§5.3); "Karl Popper, for instance, regarded the problem of induction as insurmountable, but he argued that science is not in fact based on inductive inferences at all (Popper 1935 [1959])." (§6).
- **Nelson Goodman** (later). "But Hume overlooks the fact that some regularities do and some do not establish such habits; that predictions based on some regularities are valid while predictions based on other regularities are not." (*Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, 1955, p. 81; see [new riddle of induction](../problems/new-riddle-of-induction.md)).
- **Saul Kripke** (later). Models his sceptical solution to the [rule-following paradox](../problems/rule-following-paradox.md) on Hume's: "The impossibility of private language emerges as a corollary of his sceptical solution of his own paradox, as does the impossibility of ‘private causation’ in Hume." (Kripke 1982, p. 68).
- **[G. E. M. Anscombe](anscombe.md)** (later). Wiland and Driver (SEP "Elizabeth Anscombe" §2) report that in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) "she brusquely and perhaps uncharitably attacks Butler, Hume, Kant, Bentham, and Mill" (their assessment; `raw/sep-anscombe-fall-2024-life-ethics-intention.md`).
- **Paul Draper** (later). Tooley (SEP "The Problem of Evil" §3.1) on the indirect evidential approach: "This approach, which was originally used by David Hume in one of his arguments in his Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, and which has been set out and defended in a detailed way by Paul Draper".

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing (Morris & Brown, preamble).** "Generally regarded as one of the most important philosophers to write in English, David Hume (1711–1776) was also well known in his own time as an historian and essayist."
  "Although Hume’s more conservative contemporaries denounced his writings as works of scepticism and atheism, his influence is evident in the moral philosophy and economic writings of his close friend Adam Smith."
  "Today, philosophers recognize Hume as a thoroughgoing exponent of philosophical naturalism, as a precursor of contemporary cognitive science, and as the inspiration for several of the most significant types of ethical theory developed in contemporary moral philosophy."
- **On his ethics (Morris & Brown §7).** "He became the most famous proponent of sentimentalism." On justice (§7.4): "Hume rightly showcases his pioneering account of justice." Cohon (§1): "Hume explicitly favors an ethic of character along “ancient” lines."
- **On the induction argument (Henderson §6).** "Some philosophers have however seen his argument as unassailable, and have thus accepted that it does lead to inductive skepticism, the conclusion that inductive inferences cannot be justified." Others reply (Kant, Reichenbach, Popper, above); Engel reads the Tortoise of [Carroll's regress](../problems/tortoise-and-achilles.md) as a deductive counterpart: "In other words the Tortoise would be a radical sceptic about deduction, just as Hume is sceptic with respect to induction." (Engel 2005, p. 4).
- **On the Dialogues (Morris & Brown §8).** "Hume’s greatest achievement in the philosophy of religion is the Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, which is generally regarded as one of the most important and influential contributions to this area of philosophy."
  On Philo's concessions in Part XII: "Some take Philo—and, by implication, Hume—to be outing himself as a closet theist. Others conclude that, since he holds all the cards at this point, he can afford to be conciliatory." (§8.4).
- **Was Hume an atheist? (Russell, side by side).** "One of the most hotly debated issues arising out of Hume’s philosophy is whether or not he was an atheist." (§10).
  - *Soft sceptic:* "The view that has, perhaps, been most dominant during the past century has been that Hume was a skeptic and, as such, stands in a position that endorses neither theism nor atheism." (§10).
  - *Its critics:* "Against this (soft) skeptical reading critics have argued both that it exaggerates and that it underestimates Hume’s skeptical commitments." (§10).
  - *Russell's own label:* "The most accurate and informative label for describing Hume’s views on this subject is perhaps irreligion." (§11). He adds: "There are, however, some significant points of disagreement about the exact nature and extent of Hume’s irreligious intentions." (preamble).

## Vocabulary

- *Reason*: in T 2.3.3, on Cohon's account (§3), a faculty that cannot "evaluate the ends people set themselves"; narrower than the implant's [argument](../vocabulary/argument.md)-giving sense of reasoning.
- *Passion*: "A passion is an original existence, or, if you will, modification of existence, and contains not any representative quality," (T 2.3.3.5, SBN 415; read on [Hume Texts Online](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/2/3/3), excerpt in `raw/hume-1739-treatise-2-3-3-5-passion-original-existence.md`).
- *Custom*: the principle that "makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past." (E 5.6); the core of what Hume calls his "sceptical solution" (Morris & Brown §5.2).
- *Sentiment*: the "pleasing sentiment of approbation" a spectator feels (M App 1.10); *artificial virtue*: per Morris & Brown (§7.4), a disposition "based on social practices and institutions that arise from conventions" (justice, fidelity to promises, allegiance).
- *"Hume's Law"*: Hare's and others' name for the reading of T 3.1.1.27 as a thesis of logic (Cohon §5); the other readings deny that the paragraph states such a thesis.

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates.** Konstan: "The philosophy of Epicurus (341–270 B.C.E.) was a complete and interdependent system" (preamble); "The dates for Epicurus’ birth and first move to Athens are thus 341 B.C.E. and 323 respectively." (§2). Diogenes' own report, from Apollodorus' *Chronology*: "He was born, according to Apollodorus in his Chronology, in the third year of the 109th Olympiad, in the archonship of Sosigenes, on the seventh day of the month Gamelion, in the seventh year after the death of Plato." (X.14; Hicks's note: 341 B.C.). O'Keefe gives the death year less exactly: "Epicurus died from kidney stones around 271 or 270 B.C.E." (§1).
- **Origin.** "Epicurus, son of Neocles and Chaerestrate, was a citizen of Athens of the deme Gargettus, and, as Metrodorus says in his book On Noble Birth, of the family of the Philaidae." (DL X.1). O'Keefe: he "grew up in the Athenian colony of Samos, an island in the Mediterranean Sea." (§1).
- **Teacher.** "Here he studied philosophy under the tutelage of Nausiphanes, a Democritean philosopher with skeptical leanings" (Konstan §2, of Colophon).
- **School.** "When he was thirty-two he founded a school of philosophy, first in Mitylene and Lampsacus, and then five years later removed to Athens, where he died in the second year of the 127th Olympiad, in the archonship of Pytharatus, at the age of seventy-two; and Hermarchus the son of Agemortus, a Mitylenaean, took over the School." (DL X.15). Konstan: "In Athens, he purchased the property that became known as the “Garden” (later used as a name for his school itself) and began to develop his own school in earnest." (§2).
- **Death.** "Epicurus died of renal calculus after an illness which lasted a fortnight: so Hermarchus tells us in his letters." (DL X.15). His last letter, to Idomeneus: "On this blissful day, which is also the last of my life, I write this to you. My continual sufferings from strangury and dysentery are so great that nothing could augment them; but over against them all I set gladness of mind at the remembrance of our past conversations." (DL X.22).
- **What survives.** "The major source for Epicurean doctrine is Diogenes Laertius’ third-century C.E. Lives of Eminent Philosophers" (Konstan §1), which preserves three letters: "The Letter to Herodotus summarizes Epicurus’ physical theory, the Letter to Menoeceus offers a précis of Epicurean ethics, and the Letter to Pythocles treats astronomical and meteorological matters." (§1). On the *Principal Doctrines*: they are "excerpted from the writings of Epicurus or, in some cases, of his close associates" (§1). O'Keefe: "Epicurus was a voluminous writer, but almost none of his own work survives. A likely reason for this is that Christian authorities found his ideas ungodly." (§2) — O'Keefe's conjecture. Diogenes counts "about three hundred rolls" (X.26).
- **Lucretius as source.** Konstan: "As a dedicated Epicurean, passionate to promulgate the message of the founder, Lucretius reproduced Epicurean doctrine faithfully (Sedley 1998; Clay 1983 allows Lucretius more originality)." (§1) — two scholarly positions, reported side by side; and "His poem concentrates principally on the physical and psychological or epistemological aspects of Epicureanism, and to a great extent omits the ethical." (§1).

## Problems addressed

- **Is death bad for the one who dies?** (no problem page yet; Luper frames it as the "harm thesis", SEP "Death" §3.)
  - *The argument, in his words.* "Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an illimitable time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality." (LM, DL X.124). "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not. It is nothing, then, either to the living or to the dead, for with the living it is not and the dead exist no longer." (X.125). KD 2: "Death is nothing to us; for the body, when it has been resolved into its elements, has no feeling, and that which has no feeling is nothing to us."
  - *Reconstructions.* O'Keefe names it: "Epicurus’ main argument for why death is not bad is contained in the Letter to Menoeceus and can be dubbed the ‘no subject of harm’ argument. If death is bad, for whom is it bad? Not for the living, since they’re not dead, and not for the dead, since they don’t exist." (§5g.i). Luper ties it to "a version of hedonism according to which pleasure (or pleasant experiences) is the only thing that is intrinsically good for us" (§3.1) and judges: "Much about Epicurus’s argument is unclear, so let us work through it more carefully and see if we can fill in some details that he did not supply." (§3.1).
  - *Structural note (implant's own, [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)).* The X.125 sentence has the form of a constructive dilemma: (1) if one is alive, death is not present to one; (2) if one is dead, one does not exist; (3) what is not present, or happens to no existing subject, is "nothing to" that subject (the premise LM X.124 grounds in sentience); (4) one is alive or dead; so death is nothing to one. Writing A = alive, D = dead, N = death is nothing to one, and folding (1)–(3) into A → N and D → N, `logic.py check --premises 'A -> N' 'D -> N' 'A | D' --conclusion N` prints `VALID`. The form settles validity only; whether A → N and D → N hold depends on (3), the premise the deprivationist reply under *In dialogue with* (Nagel, as Luper reports him) denies.
  - *The symmetry argument (Lucretius).* O'Keefe: "A second Epicurean argument against the fear of death, the so-called ‘symmetry argument,’ is recorded by the Epicurean poet Lucretius. He says that anyone who fears death should consider the time before he was born." (§5g.ii). Lucretius in Leonard's translation: "Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld / Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth. / And Nature holds this like a mirror up / Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone." (*De Rerum Natura* III). Luper: "Lucretius, a follower of Epicurus, extended Epicurus’s case against the harm thesis." (§5.2). The two sources word its origin differently: O'Keefe calls it "Epicurean" and "recorded by" Lucretius; Luper assigns its development to Lucretius.
- **[The Epicurean trilemma and the logical problem of evil](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md)** — attributed author, attribution disputed.
  - *For the attribution.* Lactantius, *De Ira Dei* 13, introduces "that argument also of Epicurus" and states it with four cases (`raw/lactantius-de-ira-dei-13-epicurus-argument-fletcher.md`); Hume's Philo: "EPICURUS's old questions are yet unanswered." (*Dialogues* X, 1779; `raw/hume-1779-dialogues-part-10-epicurus-old-questions.md`). O'Keefe: "Epicurus is one of the earliest philosophers we know of to have raised the Problem of Evil, arguing against the notion that the world is under the providential care of a loving deity by pointing out the manifold suffering in the world." (IEP §3; `raw/epicurean-trilemma-attribution-sources-okeefe-russell-wikipedia.md`).
  - *Against it.* Wikipedia ("Epicurean paradox", rev. 1368515956): "There is no text by Epicurus confirming his authorship of the argument." (citing McBrayer 2013, no page), and it reports that Glei "believes that the theodicy argument is from a non-Epicurean or anti-Epicurean academic source" — Glei, "Et invidus et inbecillus", *Vigiliae Christianae* 42 (1988): 47–58, [doi:10.1163/157007288x00327](https://doi.org/10.1163/157007288x00327) (verified; not read). Details on the problem page.
  - *What the surviving texts do say about the gods.* KD 1: "A blessed and eternal being has no trouble himself and brings no trouble upon any other being; hence he is exempt from movements of anger and partiality, for every such movement implies weakness". LM: "Not the man who denies the gods worshipped by the multitude, but he who affirms of the gods what the multitude believes about them is truly impious." (X.123). Konstan: "the gods, for him, do not interest themselves in human affairs, since this would compromise their beatitude (see Obbink 1996: 321–23)." (§5).
- **[The paradox of hedonism](../problems/paradox-of-hedonism.md)** — Martínez & Labiano (2022, abstract): "We intend to show in this paper that some of the main elements in Epicurus' ethical position enable him to avoid the negative conclusion of this paradox." (`raw/crossref-paradox-of-hedonism-analysis-2005-2006-glb-2022.md`; full text not read). His own selection rule: "While therefore all pleasure because it is naturally akin to us is good, not all pleasure is choiceworthy, just as all pain is an evil and yet not all pain is to be shunned." (LM, X.129).
- **[Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md)** — preconceptions (*prolēpsis*), as O'Keefe reports: "Epicurus thinks that, in order to make judgments about the world, or even to start any inquiry whatsoever, we must already be in possession of certain basic concepts" and "This concern is similar to the Paradox of Inquiry explored by Plato in the Meno" (IEP §4a; `raw/iep-meno-rawson-and-epicurus-okeefe-paradox-of-inquiry.md`).
- **What is the end of life? (hedonism)** (no problem page yet).
  - "Wherefore we call pleasure the alpha and omega of a blessed life." (LM, X.128). "For the end of all our actions is to be free from pain and fear, and, when once we have attained all this, the tempest of the soul is laid" (X.128). "When we say, then, that pleasure is the end and aim, we do not mean the pleasures of the prodigal or the pleasures of sensuality, as we are understood to do by some through ignorance, prejudice, or wilful misrepresentation. By pleasure we mean the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul." (X.131). KD 3: "The magnitude of pleasure reaches its limit in the removal of all pain."
  - *Ataraxia and aponia; katastematic and kinetic pleasure.* Epicurus' *On Choice*, as Diogenes quotes it: "ἡ μὲν γὰρ ἀταραξία καὶ ἀπονία καταστηματικαί εἰσιν ἡδοναί· ἡ δὲ χαρὰ καὶ ἡ εὐφροσύνη κατὰ κίνησιν ἐνεργείᾳ βλέπονται." (X.136), in Hicks: "Peace of mind and freedom from pain are pleasures which imply a state of rest; joy and delight are seen to consist in motion and activity." Konstan: happiness is "a form of pleasure in its own right — what Epicurus called catastematic or (following Cicero’s Latin translation) “static” as opposed to “kinetic” pleasure." and "Although the precise nature of this distinction is debated, kinetic pleasures seem to be of the non-necessary kind (see below), such as those resulting from agreeable odors or sounds, rather than deriving from replenishment, as in the case of hunger or thirst." (§4). O'Keefe reads it differently: "‘Moving’ pleasures occur when one is in the process of satisfying a desire, e.g., eating a hamburger when one is hungry." (structural note: Konstan's sentence above sets kinetic pleasure apart from replenishment; O'Keefe's example is a replenishment) and reports: "Epicurus calls this a ‘static’ pleasure, and says that these static pleasures are the best pleasures." (§5b). The two encyclopedias' readings are given side by side.
  - *Desires.* "We must also reflect that of desires some are natural, others are groundless; and that of the natural some are necessary as well as natural, and some natural only." (LM, X.127).
  - *Virtue.* "Of all this the beginning and the greatest good is prudence." (X.132). KD 5: "It is impossible to live a pleasant life without living wisely and well and justly, and it is impossible to live wisely and well and justly without living pleasantly." Diogenes: "And we choose the virtues too on account of pleasure and not for their own sake, as we take medicine for the sake of health." (X.138).
- **What is justice?** (no problem page yet). KD 31: "Natural justice is a symbol or expression of expediency, to prevent one man from harming or being harmed by another." KD 33: "There never was an absolute justice, but only an agreement made in reciprocal intercourse in whatever localities now and again from time to time, providing against the infliction or suffering of harm." Konstan: "Justice, for Epicurus, depends on the capacity to make compacts neither to harm others nor be harmed by them, and consists precisely in these compacts; justice is nothing in itself, independent of such arrangements (KD 31–33)." (§5).
- **Freedom and necessity** (no problem page yet). "For he sees that necessity destroys responsibility and that chance or fortune is inconstant; whereas our own actions are free, and it is to them that praise and blame naturally attach." (LM, X.133). The atomic swerve, per Konstan: "evidence for this doctrine derives chiefly from later sources, including Lucretius and Cicero" (§4).

## In dialogue with

- **[Aristippus](aristippus.md) and the Cyrenaics** — on which pleasure is the end. Diogenes: "He differs from the Cyrenaics with regard to pleasure. They do not include under the term the pleasure which is a state of rest, but only that which consists in motion. Epicurus admits both; also pleasure of mind as well as of body" (X.136). The Cyrenaic side: "The removal of pain, however, which is put forward in Epicurus, seems to them not to be pleasure at all, any more than the absence of pleasure is pain." (DL II.89; `raw/diogenes-laertius-2-65-104-aristippus-cyrenaics-hicks.md`). Konstan: "The Cyrenaics and others, such as Cicero, maintained, in turn, that this condition is not pleasurable but rather neutral — neither pleasurable nor painful." (§4). Tsouna (SEP "The Cyrenaics", 2026, §4.2.1): Anniceris "defended the main tenets of his school against Epicurus" (`raw/sep-cyrenaics-fall-2026-aristippus-presentism-epicurus.md`).
- **The Stoics; [Epictetus](epictetus.md)** — rival school: "The Stoa competed with the school founded only a little before in Athens by Epicurus, and Stoic and Epicurean views are often compared and contrasted." (SEP "Stoicism" §1.1; `raw/sep-stoicism-fall-2024-school-and-doctrines.md`; see [Stoicism](../schools/stoicism.md)). O'Keefe: "In this, Epicurus goes against the majority of Greek ethical theorists, such as the Stoics, who identify happiness with virtue, and Aristotle, who identifies happiness with a life of virtuous activity." (§5d) — which also places him against **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)**.
- **[Plato](plato.md)** — on the paradox of inquiry, O'Keefe §4a (above); Konstan notes that "Plato had demonstrated the problematic nature of this kind of pleasure" (§4, citing *Gorgias* and *Philebus*) — Konstan's wording.
- **Lucretius** (1st c. BCE) — follower and source; his praise is under Reception.
- **Cicero** — "From a hostile point of view, Cicero rehearsed and criticized Epicurus’ ideas, especially concerning ethics, in several of his philosophical works, including On Moral Ends (De finibus) and the Tusculan Disputations." (Konstan §1).
- **Lactantius; David Hume** — transmit the problem-of-evil argument under his name (Problems addressed, above).
- **[Thomas Nagel](nagel.md)** — against the Epicurean case on death. Luper: "This view overlooks something that Thomas Nagel (1970) noted in his seminal essay “Death,” namely the fact that things may be extrinsically good or bad for us, other things being equal, by virtue of causing us not to have—by virtue of precluding our having—things that are intrinsically good or bad for us." (§3.1). On Lucretius: "In “Death” Thomas Nagel offered a response to Lucretius that has been widely discussed." (§5.2). Nagel, "Death", *Noûs* 4(1), 1970, from p. 73, [doi:10.2307/2214297](https://doi.org/10.2307/2214297) (verified; not read).

## Reception

Assessments, each with its owner, listed in parallel and unranked.

- **Lucretius (1st c. BCE), for.** "O thee I follow, glory of the Greeks" (*De Rerum Natura* III, proem); "Even Epicurus went, his light of life / Run out, the man in genius who o'er-topped / The human race, extinguishing all others, / As sun, in ether arisen, all the stars." (III). These are Lucretius' assessments, in Leonard's translation.
- **Ancient critics, against.** O'Keefe: "Epicurus’ hedonism was widely denounced in the ancient world as undermining traditional morality." (§5d). Cicero's verdict as Tsouna reports it: "Cicero’s verdict is that, even though both doctrines ought to be rejected because they insult the dignity of human beings, nonetheless Aristippus defended his views in a clearer and more consistent way than Epicurus (De fin. 1.23)." (SEP "The Cyrenaics" §5). LM X.131, quoted under *Problems addressed*, speaks of being misunderstood "by some through ignorance, prejudice, or wilful misrepresentation".
- **On the sources.** O'Keefe: "However, both Cicero and Plutarch were very hostile toward Epicureanism, so they must be used with care, since they often are less than charitable toward Epicurus, and may skew his views to serve their own purposes." (§2); "Although the major outlines of Epicurus’ thought are clear enough, the lack of sources means many of the details of his philosophy are still open to dispute." (§2).
- **Encyclopedia assessments.** Konstan calls the philosophy "a complete and interdependent system" (preamble). O'Keefe: "In fact, given Epicurus’ conception of pleasure, it might be less misleading to call him a ‘tranquillist’ instead of a ‘hedonist.’" (§5b); "Epicurus is one of the first philosophers to give a well-developed contractarian theory of justice." (§5e).
- **On the death arguments.** Luper on the symmetry argument read as concerning the ending of life: "So understood, the symmetry argument is weak." On the reading concerning being dead: "So interpreted, there is a kernel of truth in Lucretius’s argument." (§5.2). On Nagel's reply: "However, his response hinges on questionable assumptions about the essential features of people’s origins, as Nagel acknowledges (in footnote 3 of the reprint of “Death” in his collection Mortal Questions.)" (§5.2). All three are Luper's assessments.

## Vocabulary

- *Ataraxia* (ἀταραξία) — Hicks "peace of mind"; Konstan "ataraxy, lack of perturbation" (§4); O'Keefe "tranquility (ataraxia)" (§5b). No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Aponia* (ἀπονία) — Hicks "freedom from pain" (DL X.136). No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Katastematic* vs *kinetic* pleasure — "which imply a state of rest" vs "in motion" (Hicks, X.136); "static" is Cicero's Latin rendering per Konstan §4; O'Keefe's "static" and "moving" (§5b). No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Pleasure* (*hēdonē*) — in Epicurus' definition, "the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul" (LM X.131); Konstan: "not simply a neutral or privative condition but rather a form of pleasure in its own right" (§4); the Cyrenaics deny that this condition is pleasure (DL II.89).
- *Preconception* (*prolēpsis*) — see [Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md).
- *Dilemma* — the X.125 death argument and the attributed problem-of-evil argument are dilemmas in the sense of [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates (traditional):** "At different times in Chinese history, Confucius (trad. 551–479 BCE) has been portrayed as a teacher, advisor, editor, philosopher, reformer, and prophet." (Csikszentmihalyi, preamble). The birth year rests on one commentary: "A date of 551 BCE is given for his birth in the Gongyang Commentary (Gongyang zhuan 公羊傳) to the classic Spring and Autumn Annals (Chunqiu 春秋)" (§2). Wong gives the range only: "lived in the 6 th and 5 th century B.C.E" (§1).
- **Name:** "The name Confucius, a Latinized combination of the surname Kong 孔 with an honorific suffix “Master” (fuzi 夫子), has also come to be used as a global metonym for different aspects of traditional East Asian society." (Csikszentmihalyi, preamble). Wong: "Kongzi, best known in the West under his latinized name" (§1).
- **Place:** "Confucius was born in the domain of Zou, in modern Shandong Province, south of the larger kingdom of Lu." (§2).
- **Career:** "Different sources identify Confucius as having held a large number of different offices in Lu." (§2); "Late in life, Confucius left service and turned to teaching." (§2).
- **Students:** Sima Qian's *Shiji* (c. 145–c. 86 BCE, per §2) names them: "His account identifies 77 direct disciples"; "Altogether, some 3000 students received some form of this training regimen." (§2). Mencius, per Van Norden, "is said to have studied under Confucius’s grandson Zisi" (§1).
- **Caution on the biography**, as Csikszentmihalyi gives it: "Tying particular elements of his philosophy to the life experiences of Confucius is a risky and potentially circular exercise, since many of the details of his biography were first recorded in instructive anecdotes linked to the expression of didactic messages." (§2).

## Problems addressed

- **Family loyalty against public justice — Analects 13.18** (no problem page yet). The Duke of Sheh: "Among us here there are those who may be styled upright in their conduct. If their father have stolen a sheep, they will bear witness to the fact." Confucius: "Among us, in our part of the country, those who are upright are different from this. The father conceals the misconduct of the son, and the son conceals the misconduct of the father. Uprightness is to be found in this." (13.18, Legge). Wong's reading: "In this case, at least, loyalty to parents or to children takes precedence over loyalty to ruler or to public justice." (§2.4), which he files under "care with distinctions": "Though all people are owed moral concern, some are owed more than others, according to the agent’s relationship to them." (§2.4).
  - *Beside the Euthyphro.* Wong sets 13.18 next to the frame of Plato's dialogue, whose title character is "on his way to bring charges against his father for murder" (§1), and writes: "The practical problem discussed by Confucius and Socrates is arguably a universal one: the conflict between loyalty owed to a family member and duty to uphold public justice within the larger community." (§1). The dialogue's central question is mapped at **[the Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md)**; Wong's comparison concerns the frame case, not the 10a question.
- **[Moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)** — Wong locates Confucian attention elsewhere: "The focus of Book Ten and elsewhere in the Analects also suggests that the primary locus of virtue is to be found in how people treat each other in the fabric of everyday life and not in the dramatic moral dilemmas so much discussed in contemporary Western moral philosophy." (§2.1). Csikszentmihalyi reports a role conflict the sources work through: "Positing a parallel between the nature of reciprocal responsibilities of individuals in different roles in two domains of social organization, in the Analects Confucius linked filial piety in the family to loyalty in the political realm" (§5).
- **[Just war](../problems/just-war.md)** — Van Norden: "Confucius did not completely dismiss the use of force, but he regarded it as a last resort, and as ultimately ineffective when used as the primary method of governing." (§2).
- **Rectifying names** (no problem page yet). Asked what he would do first in government: "What is necessary is to rectify names." and "If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success." (13.3, Legge). On government by roles: "There is government, when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son." (12.11). The [white horse](../problems/white-horse-not-horse.md) page reports Hansen's reading, via Fraser, that Gongsun Long defended "the Confucian theory of “correcting names.”", and Fraser's dissent.
- **What a good life consists in — ren, li, junzi, xiao.** Csikszentmihalyi's list: "Five behaviors of the gentleman most central to the Analects are benevolence (ren 仁), righteousness (yi 義), ritual propriety (li 禮), wisdom (zhi 智), and trustworthiness (xin 信)." (§4).
  - *Ren* 仁. Legge renders it several ways. "Fan Ch'ih asked about benevolence. The Master said, 'It is to love all men.' He asked about knowledge. The Master said, 'It is to know all men.'" (12.22). "To subdue one's self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue." (12.1). To Chung-kung, part of the answer is "not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself" (12.2); to Tsze-kung: "Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." (15.23). Wong on the word's history: "Before Confucius’s time, the concept of ren referred to the aristocracy of bloodlines, meaning something like the strong and handsome appearance of an aristocrat. But in the Analects the concept is of a moral excellence that anyone has the potential to achieve." (§2.1).
  - *Li* 禮. "Look not at what is contrary to propriety; listen not to what is contrary to propriety; speak not what is contrary to propriety; make no movement which is contrary to propriety." (12.1). "If a man be without the virtues proper to humanity, what has he to do with the rites of propriety? If a man be without the virtues proper to humanity, what has he to do with music?" (3.3). Csikszentmihalyi: "Where Confucius clearly innovated was in his rationale for performing the rites and music." (§3); "In Analects 3.26, Confucius condemns the performance of ritual without reverence (jing 敬)." (§3).
  - *Junzi* 君子. Legge's "superior man": asked what makes one, "The cultivation of himself in reverential carefulness." (14.45). Wong: "The Chinese word originally meant “prince’s son,” but in the Analects it refers to ethical nobility. The first English translations rendered it as “gentleman,” but Ames and Rosemont (1998) have usefully suggested “exemplary person.”" (§2.1).
  - *Xiao* 孝. "That parents, when alive, be served according to propriety; that, when dead, they should be buried according to propriety; and that they should be sacrificed to according to propriety." (2.5). "The filial piety of now-a-days means the support of one's parents. But dogs and horses likewise are able to do something in the way of support;-- without reverence, what is there to distinguish the one support given from the other?" (2.7). The disciple Yu, not Confucius, says at 1.2: "Filial piety and fraternal submission!-- are they not the root of all benevolent actions?"
  - *Nature and practice.* "By nature, men are nearly alike; by practice, they get to be wide apart." (17.2). Csikszentmihalyi: it "suggests the importance of training one’s dispositions through ritual and the classics in a manner closer to the program of Xunzi (third century BCE)." (§4).

## Works

Works pages are not yet written ([works](../works/index.md)).

- **The *Analects* (*Lunyu* 論語)**, twenty books. Locator: book.chapter (Legge's numbering; Legge's 15.8 is cited by Csikszentmihalyi as 15.9). No writing by Confucius himself is attested in the sources read here; the *Analects* records sayings and dialogues.
- **The compilation question**, as Csikszentmihalyi reports it (§2):
  - *Traditional account.* "The last millennium has seen the development of a conventional view that materials preserved in the twenty chapters of the transmitted Analects most accurately represent Confucius’s original teachings." It derives "in part from a second century CE account by Ban Gu 班固 (39–92 CE)" that "describes the work as having been compiled by first and second generation disciples of Confucius and then transmitted privately for centuries". A variant: "Liu Baonan’s 劉寳楠 (1791–1855) view in Corrected Meanings of the Analects (Lunyu zhengyi 論語正義) that each chapter was written by a different disciple."
  - *Late-fixing account.* Zhu Weizheng 朱維錚 (1986, *Kongzi yanjiu* 1986(1): 40–52) "argued that the lack of attributed quotations from the Analects, and of explicit references to it, prior to the second century BCE, meant that its traditional status as the oldest stratum of the teachings of Confucius was undeserved." Then "a number of historians, including Michael J. Hunter, have systematically shown that writers started to demonstrate an acute interest in the Analects only in the late second and first centuries BCE". Of the Dingzhou, Pyongyang and Haihun manuscripts: "All in all, these finds confirm the sudden wide circulation of the Analects in the middle of the first century BCE."
  - *What the critique targets*, in Csikszentmihalyi's assessment: "Some have suggested this critical approach to sources is an attack on the historicity of Confucius, but a more reasonable description is that it is an attack on the authoritativeness of the Analects that broadens and diversifies the sources that may be used to reconstruct the historical Confucius." His conclusion: "the essential core of the teachings of Confucius is historically underdetermined, and the correct identification of the core teachings is still avidly debated."
- **Other Confucius materials** named in §2: the *Records of Ritual* (*Liji*), the *Elder Dai's Records of Ritual*, the *Family Discussions of Confucius* (*Kongzi jiayu*), the *Zuo Commentary*, and excavated texts. Not read here.
- **Self-description**: "A transmitter and not a maker, believing in and loving the ancients, I venture to compare myself with our old P'ang." (7.1, Legge). Csikszentmihalyi: "the virtues that Confucius taught were not original to him, but represented his adaptations of existing cultural ideals" (§4).

## In dialogue with

- **[Mencius](mencius.md)** — Csikszentmihalyi calls him "a figure generally regarded as the most important early developer of the thought of Confucius, Mencius (trad. 372–289 BCE)" (§2; the ranking is the one he reports). Van Norden: "Mencius (fourth century BCE) was a Confucian philosopher." (preamble).
- **Xunzi** (no page) — 17.2 read as closer to Xunzi's program (Csikszentmihalyi §4, above).
- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** — Wong's 13.18 / *Euthyphro* juxtaposition (§1, above). Hu Shi, per Csikszentmihalyi, "compared what he called the conservative aspect of the philosophy of Confucius to Socrates and Plato." (§1); see also [Plato](plato.md).
- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** — the virtue-ethics comparison: "Confucius taught that the measure of a good action was whether it was an expression of the actor’s virtue, something his lessons share with those of philosophies like Aristotle’s that are generally described as “virtue ethics”." (Csikszentmihalyi §4). Wong reports Yu (2007) as holding that the dao plays a role in ancient Chinese ethics analogous to that of eudaimonia in Greek ethics (§2.1).
- **Zhu Xi** (1130–1200; no page) — "The Neo-Confucian interpretation of the Analects by Zhu Xi 朱熹 (1130–1200) integrated the study of the Analects into a curriculum based on the “Four Books” (Sishu 四書) that became widely influential in China, Korea, and Japan." (§1).
- **Leibniz and Clerc** (no pages) — "Back in Europe, intellectuals read missionary descriptions and translations of Chinese literature, and writers like Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) and Nicolas-Gabriel Clerc (1726–1798) praised Confucius for his discovery of universal natural laws through reason." (§1).

## Reception

Readings of the ethics, side by side and unranked; each assessment is its
author's.

- **Virtue-ethics reading.** Csikszentmihalyi: "A modern evaluation of the teachings of Confucius as a “virtue ethics” is articulated in Bryan W. Van Norden’s Virtue Ethics and Consequentialism in Early Chinese Philosophy, which pays particular attention to analogies between the way of Confucius and Aristotle’s “good life”." (§4). Van Norden 2007, Cambridge University Press, DOI [10.1017/CBO9780511497995](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511497995) (verified by content negotiation; not read). Csikszentmihalyi's qualification: "The nature of the available source materials about Confucius, however, means that the diverse texts from early China lack the systematization of a work like Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics." (§4).
- **Wong on the dispute.** "A common way to understand Confucian ethics is that it is a virtue ethic. For some scholars this will be an obvious, uncontroversial truth. For others, it is a misconstrual that imposes contentious Western assumptions on Confucianism about what it is to be a person and what an ethics should be about." (§2.1). On the narrow sense of virtue ethics as a rival to deontology and consequentialism: "There simply is not enough discussion in the Confucian texts, especially in the classical period, that is addressed to the kind of questions these Western theories seek to answer." (§2.1). His position: virtue language in a broad sense fits, and "None of this is inconsistent with virtue characterizations in the broad sense (for an alternative role-ethic characterization of Confucian ethics that incorporates these insights in a different way, see Ames, 2011, 2021)." (§2.1).
- **Role-ethics reading (Hall, Ames, Rosemont).** Csikszentmihalyi reports Hall and Ames (*Thinking through Confucius*, 1987) on ren: "Benevolence entails a kind of unselfishness, or, as David Hall and Roger Ames suggest, it involves forming moral judgments from a combined perspective of self and others." (§4). On Rosemont and Ames (*Confucian Role Ethics*, 2016): "Writing with particular reference to the Classic of Filial Piety, Henry Rosemont and Roger Ames argue that prescribed social roles are a defining characteristic of the “Confucian tradition”, and that such roles were normative guides to appropriate conduct. They contrast this with the “virtue ethics” approach they say requires rational calculation to determine moral conduct, while filial piety is simply a matter of meeting one’s family obligations." (§5). Wong names the critics of the virtue label: "Prominent and influential critics of the “virtue” characterization of Confucian ethics--Roger Ames (2011, 2021) and Robert Neville (2016)--seem to be supposing that the term is loaded with such controversial presuppositions." (§2.1; "seem to be supposing" is Wong's reading of them). The role-ethics works are held bibliographically only, as the SEP bibliographies give them.
- **Fingarette's reading of li.** Wong: "In his influential interpretation (1972) of the Analects , Herbert Fingarette construes ritual performance as an end in itself, as beautiful and dignified, open and shared participation in ceremonies that celebrate human community." (§2.2; *Confucius: The Secular as Sacred*, Harper, 1972, per Wong's bibliography; not read). Wong's own position on ritual and ren: "Ritual constitutes an important part of what ren is, and hence it is not merely an instrument for refining the substance of ren . At the same time it is not the whole of ren ." (§2.2).
- **Ivanhoe on ritual**, as Csikszentmihalyi reports: "As Philip J. Ivanhoe has written, ritual and music are not just an indicator of values in the sense that these examples show, but also an inculcator of them." (§3).
- **Europe, praise and criticism.** "While at times he was criticized as an atheist or an advocate of despotism, many Europeans viewed Confucius as a moral philosopher whose approach was in line with rationalism and humanism." (Csikszentmihalyi §1).
- **Twentieth-century rejection.** "In the twentieth century, the pursuit of modernization also led to the rejection of Confucius by some reformers in the May Fourth and New Culture movements, as well as by many in the Communist Party, who identified the traditional hierarchies implicit in his social and political philosophy with the social and economic inequalities that they sought to eliminate." (§1).
- **Heaven.** Csikszentmihalyi's assessment: "Confucius himself arguably served as a historical counterexample to the classical “Mandate of Heaven” theory, calling into question the direct nature of the support given by Heaven to the person with virtue." (§5). On spirits, Analects 11.11: "While you are not able to serve men, how can you serve their spirits?" (Legge).

## Vocabulary

Translations differ; the terms have no vocabulary entries yet.

- *Ren* 仁 — Legge: "benevolence" (12.22), "perfect virtue" (12.1), "the virtues proper to humanity" (3.3). Wong: "D.C. Lau, 1970a, use ‘benevolence’ to translate ren", while ren "in some places in the Analects is treated as one virtue among others such as wisdom and courage." (§2.1).
- *Li* 禮 — Legge: "propriety", "rites of propriety". Wong: it ranges over "ceremonies of ancestor worship, the burial of parents, and customs governing respectful and appropriate behavior within the family and with friends" (§2.2).
- *Junzi* 君子 — Legge: "superior man"; "gentleman" (Csikszentmihalyi); "exemplary person" (Ames and Rosemont 1998, per Wong §2.1).
- *Xiao* 孝 — "filial piety" (Legge, Csikszentmihalyi, Wong).
- *Dilemma* — Wong's "dramatic moral dilemmas" uses the word in the sense of [dilemma](../vocabulary/dilemma.md).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Born** 384 BCE in Stagira: "Born in 384 B.C.E. in the Macedonian region of northeastern Greece in the small city of Stagira (whence the moniker ‘the Stagirite’, which one still occasionally encounters in Aristotelian scholarship), Aristotle was sent to Athens at about the age of seventeen to study in Plato’s Academy, then a pre-eminent place of learning in the Greek world." (Shields §1).
- **Academy** until 347: "Once in Athens, Aristotle remained associated with the Academy until Plato’s death in 347," (Shields §1); Assos and Lesbos followed (§1).
- **Alexander**, from 343: "In 343, upon the request of Philip, the king of Macedon, Aristotle left Lesbos for Pella, the Macedonian capital, in order to tutor the king’s thirteen-year-old son, Alexander—the boy who was eventually to become Alexander the Great." Shields adds: "Although speculation concerning Aristotle’s influence upon the developing Alexander has proven irresistible to historians, in fact little concrete is known about their interaction." (§1).
- **Lyceum**, 335: "He evidently remained a further five years in Stagira or Macedon before returning to Athens for the second and final time, in 335. In Athens, Aristotle set up his own school in a public exercise area dedicated to the god Apollo Lykeios, whence its name, the Lyceum." (Shields §1).
- **Nicomachus**: "In any event, they had children together, including a son, Nicomachus, named for Aristotle’s father and after whom his Nicomachean Ethics is presumably named." (Shields §1; "they" is Aristotle and Herpyllis, whose relation to him Shields reports as disputed).
- **Died** 322 at Chalcis: "After thirteen years in Athens, Aristotle once again found cause to retire from the city, in 323." "He withdrew directly to Chalcis, on Euboea, an island off the Attic coast, and died there of natural causes the following year, in 322." (Shields §1).
- **The two Ethics.** "Aristotle wrote two ethical treatises: the Nicomachean Ethics and the Eudemian Ethics." "The words “Eudemian” and “Nicomachean” were added later, perhaps because the former was edited by his friend, Eudemus, and the latter by his son, Nicomachus." (Kraut §1). On their order Kraut reports: "it is widely assumed that the Nicomachean Ethics is a later and improved version of the Eudemian Ethics." (§1). Bekker numbers below refer to the NE.

## Problems addressed

- **What is the human good? (eudaimonia; no problem page yet).** The NE opens: "Every art and every inquiry, and similarly every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good; and for this reason the good has rightly been declared to be that at which all things aim." (I.1, 1094a, opening lines). Kraut: "Aristotle’s search for the good is a search for the highest good, and he assumes that the highest good, whatever it turns out to be, has three characteristics: it is desirable for itself, it is not desirable for the sake of some other good, and all other goods are desirable for its sake." (§2).
  - *The function argument (NE I.7).* "To resolve this issue, Aristotle asks what the ergon (“function”, “task”, “work”) of a human being is, and argues that it consists in activity of the rational part of the soul in accordance with virtue (1097b22–1098a20)." (Kraut §2). Aristotle's question: "Have the carpenter, then, and the tanner certain functions or activities, and has man none? Is he born without a function?" His conclusion: "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete." (1098a, Perseus lookup at line 15), with the rider "For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy." (1098a, following).
  - *Activity, not state; external goods.* Kraut: "He says, not that happiness is virtue, but that it is virtuous activity." (§2), and on why fortune matters: "Aristotle’s reply is that one’s virtuous activity will be to some extent diminished or defective, if one lacks an adequate supply of other goods (1153b17–19)." (§2). Nelkin (SEP "Moral Luck" §4.1.2) links this tradition to [moral luck](../problems/moral-luck.md): "The idea that we ought to care about ethics, understood as Williams does, finds inspiration in the work of Aristotle." and "Hence, “the fragility of goodness” (Nussbaum)." (`raw/sep-moral-luck-fall-2024-nelkin-map.md`).
- **What is ethical virtue? (no problem page yet).** Habituation: "Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit." (NE II.1). Kraut: "Aristotle describes ethical virtue as a “hexis” (“state” “condition” “disposition”)—a tendency or disposition, induced by our habits, to have appropriate feelings (1105b25–6)." (§5.1).
  - *The mean.* Aristotle's definition: "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it." (II.6, 1106b–1107a, Perseus lookups at 1106b35 and 1107a1). Kraut: "Furthermore, every ethical virtue is a condition intermediate (a “golden mean” as it is popularly known) between two other states, one involving excess, and the other deficiency (1106a26–b28)." (§5.1), and "So far from offering a decision procedure, Aristotle insists that this is something that no ethical theory can do." (§5.2).
  - *Practical wisdom (phronesis).* "Practical wisdom, then, must be a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods." (VI.5, 1140b, Perseus lookup at line 20). Kraut: "Ethical virtue is fully developed only when it is combined with practical wisdom (1144b14–17)." (§4), and on Book VI's ranking of the two wisdoms: "So it is clear that exercising theoretical wisdom is a more important component of our ultimate goal than practical wisdom." (§6) — Kraut's reading of 1141a20–22, 1144a3–6 and 1145a8–11.
- **Is akrasia (acting against one's better judgement) possible? (NE VII; no problem page yet).** Method: "We must, as in all other cases, set the observed facts before us and, after first discussing the difficulties, go on to prove, if possible, the truth of all the common opinions" (VII.1). He reports Socrates: "Socrates was entirely opposed to the view in question, holding that there is no such thing as incontinence; no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best-people act so only by reason of ignorance." and replies "Now this view plainly contradicts the observed facts" (VII.2, 1145b, Perseus lookup at line 25). His own analysis compares the incontinent's knowledge to that of a man who speaks "as a drunken man may utter the verses of Empedocles." and concludes "the position that Socrates sought to establish actually seems to result; for it is not in the presence of what is thought to be knowledge proper that the affection of incontinence arises (nor is it this that is 'dragged about' as a result of the state of passion), but in that of perceptual knowledge." (VII.3).
  - Kraut's reading: "What is most remarkable about Aristotle’s discussion of akrasia is that he defends a position close to that of Socrates." "in a way Socrates was right after all (1147b13–17). For, he says, the person who acts against reason does not have what is thought to be unqualified knowledge; in a way he has knowledge, but in a way does not." "But Aristotle’s agreement with Socrates is only partial, because he insists on the power of the emotions to rival, weaken or bypass reason." (§7). Kraut also reports the two kinds: "Aristotle distinguishes two kinds of akrasia: impetuosity (propeteia) and weakness (astheneia)." (§7).
- **Which life is best? (NE X.7–8).** "If happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, it is reasonable that it should be in accordance with the highest virtue; and this will be that of the best thing in us. Whether it be reason or something else that is this element which is thought to be our natural ruler and guide and to take thought of things noble and divine, whether it be itself also divine or only the most divine element in us, the activity of this in accordance with its proper virtue will be perfect happiness." "That this activity is contemplative we have already said." (X.7, 1177a, Perseus lookups at lines 10 and 15). Kraut: "Perfect happiness, he says, consists in contemplation; but he indicates that the life devoted to practical thought and ethical virtue is happy in a secondary way." (§10). The rival readings are under *Reception*.
- **[Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md).** *Posterior Analytics* I.1, 71a: "clearly he knows not without qualification but only in the sense that he knows universally. If this distinction is not drawn, we are faced with the dilemma in the Meno: either a man will learn nothing or what he already knows; for we cannot accept the solution which some people offer." (Mure tr.; `raw/aristotle-posterior-analytics-1-1-71a-meno-dilemma-mure.md`).
- **[Agrippan trilemma](../problems/agrippan-trilemma.md).** *Posterior Analytics* I.3, 72b: the regress school is "right, for one cannot traverse an infinite series" (within the sentence quoted in `raw/aristotle-posterior-analytics-1-3-72b-regress-and-circular-demonstration-mure.md`), and "Our own doctrine is that not all knowledge is demonstrative: on the contrary, knowledge of the immediate premisses is independent of demonstration." Hasan & Fumerton (SEP) call I.3 "an early version of this argument" (`raw/sep-justep-foundational-and-coherence-fall-2024-regress-argument.md`).
- **[Zeno's paradoxes](../problems/zenos-paradoxes.md).** Huggett (SEP "Zeno's Paradoxes"): "Aristotle speaks of a further four arguments against motion (and by extension change generally), all of which he gives and attempts to refute." Aristotle on the Achilles: "the axiom that that which holds a lead is never overtaken is false" (within *Physics* VI.9, 239b26–29; `raw/aristotle-physics-6-9-and-8-8-zeno-motion-hardie-gaye.md`, `raw/sep-paradox-zeno-fall-2024-motion-paradoxes-and-responses.md`).
- **[Buridan's ass](../problems/buridans-ass.md).** Zupko (SEP "John Buridan" §7): the example has "versions of it going back at least to Aristotle (see De Caelo 295b32)." (`raw/sep-buridan-fall-2024-buridans-ass-origin.md`; the passage: `raw/aristotle-de-caelo-2-13-295b-indifference-hungry-man-stocks.md`).
- **[Argument from free will](../problems/argument-from-free-will.md).** On future contingents, Zagzebski & Hunt (SEP "Foreknowledge and Free Will" §2.1): "This seems to have been the position of Aristotle in the famous Sea Battle argument of De Interpretatione IX" (`raw/sep-free-will-foreknowledge-fall-2024-theological-fatalism.md`).
- **Further mentions on existing pages.** The [liar paradox](../problems/liar-paradox.md): "it was also mentioned by Aristotle and by Cicero" (Beall et al., in `raw/sep-sorites-and-liar-fall-2024-paradox-formulations-and-responses.md`). [Paradoxes of material implication](../problems/paradoxes-of-material-implication.md): "Aristotle held that some syllogisms with inconsistent premises are valid, whereas others are not (An. Pr. 64a 15)." (`raw/sep-dialetheism-fall-2024-explosion-definition-and-aristotle.md`). The [paradox of the court](../problems/paradox-of-the-court.md): Diogenes Laertius IX.54, "Aristotle, however, says it was Euathlus." (`raw/gellius-attic-nights-5-10-protagoras-euathlus-rolfe.md`). The [paradox of fiction](../problems/paradox-of-fiction.md): Schneider (IEP) on "the old and established thesis, traceable as far back as Aristotle" (`raw/iep-paradox-of-fiction-schneider.md`). Rhetoric: the three proofs (*Rhetoric* I.2, 1356a), used on [appeal to authority](../persuasion/appeal-to-authority.md).

## In dialogue with

- **[Plato](plato.md)** — teacher (Shields §1). On the Form of the Good, NE I.6 prefaces the critique with "for, while both are dear, piety requires us to honour truth above our friends." Kraut: "He rejects the existence of Plato’s forms in general and the form of the good in particular;" (§3.2), and on Book VII: "It should be noticed that Aristotle’s treatment of akrasia is heavily influenced by Plato’s tripartite division of the soul in the Republic." (§7). On virtue as disposition: "The significance of Aristotle’s characterization of these states as hexeis is his decisive rejection of the thesis, found throughout Plato’s early dialogues, that virtue is nothing but a kind of knowledge and vice nothing but a lack of knowledge." (§5.1). Meno's paradox: above.
- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** — akrasia, above; and on virtue and practical wisdom: "in thinking that all the virtues were forms of practical wisdom he was wrong, but in saying they implied practical wisdom he was right." (NE VI.13).
- **The Stoics; [Epictetus](epictetus.md)** — on the emotions in virtue, Kraut: "These are precisely the questions that were asked in antiquity by the Stoics, and they came to the conclusion that such common emotions as anger and fear are always inappropriate." (§5.1). See [Stoicism](../schools/stoicism.md).
- **[Aquinas](aquinas.md)** — Shields: "Among Aristotle’s greatest exponents during the early period of his reintroduction to the West, Albertus Magnus, and above all his student Thomas Aquinas, sought to reconcile Aristotle’s philosophy with Christian thought." (§14). Assessments of that project are under *Reception*.
- **Avicenna and Averroes** — Shields §14 reports the Arabic tradition, "where Aristotle was so prominent that be became known simply as The First Teacher" ("be" sic).
- **Zeno, Anaximander, Protagoras and Euathlus, Agrippa's regress schools** — through the problem pages above.
- **[Bernard Williams](williams.md), Nussbaum** — Nelkin's moral-luck link, above.
- **[Philippa Foot](foot.md), [Elizabeth Anscombe](anscombe.md)** — Hacker-Wright (SEP "Philippa Foot"): "Foot is also known for contributing to the revival of Aristotelian virtue ethics in contemporary philosophy, though it is less well known that she emphatically disavowed being an adherent of this view as it is currently understood." Wiland & Driver (SEP "Elizabeth Anscombe" §2) report: "Crisp 2004 suspected that she was surreptitiously arguing for the superiority of a God-based moral philosophy over any secular moral philosophy, even one like Aristotle’s." (`raw/sep-philippa-foot-fall-2024-life-applied-ethics-natural-goodness.md`, `raw/sep-anscombe-fall-2024-life-ethics-intention.md`).

## Reception

Assessments, each with its owner; for and against listed in parallel,
unranked.

- **Standing.** Shields (SEP "Aristotle", preamble): "Aristotle (384–322 B.C.E.) numbers among the greatest philosophers of all time. Judged solely in terms of his philosophical influence, only Plato is his peer:". Kraut (§1): "No one had written ethical treatises before Aristotle." Both are the encyclopedia authors' assessments.
- **Aquinas's synthesis — both camps, as Shields reports them.** "Some Aristotelians disdain Aquinas as bastardizing Aristotle, while some Christians disown Aquinas as pandering to pagan philosophy. Many others in both camps take a much more positive view, seeing Thomism as a brilliant synthesis of two towering traditions;" (§14).
- **Virtue ethics.** Shields: "though perhaps Aristotle’s influence is seen most overtly and avowedly in the resurgence of virtue ethics which began in the last half of the twentieth century." (§14). Hursthouse & Pettigrove (SEP "Virtue Ethics" §3) report critics who say neo-Aristotelians rely on "Aristotle’s discredited natural teleology (Williams 1985)" — the critics' words as reported (raw file above, section D context).
- **On the function argument and the traditional virtues — the objection.** Kraut reports: "A common complaint about Aristotle’s attempt to defend his conception of happiness is that his argument is too general to show that it is in one’s interest to possess any of the particular virtues as they are traditionally conceived." (§3.1). Kraut's own reply follows in §§3.1–3.2 (not quoted here).
- **On the mean — objection and defence, both in Kraut.** Objection: "The objection, then, is that Aristotle’s doctrine of the mean, taken as a doctrine about what the ethical agent does when he deliberates, is in many cases inapplicable or unilluminating." (§5.1). Kraut then sketches a defence with a juror example (§5.1; raw file context).
- **On NE X.7–8 — intellectualist vs inclusivist readings.** Green (2017, SAGP paper, `raw/green-2017-secondary-happiness-intellectualist-inclusivist.md`) frames the debate: "Nicomachean Ethics (NE) X.7-8 has long vexed commentators by apparently endorsing an ‘intellectualist’ view of eudaimonia¸ where contemplation is the sole constituent of happiness." "Hence there has been a long-running effort to show that the NE actually endorses an ‘inclusivist’ view of eudaimonia instead, where moral virtue and contemplation are both features of a single kind of happiness." A third group: "Others have argued that X.7-8 is flatly inconsistent with the rest of the NE, and should be ignored if not excised." His list of contributors: "To give just a very incomplete list, see Ackrill (1974), Annas (1988), Bostock (2000), Charles (1999) and (2014), Cooper (1975), Crisp (1994), Devereux (1981), Hardie (1965) and (1979), Irwin (1988), Keyt (1983), Moline (1983), Reeve (2012), Roche (1989), White (1990)." (n. 1).
  - Kraut reports the inconsistency reading: "In fact, some scholars have held that X.7–8 are deeply at odds with the rest of the Ethics; they take Aristotle to be saying that we should be prepared to act unethically, if need be, in order to devote ourselves as much as possible to contemplation." His own reading: "The best way to understand him is to take him to be assuming that one will need the ethical virtues in order to live the life of a philosopher, even though exercising those virtues is not the philosopher’s ultimate end." (§10).
  - Two founding papers, held bibliographically only (DOIs verified 2026-10-02): W. F. R. Hardie, "The Final Good in Aristotle's Ethics", *Philosophy* 40(154), 1965, pp. 277–295, [10.1017/s0031819100069709](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0031819100069709); J. L. Ackrill, "Aristotle on Eudaimonia", in A. O. Rorty (ed.), *Essays on Aristotle's Ethics*, University of California Press, 1980, pp. 15–34, [10.1525/9780520340985-005](https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520340985-005) (Green dates the original 1974). Also Kraut, "Two Conceptions of Happiness", *Philosophical Review* 88(2), 1979, pp. 167–197, [10.2307/2184505](https://doi.org/10.2307/2184505). Which side each takes is not stated here: the read sources list them without assigning sides.

## Vocabulary

Kraut's glossary renders the Greek terms; the senses below are his.

- *Eudaimonia* — "happiness", "flourishing" (Kraut glossary; §1). Kraut on eudaimonia and eu zên: "These terms play an evaluative role, and are not simply descriptions of someone’s state of mind." (§2). No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Ergon* — "function", "task", "work" (Kraut §2).
- *Hexis* — "state", "condition", "disposition" (Kraut §5.1).
- *Phronesis* — "practical wisdom" (Kraut glossary); defined at NE VI.5 as quoted above.
- *Akrasia* — "incontinence (literally: lack of mastery)" (Kraut glossary); its opposite *enkrateia*, continence.
- *Knowledge* — Aristotle's "knowing universally" (Post. An. I.1) and "knowledge proper" vs "perceptual knowledge" (NE VII.3) are distinctions within knowing; this implant's sense is in [knowledge](../vocabulary/knowledge.md).

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates (modern reference):** "Plato (429?–347 B.C.E.) is, by any reckoning, one of the most dazzling writers in the Western literary tradition" (Kraut, preamble). The birth year carries Kraut's question mark.
- **Dates (ancient reports):** Diogenes Laertius (Hicks tr., Loeb 1925, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_III)):
  "Apollodorus in his Chronology fixes the date of Plato's birth in the 88th Olympiad, on the seventh day of the month Thargelion, the same day on which the Delians say that Apollo himself was born." (D.L. 3.2);
  "He died, according to Hermippus, at a wedding feast, in the first year of the 108th Olympiad, in his eightyfirst year." (3.2; Hicks's note converts to 427–347 B.C.);
  "Neanthes, however, makes him die at the age of eighty-four." (3.3). Diogenes also reports birth "in the archonship of Ameinias, the year of Pericles' death" (3.3; Hicks: 429 B.C.) — so the sources Diogenes names disagree among themselves
  (`raw/diogenes-laertius-3-hicks-life-of-plato.md`).
- **Athens and status:** "An Athenian citizen of high status, he displays in his works his absorption in the political events and intellectual movements of his time" (Kraut, preamble).
- **Family:** "He had two brothers, Adeimantus and Glaucon, and a sister, Potone, who was the mother of Speusippus." (D.L. 3.4). Glaucon and Adeimantus are the speakers who press Socrates in *Republic* II (Frede & Lee §3.1).
- **Socrates:** "From that time onward, having reached his twentieth year (so it is said), he was the pupil of Socrates." (D.L. 3.6). Kraut (§11): Plato "probably began to write dialogues somewhere in his thirties (he was around 28 when Socrates was killed)".
- **The Academy:** "Having returned to Athens, he lived in the Academy, which is a gymnasium outside the walls, in a grove named after a certain hero, Hecademus" (D.L. 3.7).
- **Aristotle:** Kraut's preamble names "Aristotle (who studied with him)".
- **Letters:** "Further evidence of Plato’s interest in practical matters can be drawn from his letters, if they are genuine." (Kraut §10) — and: "In most of them, he presents himself as having a deep interest in educating (with the help of his friend, Dion) the ruler of Syracuse, Dionysius II, and thus reforming that city’s politics." Authenticity of the letters is left open by Kraut.

## Problems addressed

- **[Euthyphro dilemma](../problems/euthyphro-dilemma.md)** (*Euthyphro* 10a). Socrates' question, Fowler's translation: "Just consider this question:—Is that which is holy loved by the gods because it is holy, or is it holy because it is loved by the gods?" (10a).
  Euthyphro takes the first horn: "It is loved because it is holy, not holy because it is loved?" (10d), and Socrates infers: "Then that which is dear to the gods and that which is holy are not identical, but differ one from the other." (10e)
  (`raw/plato-euthyphro-9e-11b-pious-loved-because-pious-jowett-fowler.md`).
  Hare (SEP "Religion and Morality" §1): "Socrates's problem with the traditional stories about the gods gives rise to what is sometimes called ‘the Euthyphro dilemma’."
  (`raw/sep-religion-morality-fall-2024-euthyphro-ashari-voluntarists.md`).
  On the dialogue and the forms, Frede & Lee (§2.2): "This presupposition is indeed made explicit in the Euthyphro, where Plato employs, for the first time, the terminology that will be characteristic of his full-fledged theory of Forms. In response to Euthyphro’s enumeration of various examples of pious behavior, Socrates demands an account of the one feature (Euthphr. 5d: idea; 6d: eidos; 6e: paradeigma) that is common to all cases of what is holy or pious."
  — and they add: "Despite this pregnant terminology, few scholars nowadays hold that the Euthyphro already presupposes transcendent Forms, in a realm of their own".
- **[Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md)** (*Meno* 80d–86c, 98a). Meno, Jowett tr.: "And how will you enquire, Socrates, into that which you do not know? What will you put forth as the subject of enquiry? And if you find what you want, how will you ever know that this is the thing which you did not know?" (80d).
  Socrates' reply introduces recollection: "for all enquiry and all learning is but recollection." (81d, Jowett); later, true opinions "are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it." (97e–98a, Jowett)
  (`raw/plato-meno-80d-86c-98a-paradox-and-recollection-jowett-lamb.md`).
  Rawson (IEP "Meno" §2b) on the slave-boy passage: "But the geometry lesson with the slave clearly does not demonstrate the reminding of something that was learned in a previous life."
  and the later formulation at 86a turns recollection "into the beginnings of a theory of innate ideas" (Rawson §2b, `raw/iep-meno-rawson-and-epicurus-okeefe-paradox-of-inquiry.md`).
- **[I know that I know nothing](../problems/i-know-that-i-know-nothing.md)** (*Apology* 21d; *Republic* I 354c). The *Apology*, Fowler tr.: "I am wiser than this man; for neither of us really knows anything fine and good, but this man thinks he knows something when he does not, whereas I, as I do not know anything, do not think I do either." (21d; `raw/plato-apology-21b-22d-29b-socratic-ignorance-jowett-fowler.md`).
  Frede & Lee (§3.1) translate Republic 354c: "As far as I am concerned, the result is that I know nothing, for when I don’t know what justice is, I’ll hardly know whether it is a kind of virtue or not, or whether a person who has it is happy or unhappy."
- **[Moral dilemmas](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)** (*Republic* I 331c). McConnell (SEP "Moral Dilemmas") opens with it: "In Book I of Plato’s Republic, Cephalus defines ‘justice’ as speaking the truth and paying one’s debts." Shorey's 331c: returning weapons to a friend who has gone mad, "he who did so return them would not be acting justly" (`raw/sep-moral-dilemmas-fall-2024-concept-and-consistency-arguments.md`).
- **[Experience machine](../problems/experience-machine.md)** (*Philebus* 21a). Andrew Moore (SEP "Hedonism" §2.3.1) cites the dialogue as a precedent: "His conclusion was that “your life would be the life, not of a man, but of an oyster” (Philebus 21a)." (`raw/sep-hedonism-fall-2024-experience-machine.md`).
- **[Paradox of nihilism](../problems/paradox-of-nihilism.md)** (*Theaetetus* 171a–b). The turning of Protagoras' measure doctrine on itself, Jowett tr.: "SOCRATES: And the best of the joke is, that he acknowledges the truth of their opinion who believe his own opinion to be false; for he admits that the opinions of all men are true." (`raw/plato-theaetetus-171a-jowett-peritrope.md`).
- **[Zeno's paradoxes](../problems/zenos-paradoxes.md)** (*Parmenides* 127b–128d) — the biographical source on Zeno, as that page reports from Huggett (`raw/plato-parmenides-127b-128d-zeno-and-his-book.md`).
- **Justice and whether it pays** (*Republic* II–IV; no problem page yet). Glaucon's Ring of Gyges (359d–360d), Shorey tr.: "And yet this is a great proof, one might argue, that no one is just of his own will but only from constraint, in the belief that justice is not his personal good, inasmuch as every man, when he supposes himself to have the power to do wrong, does wrong." (360c).
  Frede & Lee (§3.1), on the brothers' case: "As the story of the Ring of Gyges and its gift of invisibility proves, everyone who does not have a god-like character will eventually succumb to such a ring’s temptations (359c–360d)."
  The answer in Book IV: "And again that to do one's own business and not to be a busybody is justice," (433a); the soul's parts: "Does it not belong to the rational part to rule, being wise and exercising forethought in behalf of the entire soul, and to the principle of high spirit to be subject to this and its ally?" (441e).
  Frede & Lee (§3.2): "The lengthy argument for the tri-partition of the soul into a rational (logistikon), a spirited (thumoeides), and an appetitive (epithumêtikon) part (434d–441c) can here be neither reproduced nor subjected to a critical evaluation."
  "Justice turns out to be the overall unifying quality of the soul, for the just person not only refrains from meddling with what is not his externally but also harmonizes the three parts of the soul internally."
  "Justice and injustice in the soul are, then, analogous to health and illness in the body."
- **Doing and suffering wrong** (*Gorgias*). Socrates to Polus, Lamb tr.: "if it were necessary either to do wrong or to suffer it, I should choose to suffer rather than do it." (469c); "for a good and honorable man or woman, I say, is happy, and an unjust and wicked one is wretched." (470e).
  Frede & Lee (§2.1) describe "Socrates’ long and passionate argument in the Gorgias against Polus and Callicles that the just life is better for the soul of its possessor than the unjust life, an argument that he fortifies with a mythical depiction of the soul’s reward and punishment after death (523a–527e)."
- **Pleasure and the good** (*Philebus*). The opening thesis, Fowler tr.: "Philebus says that to all living beings enjoyment and pleasure and gaiety and whatever accords with that sort of thing are a good;" (11b), against Socrates' claim for "wisdom and thought and memory and their kindred" (11b–c).
  Frede & Lee (§4.2): the dialogue "presents happiness as a mixture of all kinds of knowledge with true and pure kinds of pleasure (59d-64b)."
  "If Plato in the Philebus is more favorably disposed towards a hedonist stance than in some of his earlier works, he is so only to a quite limited degree: He regards pleasure as a necessary ingredient in human life because both the physical and the psychic equilibria that constitute human nature are unstable."
- **The forms** (no position page yet). Kraut (§1): "The most fundamental distinction in Plato’s philosophy is between the many observable objects that appear beautiful (good, just, unified, equal, big) and the one object that is what beauty (goodness, justice, unity) really is, from which those many beautiful (good, just, unified, equal, big) things receive their names and their corresponding characteristics."
  Frede & Lee (preamble): "This changes in the middle dialogues that show a growing interest in an all-encompassing metaphysical grounding of knowledge, a development that leads to the positing of the ‘Forms’ as the determinants of the true nature of all things, culminating in the Form of the Good as the transcendent principle of all goodness."
  In *Republic* V–VII, "for the ‘Form of the Good’ turns out to be the ultimate source of all being and knowledge." (Frede & Lee §3.2).
  The *Parmenides* criticises them: "In Parmenides, the principal interlocutor (not Socrates—he is here portrayed as a promising, young philosopher in need of further training—but rather the pre-Socratic from Elea who gives the dialogue its name: Parmenides) subjects the forms to withering criticism, and then consents to conduct an inquiry into the nature of oneness that has no overt connection to his critique of the forms." (Kraut §9). Whether Plato then revised his view of forms is a question Kraut (§9) leaves open.

**Order of composition — a reported dispute.**

- *The three-period hypothesis.* Kraut (§11) reports it "associated especially with Gregory Vlastos’s name (see especially his Socrates Ironist and Moral Philosopher, chapters 2 and 3)". Its early group: "Into this category of early dialogues (they are also sometimes called “Socratic” dialogues, possibly without any intended chronological connotation) are placed: Charmides, Crito, Euthydemus, Euthyphro, Gorgias, Hippias Major, Hippias Minor, Ion, Laches, Lysis, and Protagoras,". Middle, per Kraut: *Phaedo*, *Cratylus*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus*.
- *Stylometry, for the late group.* "These five dialogues together with Laws are generally agreed to be his late works, because they have much more in common with each other, when one counts certain stylistic features apparent only to readers of Plato’s Greek, than with any of Plato’s other works." The five: *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Timaeus*, *Critias*, *Philebus*. "(Computer counts have aided these stylometric studies, but the isolation of a group of six dialogues by means of their stylistic commonalities was recognized in the nineteenth century. See Brandwood 1990, Young 1994.)" (Kraut §11).
- *The dispute.* "In fact, it remains a matter of dispute whether the division of Plato’s works into three periods—early, middle, late—does correctly indicate the order of composition, and whether it is a useful tool for the understanding of his thought (See Cooper 1997, vii–xxvii)." Kraut's own suggestion: "For example although both Euthydemus and Charmides are widely assumed to be early dialogues, they might have been written around the same time as Symposium and Republic, which are generally assumed to be compositions of his middle period—or even later."
- *Unitarians, revisionists, developmentalists* (Frede & Lee §2.1): "Whereas unitarians regard the dialogues as pieces of one mosaic and take the view that Plato, in essence, maintains a unified doctrine from his earliest to his latest works, revisionists maintain that Plato’s thought underwent a fundamental transformation later in his life, while ‘developmentalists’ hold that Plato’s views evolved significantly throughout his career."
  Their report of the state of play: "While revisionism has lost its impact in recent years, developmentalism has gained in influence." and "Although there is no unanimity, few unitarians nowadays deny that the character of Plato’s early, middle, and late works differs in style, language, scope, and content, as is to be expected in a philosopher who was at work for more than fifty years."
- *Voice.* "In all of his writings—except in the letters, if any of them are genuine—Plato never speaks to his audience directly (see Frede 1992) and in his own voice." (Kraut §5). Kraut (§11) argues that the moves in even the early dialogues are more likely "the products of Plato’s mind" than records of conversations; see [Socrates](socrates.md).

## In dialogue with

- **[Socrates](socrates.md)** — his teacher (D.L. 3.6) and main speaker. Kraut (§11): "The idea that it is important to search for definitions may have been Socratic in origin."
- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** — student ("who studied with him", Kraut preamble). On Meno's paradox, *Posterior Analytics* I.1, Mure tr.: "If this distinction is not drawn, we are faced with the dilemma in the Meno" (71a; `raw/aristotle-posterior-analytics-1-1-71a-meno-dilemma-mure.md`). On akrasia, Frede & Lee (§2.1): "Socrates’ proof in the Protagoras that reason cannot be overcome by the passions has, from Aristotle on, been treated as a denial of akrasia, of the phenomenon that was later somewhat misleadingly dubbed as ‘weakness of the will’."
- **[Epicurus](epicurus.md)** — on inquiry. O'Keefe (IEP "Epicurus" §4a): "This concern is similar to the Paradox of Inquiry explored by Plato in the Meno, that one must already know about something in order to be able to inquire about it." and "However, instead of postulating that our immaterial souls had acquaintance with transcendent Forms in a pre-natal existence, as Plato does, Epicurus thinks that we have certain ‘preconceptions’–concepts such as ‘body,’ ‘person,’ ‘usefulness,’ and ‘truth’–which are formed in our (material) minds as the result of repeated sense-experiences of similar objects." (`raw/iep-meno-rawson-and-epicurus-okeefe-paradox-of-inquiry.md`).
- **[Aquinas](aquinas.md)** — on the Euthyphro dilemma, through later readers. Austin (IEP "Divine Command Theory" §4b): "Another response to the Euthyphro Dilemma which is intended to avoid the problem of arbitrariness is discussed by Clark and Poortenga (2003), drawing upon the moral theory of Thomas Aquinas." (`raw/iep-divine-command-theory-austin-euthyphro-responses.md`).
- **Protagoras** (no page) — the target of the *Theaetetus* peritrope (171a–b), above.
- **Parmenides and Zeno** (no pages) — interlocutors in the *Parmenides* (127b–128d), above.

## Reception

- Kraut (preamble), his assessment: "Plato (429?–347 B.C.E.) is, by any reckoning, one of the most dazzling writers in the Western literary tradition"; and "Few other authors in the history of Western philosophy approximate him in depth and range: perhaps only Aristotle (who studied with him), Aquinas, and Kant would be generally agreed to be of the same rank."
- Kraut (§2), on open-endedness: "All of Plato’s works are in some way meant to leave further work for their readers, but among the ones that most conspicuously fall into this category are: Euthyphro, Laches, Charmides, Euthydemus, Theaetetus, and Parmenides."
- Kraut (§10), on politics: "For example, on any plausible reading of Republic, Plato evinces a deep antipathy to rule by the many." Kraut sets this beside the *Laws*, where non-philosophers are given powers as rulers, and asks whether Plato changed his mind.
- Frede & Lee (§2.1), classifying his ethics: "Like most other ancient philosophers, Plato maintains a virtue-based eudaemonistic conception of ethics. That is to say, happiness or well-being (eudaimonia) is the highest aim of moral thought and conduct, and the virtues (aretê: ‘excellence’) are the dispositions/skills needed to attain it."
- Frede & Lee (§3.3), on *Republic* I: "Though Socrates’ refutation of Thrasymachus is found wanting as a proof of justice’s superiority, the ergon-argument is nowhere revoked."
- Austin (IEP §3), on the *Euthyphro*: "The dialogue between Socrates and Euthyphro is nearly omnipresent in philosophical discussions of the relationship between God and ethics."
- Rawson (IEP "Meno" §2b), on the paradox: "That is enough to refute Meno’s Paradox, which inferred the impossibility of learning from a false dichotomy between complete knowledge and pure ignorance."
- Critical readings of particular arguments are on the problem pages linked above (e.g. the replies to the Euthyphro dilemma and to recollection); no general assessment *against* Plato from a read source is recorded here yet — a visible gap.

## Vocabulary

- **form / idea** (*eidos*, *idea*): Kraut (§1) reports the forms are "often capitalized by those who write about Plato" (Frede & Lee write "Forms"); the Greek terms in *Euthyphro* 5d–6e as Frede & Lee give them: "idea", "eidos", "paradeigma". No vocabulary page yet.
- **recollection** (*anamnesis*, *Meno* 81d): learning as recollection; see [Meno's paradox](../problems/menos-paradox.md).
- **knowledge**: in the *Meno* (98a) true opinion becomes knowledge when "fastened by the tie of the cause"; the implant's normative sense is in [knowledge](../vocabulary/knowledge.md), which takes no side on the analysis.
- **justice** (*Republic* IV 433a): "to do one's own business and not to be a busybody"; in the soul, per Frede & Lee (§3.2), the harmony of its three parts. No vocabulary page yet.

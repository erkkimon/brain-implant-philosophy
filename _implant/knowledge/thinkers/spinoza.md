## Facts a claim depends on

- **Names and birth:** "Bento (in Hebrew, Baruch; in Latin, Benedictus: all three names mean “blessed”) Spinoza was born in 1632 in Amsterdam." (Nadler §1).
- **Family:** "He was the middle son in a prominent family of moderate means in Amsterdam’s Portuguese-Jewish community." (§1).
- **Herem (1656), as reported:** "And then, on July 27, 1656, Spinoza was issued the harshest writ of herem, ban or excommunication, ever pronounced by the Sephardic community of Amsterdam; it was never rescinded." (Nadler §1). The ban's text
  is not held here; Nadler reports its wording "monstrous deeds" and
  "abominable heresies" and gives his own reading of the grounds:
  "We do not know for certain what Spinoza’s “monstrous deeds” and “abominable heresies” were alleged to have been, but an educated guess comes quite easy." He bases the guess on the later treatises:
  "In those works, Spinoza denies the immortality of the soul; strongly rejects the notion of a transcendent, providential God—the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; and claims that the Law (i.e., the commandments of the Torah and rabbinic legal principles) was neither literally given by God nor any longer binding on Jews." (§1). This is Nadler's inference, labelled by him as a guess;
  no statement by the community's officers giving the grounds is held here.
- **Residences:** "By the time his extant correspondence begins, in 1661, he is living in Rijnsburg, not far from Leiden." (§1).
- **Only book under his name:** "His critical exposition of Descartes’s Principles of Philosophy, the only work he published under his own name in his lifetime, was completed in 1663, after he had moved to Voorburg, outside The Hague." (§1).
- **Theological-Political Treatise (1670):** "published anonymously and to great alarm in 1670 (one overwrought critic called it “a book forged in hell by the devil himself”)." (§1).
- **Death:** "When Spinoza died in 1677, in The Hague, he was still at work on his Political Treatise; this was soon published by his friends along with his other unpublished writings," (§1); Steinberg (§4.3.3) gives the day: "when he died on February 21, 1677."
- **The Ethics in print:** "The publication of the unfinished TP in Spinoza’s posthumous Opera was met with relative indifference, upstaged as it was by the simultaneous appearance of Ethics (Laerke 2010, 122)." (Steinberg §6). The Buridan
  excerpt cites Zupko (SEP "John Buridan", n. 57) for "completed 1676".

## Problems addressed

- **God or Nature, and necessity** (*Ethics* I, IV Pref). "PROP. XV. Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or be conceived." (I P15).
  The phrase itself: "For the eternal and infinite Being, which we call God or Nature, acts by the same necessity as that whereby it exists." (IV Pref). Nadler (§2.1): "Spinoza’s metaphysics of God is neatly summed up in a phrase that occurs in the Latin (but not the original Dutch) edition of the Ethics: “God, or Nature”, Deus, sive Natura: “That eternal and infinite being we call God, or Nature, acts from the same necessity from which he exists” (Part IV, Preface)."
  "The friends who, after his death, published his writings left out the “or Nature” clause from the more widely accessible Dutch version, probably out of fear of the reaction that this identification would, predictably, arouse among a vernacular audience." (§2.1).
  On necessity: "PROP. XXIX. Nothing in the universe is contingent, but all things are conditioned to exist and operate in a particular manner by the necessity of the divine nature." (I P29). Nadler (§2.1): "God or Nature does not act for any ends, and things do not exist for any set purposes."
  Newlands reports that "considerable disagreement has emerged about Spinoza’s modal commitments." (preamble); for the reading that
  finite modes are not strictly necessary, "Curley suggests that Spinoza’s finite modes are only partially determined by other finite modes (Curley 1969)." (§1.2.4).
- **Free will and determinism.** "PROP. XLVIII. In the mind there is no absolute or free will; but the mind is determined to wish this or that by a cause, which has also been determined by another cause, and this last by another cause, and so on to infinity." (II P48). Nadler (§2.4) draws
  the consequence: "First, it implies that a human being is not endowed with freedom, at least in the ordinary sense of that term."
  On [Buridan's ass](../problems/buridans-ass.md), Spinoza grants that a man
  in the described equilibrium "would die of hunger and thirst" (II P49 S;
  position 1 on that page); Bayle calls this concession "very ill grounded"
  (`raw/bayle-1697-dictionary-buridan-remark-c-ass.md`).
- **Mind and body** (see [consciousness](../problems/consciousness.md), the
  mind–body problem). "PROP. VII. The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." (II P7). Nadler (§2.2): "Spinoza, in effect, denies that the human being is a union of two substances. The human mind and the human body are two different expressions—under thought and under extension—of one and the same thing: the person. And because there is no causal interaction between the mind and the body, the so-called mind-body problem does not, technically speaking, arise."
- **Conatus (striving).** "PROP. VI. Everything, in so far as it is in itself, endeavours to persist in its own being." (III P6); "PROP. VII. The endeavour, wherewith everything endeavours to persist in its own being, is nothing else but the actual essence of the thing in question." (III P7).
  Nadler (§2.4): "This conatus, a kind of existential inertia, constitutes the “essence” of any being." "Affects that are actions are changes in this power that have their source (or adequate cause) in our nature alone; affects that are passions are those changes in this power that originate at least partly outside of us."
  Against treating humans as exempt from nature, Spinoza's preface to Part III
  says of other writers: "They appear to conceive man to be situated in nature as a kingdom within a kingdom: for they believe that he disturbs rather than follows nature's order, that he has absolute control over his actions, and that he is determined solely by himself." (III Pref).
- **Bondage to the passions** (*Ethics* IV, "Of Human Bondage, or the
  Strength of the Emotions"). "Human infirmity in moderating and checking the emotions I name bondage: for, when a man is a prey to his emotions, he is not his own master, but lies at the mercy of fortune: so much so, that he is often compelled, while seeing that which is better for him, to follow that which is worse." (IV Pref).
- **Self-preservation as the foundation of virtue.** "As reason makes no demands contrary to nature, it demands, that every man should love himself, should seek that which is useful to him--I mean, that which is really useful to him, should desire everything which really brings man to greater perfection, and should, each for himself, endeavour as far as he can to preserve his own being." (IV P18 S);
  "it follows, first, that the foundation of virtue is the endeavour to preserve one's own being, and that happiness consists in man's power of preserving his own being;" (IV P18 S); "PROP. XX. The more every man endeavours, and is able to seek what is useful to him--in other words, to preserve his own being--the more is he endowed with virtue; on the contrary, in proportion as a man neglects to seek what is useful to him, that is, to preserve his own being, he is wanting in power." (IV P20).
  On others: "Therefore, to man there is nothing more useful than man--nothing, I repeat, more excellent for preserving their being can be wished for by men, than that all should so in all points agree, that the minds and bodies of all should form, as it were, one single mind and one single body, and that all should, with one consent, as far as they are able, endeavour to preserve their being, and all with one consent seek what is useful to them all. Hence, men who are governed by reason--that is, who seek what is useful to them in accordance with reason, desire for themselves nothing, which they do not also desire for the rest of mankind, and, consequently, are just, faithful, and honourable in their conduct." (IV P18 S); "PROP. XXXVII. The good which every man, who follows after virtue, desires for himself he will also desire for other men, and so much the more, in proportion as he has a greater knowledge of God." (IV P37).
- **The free man and blessedness.** "PROP. LXVII. A free man thinks of death least of all things; and his wisdom is a meditation not of death but of life." (IV P67); "A free man is one who lives under the guidance of reason, who is not led by fear (IV. lxiii.)" (IV P67 D).
  "PROP. XLII. Blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself; neither do we rejoice therein, because we control our lusts, but, contrariwise, because we rejoice therein, we are able to control our lusts." (V P42). The work ends: "But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare." (V P42 S).
  Nadler (§2.5): "Spinoza’s “free person” is one who bears the gifts and losses of fortune with equanimity and does only those things that he believes to be “the most important in life”." "The free person neither hopes for any eternal, otherworldly rewards nor fears any eternal punishments. He knows that the soul is not immortal in any personal sense, but is endowed only with a certain kind of eternity."

### Egoist or not: readings side by side

None is ranked here; each is its author's reading of the same texts.

| Reading | Who | Textual basis cited | Statement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychological and ethical egoist | Nadler §2.5 | IV P18 S, P20 (dictates of reason) | "Spinoza is a psychological and ethical egoist. All beings naturally seek their own advantage—to preserve their own being and increase their power—and it is right for them do so. This is what virtue consists in." |
| Egoist whose free person acts altruistically, from egoistic motives | Nadler §2.5 | free-man propositions | "He also, despite the fundamental egoism, engages in behavior toward others that is typically regarded as “ethical”, even altruistic." | Orthodox egoist | LeBuffe §1.3 names Delahunty (1985, p. 221) as "perhaps" holding it | III P9, I Appendix, IV P8 D | "IIIp9 might be supposed to support a very strong version of psychological egoism, orthodox egoism (perhaps Delahunty, 221, holds this view). ### God or Nature: pantheist, not pantheist, atheist

"For centuries, Spinoza has been regarded—by his enemies and his partisans, in the scholarly literature and the popular imagination—as a “pantheist”." (Nadler §2.1). Both camps, as Nadler reports them:
"Thus, it has been argued that Spinoza is not a pantheist, because God is to be identified only with substance and its attributes, the most universal, active causal principles of Nature, and not with any modes of substance. Other scholars have argued that Spinoza is a pantheist, just because he does identify God with the whole of nature." (§2.1). Nadler's own view: "Reductive pantheism and atheism maintain extensionally equivalent ontologies." "And however one reads the relationship between God and Nature in Spinoza, it is a mistake to call him a pantheist in so far as pantheism is still a kind of religious theism."
(§2.1). Nadler also notes: "It is an ambiguous phrase, since Spinoza could be read as trying either to divinize nature or to naturalize God." (§2.1).

## Works

- Critical exposition of Descartes's *Principles of Philosophy*, completed 1663; the only work published under his name in
  his lifetime (Nadler §1).
- *Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect* and *Short Treatise on God,
  Man and His Well-Being*, worked on at Rijnsburg (Nadler §1).
- *Theological-Political Treatise* (*Tractatus Theologico-Politicus*, TTP),
  anonymous, 1670.
- *Ethics* (*Ethica*), in the posthumous *Opera* (1677); five parts, the Elwes titles of Parts I, IV and V being "CONCERNING GOD" (capitals in Elwes),
  "Of Human Bondage, or the Strength of the Emotions" and "Of the Power of the
  Understanding, or of Human Freedom". Elwes translation,
  [Gutenberg #3800](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800). Scholarly
  editions listed by Nadler: Gebhardt, *Spinoza Opera* (1925); Curley,
  *The Collected Writings of Spinoza* (1985, 2016).
- *Political Treatise* (*Tractatus Politicus*, TP), unfinished at his death.

## In dialogue with

- **[René Descartes](descartes.md)** — Nadler (preamble): "His thought combines a commitment to a number of Cartesian metaphysical and epistemological principles with elements from ancient Stoicism, Hobbes, and medieval Jewish rationalism into a nonetheless highly original system."
  Spinoza on Descartes: "I do not forget, that the illustrious Descartes, though he believed, that the mind has absolute power over its actions, strove to explain human emotions by their primary causes, and, at the same time, to point out a way, by which the mind might attain to absolute dominion over them. However, in my opinion, he accomplishes nothing beyond a display of the acuteness of his own great intellect, as I will show in the proper place." (III Pref), and on the pineal gland:
  "To this opinion Descartes not a little inclines. For he maintained, that the soul or mind is specially united to a particular part of the brain, namely, to that part called the pineal gland," (V Pref). Nadler (§2.4): "Descartes, for example, believed that if the freedom of the human being is to be preserved, the soul must be exempt from the kind of deterministic laws that rule over the material universe." On mind and
  body, Nadler places Spinoza's denial of two substances against Descartes's
  dualism (§2.2; see [dualism](../positions/dualism.md)).
- **The [Stoics](../schools/stoicism.md)** — Spinoza: "Yet the Stoics have thought, that the emotions depended absolutely on our will, and that we could absolutely govern them." (V Pref).
  Nadler (§2.5): "Spinoza’s ethical theory is, to a certain degree, Stoic, and recalls the doctrines of thinkers such as Cicero and Seneca:" (followed by a IV Appendix quotation).
- **Maimonides** — "Taking his cue from Maimonides’s view of human eudaimonia, Spinoza argues that the mind’s intellectual love of God is our understanding of the universe, our virtue, our happiness, our well-being and our “salvation”." (Nadler §2.5).
- **Thomas Hobbes** — "Hobbes’ influence on Spinoza is unmistakable. We know that Spinoza read De Cive carefully and that it was among his possessions when he died in 1677." (Steinberg §1.2).
- **Pierre Bayle** — answered II P49 S on [Buridan's ass](../problems/buridans-ass.md)
  (*Dictionary*, "Buridan", rem. C).
- **G. W. Leibniz** — Newlands (§1.2.4) on how finite modes follow from God:
  "This is a notoriously difficult question facing Spinoza’s metaphysics, one that was raised by Leibniz just a year after Spinoza’s death (Leibniz 1969, 203)."

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Standing (Nadler, preamble).** "Bento (in Hebrew, Baruch; in Latin, Benedictus) Spinoza is one of the most important philosophers—and certainly the most radical—of the early modern period." "Of all the philosophers of the seventeenth century, Spinoza is among the most relevant today."
- **The herem (Nadler §1).** "Can there be any mystery as to why one of history’s boldest and most radical thinkers was sanctioned by an orthodox Jewish community?"
- **The necessity doctrine (Newlands, preamble).** "Spinoza’s views on necessity and possibility, which he claimed were the “principal foundation” of his Ethics (Ep75), have been less than well received by his readers, to put it mildly. From Spinoza’s contemporaries to our own, readers of the Ethics have denounced Spinoza’s views on modality as metaphysically confused at best, ethically nihilistic at worst."
- **As political thinker (Steinberg, preamble).** "At least in anglophone countries, Spinoza’s reputation as a political thinker is eclipsed by his reputation as a rationalist metaphysician."
- **The TTP in 1670:** a critic quoted by Nadler (§1, quoted above under
  Facts) called it "a book forged in hell by the devil himself"; Nadler
  calls the critic "overwrought".

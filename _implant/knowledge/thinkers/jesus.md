# Jesus of Nazareth (as an ethical figure)

A hub page: the texts attributed to him, what historians say can be
attributed, and who read the ethics how. Every assessment below is someone's,
named and cited ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md); branch
rules in [thinkers](index.md)). Religious claims about him (that he is the
Incarnation, that he rose from the dead) are reported only as the claims of
those who hold them; this page neither asserts nor denies them. Main
secondary sources: Jaroslav Pelikan and E. P. Sanders, "Jesus",
[*Encyclopaedia Britannica*](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jesus)
(excerpts: `raw/britannica-jesus-pelikan-sanders-sources-ethics.md`), and
Andrew Fiala, "Pacifism", [*SEP*](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/pacifism/)
(Fall 2024; excerpts: `raw/sep-pacifism-fall-2024-fiala-jesus-sermon-nonresistance-tolstoy-gandhi.md`).

`raw/kjv-gospels-sermon-on-the-mount-golden-rule-great-commandments.md`,

## Facts a claim depends on

- **Dates.** Britannica: "Jesus (born c. 6–4 bce, Bethlehem—died c. 30 ce, Jerusalem) was a religious leader revered in Christianity, one of the world’s major religions." (Introduction). On the year of death: "Sometime between 29 and 33 ce—possibly 30 ce—he went to observe Passover in Jerusalem, where his entrance, according to the Gospels, was triumphant and infused with eschatological significance. While there he was arrested, tried, and executed." The Outline of ethics gives "(7–2 BCE – 30–36 CE)". The two ranges overlap; neither source is preferred here.
- **Religious standing, as reported.** "He is regarded by most Christians as the Incarnation of God." (Britannica, Introduction). "His disciples became convinced that he rose from the dead and appeared to them. They converted others to believe in him, which eventually led to a new religion, Christianity." (Britannica, summary). Both sentences report beliefs; neither is the implant's.
- **Sources.** The sources read here name no writings by him; the teaching is known through the Gospels. Britannica: "The only substantial sources for the life and message of Jesus are the Gospels of the New Testament, the earliest of which was Mark (written 60–80 ce), followed by Matthew, Luke, and John (75–90 ce)." (Sources for the life of Jesus).
- **Which Gospels historians use for the teaching.** Britannica: "Scholars have unanimously chosen the Synoptic Gospels’ version of Jesus’ teaching." It qualifies the sources: "The Synoptic Gospels, then, are the primary sources for knowledge of the historical Jesus. They are not, however, the equivalent of an academic biography of a recent historical figure." and "Instead, the Synoptic Gospels are theological documents that provide information the authors regarded as necessary for the religious development of the Christian communities in which they worked." On the noncanonical gospels: "It is, of course, possible or even likely that individual sayings in Thomas or other apocryphal gospels originated with Jesus, but it is unlikely that noncanonical sources can contribute much to the portrait of the historical Jesus." These are the encyclopedia authors' assessments.
- **Setting.** Britannica places the legal teaching inside the Judaism of the time: "In general, the legal disputes in the Gospels fall within the parameters of those of 1st-century Judaism." (The relation of Jesus' teaching to the Jewish law).

## Problems addressed

- **[Can war ever be justified?](../problems/just-war.md)** — not by him in
  those terms, but his sayings are the texts both sides of the Christian
  debate read. Fiala (SEP §5.2): "For Christian thinkers, one of the primary problems is trying to reconcile the pacific commandments of Jesus with the apparent moral necessity of using war to defend the innocent. This problem is acute for Christians since Jesus seems to advocate an ethic of nonviolence both in the Sermon on the Mount and in his submission to violence, while Augustine and others use both Biblical sources (such as Paul’s letter to the Romans) and natural law to argue in favor of the just war idea."
  - *The texts.* "Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: 5:39 But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:38–39). "Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword." (Matthew 26:52).
  - *Pacifist reading.* Fiala (§2.1): "In the West, absolute pacifism is often derived from the Christian ideal of nonresistance to evil as required by Jesus’ pronouncements about nonresistance in the Sermon on the Mount (in Matthew) or the Sermon on the Plain (in Luke)." (§5.1): "In Christian pacifism, it is God’s commandments as articulated by Jesus that necessitate a commitment to pacifism. Of course, this is a contentious point; and some deny that Christianity requires pacifism (see Fiala 2007)."
  - *Just-war reading.* Aquinas states Matthew 26:52 as the first objection to the lawfulness of war, "Therefore all wars are unlawful", and answers it with his three conditions (*ST* II-II q. 40 a. 1, obj. 1; `raw/aquinas-st-2-2-q40-a1-and-augustine-contra-faustum-22-74-city-of-god-19-7-war.md`). Fiala (§6.2) reports the objection to Christian pacifism: "From this standpoint, defenders of the idea of a just war argue that love of the neighbor and the need for a just social order will occasionally necessitate just wars."
  - *Reply, as reported.* "Christian pacifists will reply to the “bad theology” objection by focusing on the message of Jesus in the Gospels; and they will argue, as John Howard Yoder does, that just war theory is a later development of a lapsed sort of “Constantinian” (or Augustinian) Christianity." (Fiala §6.2).
- **The Golden Rule and love of the neighbour** (no problem page yet). "Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets." (Matthew 7:12). "And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise." (Luke 6:31). The two commandments, asked "Which is the first commandment of all?": "The first of all the commandments is, Hear, O Israel; The Lord our God is one Lord: 12:30 And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind, and with all thy strength: this is the first commandment. 12:31 And the second is like, namely this, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. There is none other commandment greater than these." (Mark 12:29–31; parallels Matthew 22:37–40, Luke 10:27). Matthew adds: "On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets." (22:40).
- **Love of enemies** (no problem page yet). "Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy. 5:44 But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you;" (Matthew 5:43–44; Luke 6:27–28). The stated ground follows: "for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust." (5:45).
- **Inward law and perfection** (no problem page yet). The antitheses extend prohibitions from act to anger: "But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment" (Matthew 5:22). The section closes: "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect." (5:48). Britannica's reading: "According to Matthew 5:21–26 and 5:27–30, Jesus also held that observance of the law should be not only external but internal: hatred and lust, as well as murder and adultery, are wrong." and "The Jesus of Matthew in particular is a moral perfectionist (5:17–48)." On whether the demands were meant as requirements, the encyclopedia reports one view and prefers another: "The impossibility of being perfect during a full lifetime leads some modern interpreters to propose that Jesus intended these admonitions to be only an ideal, not a requirement. It is more likely, however, that Jesus the eschatological prophet regarded perfection as quite possible during the short period before the arrival of the Son of Man." (Ethics). The first sentence names no interpreters; the second is the authors' own judgment.

## Works

The sources read here record no writings by him; the texts are the
Gospels' reports. Works pages are not yet written ([works](../works/index.md)).

- **Sermon on the Mount**, Matthew 5–7. Its blessings open: "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." (5:3); "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God." (5:9). Britannica: "According to one set, especially prominent in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), Jesus admonished his followers to observe the law unwaveringly (Matthew 5:17–48)." The KJV text of 5:17: "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil."
- **Sermon on the Plain**, Luke 6 (Fiala's name, SEP §2.1; he cites Luke 6.27–30): love of enemies and the Golden Rule at 6:27–31; Britannica cites Luke 6:20 for the Beatitudes.
- **The great commandments**: Mark 12:28–31; Matthew 22:37–40; Luke 10:27 (KJV text of each held).
- **Sayings on judgment and on "the least"**: "Judge not, that ye be not judged." (Matthew 7:1); "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me." (Matthew 25:40). Britannica cites Matthew 25:34–40 for "Perhaps faith in God and treating other people with love (Matthew 25:34–40) would suffice for entry into the kingdom."
- Translation: King James Version, 1611, as distributed by Project Gutenberg eBook #10 ([gutenberg.org/ebooks/10](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10)); the verse numbers are that text's.

## In dialogue with

- **The Law and the prophets.** The teaching is framed as fulfilment of the Jewish law (Matthew 5:17, above) and quotes it: the first commandment begins "Hear, O Israel" (Mark 12:29). Britannica: "Jesus advocated ethical purity. He demanded complete devotion to God, putting it ahead of devotion to self and even to family (Mark 3:31–35; Matthew 10:35–37), and taught that people should give up everything in order to obtain what was most precious (Matthew 13:44–46)."
- **[Augustine](augustine.md)** — named by Fiala (§5.2, above) among those who "use both Biblical sources (such as Paul’s letter to the Romans) and natural law to argue in favor of the just war idea"; Yoder's term for the tradition, per Fiala, is "“Constantinian” (or Augustinian) Christianity".
- **[Thomas Aquinas](aquinas.md)** — answers Matthew 26:52 in *ST* II-II q. 40 a. 1 (above).
- **Immanuel Kant** (no page yet) — on the love commandment, *Groundwork* (1785), First Section, Abbott tr.: "It is in this manner, undoubtedly, that we are to understand those passages of Scripture also in which we are commanded to love our neighbour, even our enemy. For love, as an affection, cannot be commanded, but beneficence for duty's sake may" — "and it is this love alone which can be commanded." On the negative rule ("quod tibi non vis fieri", what you do not wish done to you), Second Section footnote: "it cannot be a universal law, for it does not contain the principle of duties to oneself, nor of the duties of benevolence to others". Excerpts: `raw/kant-mill-tolstoy-on-love-commandment-golden-rule-nonresistance.md`.
- **John Stuart Mill** (no page yet) — *Utilitarianism* (7th ed. 1879), ch. II: "In the golden rule of Jesus of Nazareth, we read the complete spirit of the ethics of utility. To do as one would be done by, and to love one's neighbour as oneself, constitute the ideal perfection of utilitarian morality." (same raw file).
- **Søren Kierkegaard** (no page yet) — *Works of Love* (1847), as Lippitt and Evans report it (SEP "Søren Kierkegaard", Fall 2024, §3.3.2; `raw/kierkegaard-nietzsche-on-jesus-sep-lippitt-evans-leiter-antichrist-mencken.md`): "A central issue in this text is the love that is at stake in the command to love our neighbors as ourselves." "“The neighbor” includes everyone, and we are to strive to love all—including our enemies. This is so counter-intuitive to the “natural” human, Kierkegaard claims, that it needs to be commanded (WL 24–5, SKS 9, 32–3)." "Furthermore, the duty to love must be grounded in divine authority; no human authority is sufficient."
- **Leo Tolstoy** (no page yet) — *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (Garnett tr., 1894), ch. II: "In my book I made it an accusation against the teachers of the Church that their teaching is opposed to Christ's commands clearly and definitely expressed in the Sermon on the Mount, and opposed in especial to his command in regard to resistance to evil, and that in this way they deprive Christ's teaching of all value." Fiala (§1.1): "Nonetheless, some pacifists, such as Tolstoy, do appear to advocate nonresistance. Tolstoy said, for example, “the only means of deliverance from violence is the submissive and peaceful endurance of it” (Tolstoy 1906, 36; see Christoyannopoulos 2020)." A narrower reading of the same verse, from Adin Ballou's catechism as Tolstoy reprints it (ch. I): "it ought to be taken in the exact sense of our Saviour's teaching--that is, not repaying evil for evil. We ought to oppose evil by every righteous means in our power, but not by evil."
- **Friedrich Nietzsche** (no page yet) — *The Antichrist* (written 1888, printed 1895; Mencken tr. 1918), §29: "the very incapacity for resistance is here converted into something moral: ("resist not evil!"--the most profound sentence in the Gospels, perhaps the true key to them), to wit, the blessedness of peace, of gentleness, the inability to be an enemy." §35: "This "bearer of glad tidings" died as he lived and taught--not to "save mankind," but to show mankind how to live." §39: "The very word "Christianity" is a misunderstanding--at bottom there was only one Christian, and he died on the cross." Leiter (SEP "Nietzsche's Moral and Political Philosophy", Fall 2024, §1.3) on the critique of Christian morality: "This point is even more explicit in The Antichrist, where Nietzsche notes that Christian morality “has waged deadly war against this higher type of man; it has placed all the basic instincts of his type under ban” (5, emphasis added)."
- **Mohandas Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.** (no pages yet) — Fiala (§5.2): "Gandhi was inspired by Thoreau and by Tolstoy; and Gandhi in turn inspired Western pacifists such as Albert Einstein, Bertrand Russell, and Martin Luther King Jr. Gandhi and King both claim that one of the most important ideas underlying this sort of pacifism is love, especially the disinterested brotherly love that is described in the Greek New Testament using the word agape." Gandhi's own texts on Jesus are not read here.

## Reception

Assessments, each with its owner; readings that conflict are listed side by
side, unranked.

- **Historians on what is his.** Britannica's authors prefer the Synoptic teaching ("Scholars have unanimously chosen the Synoptic Gospels’ version of Jesus’ teaching.") and read the ethics through the expected kingdom: "This fits quite well with the proclamation of the eschatological kingdom of God because Jesus believed, as fellow moral perfectionist Paul did, that divine intervention was near at hand, and therefore people had to be “blameless” for only a short time (1 Thessalonians 5:23)." On his audience: "The Beatitudes, drawn from the Sermon on the Mount, particularly stress Jesus’ concern for the poor and the meek who will be blessed (Luke 6:20; Matthew 5:3–5)." Other historians' reconstructions are not read here and are left out.
- **Pacifist readings.** Fiala (§2.5): "For example, Jesus articulated a revaluation of values from the standpoint of a more pacific understanding of God’s commandments. Instead of retaliation and the logic of “an eye for eye,” Jesus claimed that we must evolve a new idea—of not returning evil for evil." (§5.1): "In the Christian tradition this is understood as a project in which human beings learn to imitate Jesus in order to become closer to God. The Christian model of virtue is Jesus, and Jesus’ practice of nonviolence culminated in his crucifixion." Tolstoy, Ballou, Gandhi and King are under *In dialogue with*.
- **Against the pacifist reading.** Fiala (§6.2) states the objection: "A version of this objection that is often directed against Christian pacifism holds that war will remain necessary because of the fallen and sinful nature of human beings. This objection also reminds Christian pacifists that there is explicit advocacy for war in the Old Testament and that Paul’s letter to the Romans allows the sovereign to use the sword to execute God’s wrath." Aquinas's answer to Matthew 26:52 is under *Problems addressed*.
- **Moral philosophers on the Golden Rule and the love commandment.** Mill reads the Golden Rule as "the complete spirit of the ethics of utility"; Kant reads the love command as "practical love" that "can be commanded" and denies that the negative rule can be "a universal law"; Kierkegaard, per Lippitt and Evans, holds that neighbour-love "needs to be commanded" and that "the duty to love must be grounded in divine authority". These are three readings, cited above, not ranked.
- **Nietzsche's contrast.** Nietzsche calls "resist not evil!" "the most profound sentence in the Gospels" (§29, quoted above), and in §39 sets the life "lived by him who died on the cross" against later Christian faith: "It is an error amounting to nonsensicality to see in "faith," and particularly in faith in salvation through Christ, the distinguishing mark of the Christian: only the Christian way of life, the life lived by him who died on the cross, is Christian...." Leiter (§1.1) on the reach of his critique: "Thus, it will not suffice to say that he simply attacks Christian or Kantian or European or utilitarian morality — though he certainly at times attacks all of these."
- **Kierkegaard on faith and enemies.** Lippitt and Evans (§3.3.1): "In these writings the barriers to faith are seen as pride and selfishness, since faith in Christ requires a radical willingness to love the neighbor, a category which even includes one’s enemies."

## Vocabulary

- *Golden Rule* — the name Mill uses ("the golden rule of Jesus of Nazareth") for Matthew 7:12 / Luke 6:31; Kant's negative form is "quod tibi non vis fieri". No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Non-resistance* / *nonresistance* — Tolstoy's and Ballou's term for the reading of Matthew 5:39 (Ballou: "not repaying evil for evil"); Fiala contrasts it with pacifism as active nonviolent resistance (§1.1). No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Agape* — "the disinterested brotherly love that is described in the Greek New Testament using the word agape" (Fiala §5.2); Lippitt and Evans render Kierkegaard's *Kjerlighed* as "neighbor-love" and "a form of agape" (SEP §3.3.2). No vocabulary entry yet.
- *Kingdom of God / kingdom of heaven* — the eschatological frame Britannica reads the ethics in (Ethics section). No vocabulary entry yet.

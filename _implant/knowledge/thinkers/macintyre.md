## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and education.** "Alasdair MacIntyre was born January 12, 1929 in Glasgow, Scotland." (Lutz §1). "He earned a bachelor’s degree in classics from Queen Mary College in the University of London in the city’s East End in 1949. MacIntyre attended graduate school at Manchester University, a provincial “red brick” university in the North West of England, earning his MA in Philosophy in 1951." (§1). The Notre Dame obituary adds a master's degree from Oxford: "earned master’s degrees from the University of Manchester and the University of Oxford." (`raw/notre-dame-2025-macintyre-obituary.md`).
- **Posts.** "MacIntyre began his teaching career at the University of Manchester as a Lecturer in the Philosophy of Religion in 1951, and held that post until 1957." (Lutz §1). Lutz dates the move to the United States "1970": "before emigrating from Britain to the United States in 1970, where his research interests drew him to teaching posts at Brandeis, Boston University, Vanderbilt, Notre Dame, and Duke. MacIntyre returned to Notre Dame in 2000 as the Senior Research Professor in the Notre Dame Center for Ethics and Culture until his retirement in 2010." (§1). The obituary dates it differently: "He moved to the United States in the late 1960s" and "He joined the faculty of Notre Dame in 1985 and was granted emeritus status in 2010."
- **Death.** "died on May 21, 2025. He was 96." (Notre Dame obituary, 23 May 2025).
- **Phases** (as Lutz reports them, §1). "MacIntyre began his career as a Marxist Protestant Christian philosopher of religion, basing his work on the fideism of Karl Barth and Wittgenstein’s concept of a form of life (interview with Giovanna Borradori, p. 257). By 1960 he had stopped writing on that subject, and he wrote as an atheist through the sixties and seventies." Then: "During the years 1977 through 1984 MacIntyre transitioned to an Aristotelian worldview, returned to the Christian faith and turned from Aristotle to Thomas Aquinas."
- **His own account of the Thomist turn** (2007): "When I wrote After Virtue, I was already an Aristotelian, but not yet a Thomist," (Prologue to the Third Edition).

## Problems addressed

- **The state of moral language: the "disquieting suggestion"** (*After Virtue*, ch. 1). The book opens: "Imagine that the natural sciences were to suffer the effects of a catastrophe." After describing a world in which science survives only in fragments, MacIntyre states: "The hypothesis which I wish to advance is that in the actual world which we inhabit the language of morality is in the same state of grave disorder as the language of natural science in the imaginary world which I described." and "What we possess, if this view is true, are the fragments of a conceptual scheme, parts which now lack those contexts from which their significance derived." The aim: "It is the aim of this book to make that thought available to radicals, liberals and conservatives alike." (ch. 1).
- **Emotivism.** Lutz (§3.b.i.3) traces the topic to the 1951 MA thesis: "MacIntyre criticized the reductivism of Stevenson’s conclusions in his MA thesis, but MacIntyre did not criticize Stevenson’s rejection of Moore." In *After Virtue*, Lutz reports (§4.a.i): "MacIntyre reinterprets “emotivism,” Stevenson’s “false theory of meaning” as a “cogent theory of use,” and he names the culture that uses moral rhetoric pragmatically and syncretically “the culture of emotivism.”"
- **The Enlightenment project.** Chapters 4 and 5 of *After Virtue* are titled "4. The Predecessor Culture and the Enlightenment Project of Justifying Morality" and "5. Why the Enlightenment Project of Justifying Morality Had to Fail" (contents, 3rd ed.). Lutz's summary (§4.a.i): "Modern moral philosophy had thus set for itself an incoherent goal. It was to vindicate both the moral autonomy of the individual and the objectivity, necessity, and categorical character of the rules of morality (AV, p. 62)." In 2007 MacIntyre restated the outcome: "But what those philosophers in fact provided were several rival and incompatible accounts, utilitarians competing with Kantians and both with contractarians," (Prologue, p. viii).
- **Nietzsche or Aristotle.** Chapter 9 is titled "9. Nietzsche or Aristotle?". Lutz (§4.a.i) states the alternative as MacIntyre frames it: "Either we follow Nietzsche and defend the autonomy of the individual against the arbitrary demands of conventional moral reasoning, or we reject both moral autonomy and arbitrary conventional moral reasoning to follow Aristotle".
- **Virtue: practices, narrative, tradition** (*After Virtue*, chs. 14–15). The first-level definition, as Lutz quotes it: "“A virtue is an acquired human quality the possession and exercise of which tends to enable us to achieve those goods which are internal to practices and the lack of which effectively prevents us from achieving any such goods” (AV, p. 191)." (§4.a.ii). "MacIntyre therefore adds a second level to his definition of virtue." — the quest for the good of a whole life; then a third, social level, tradition: "“I am born with a past, and to cut myself off from that past in the individualist mode, is to deform my present relationships” (p. 221)." and "This third, social, level completes MacIntyre’s account of the excellence of the human agent in AV." (§4.a.ii). The book ends, in Lutz's paraphrase (§4.a.iii): "To wait “for another—doubtless very different—St. Benedict,” is to await a person who can unify communities that encourage moral formation in judgment and action."
- **Rationality and tradition; relativism** (*Whose Justice? Which Rationality?*, 1988). As Lutz quotes it (§4.b): "So rationality itself, whether theoretical or practical, is a concept with a history: indeed, since there are also a diversity of traditions of enquiry, with histories, there are, so it will turn out, rationalities rather than rationality, just as it will also turn out that there are justices rather than justice (WJWR, p. 9)." and "“There is no standing ground, no place for enquiry . . . apart from that which is provided by some particular tradition or other” (p. 350)." (§4.b.i). Gowans (SEP "Moral Relativism", §5): "For example, Alasdair MacIntyre (1988: ch. 18 and 1994) has argued that, in some circumstances, it is possible to realize, through an exercise in imagination, that a conflicting and incommensurable moral tradition is rationally superior to one’s own tradition." MacIntyre (2007) says the claims of rival traditions "presuppose the falsity of relativism." (Prologue).
- **Three versions of moral enquiry** (1990). "MacIntyre presented his theory of rationality again in his 1988 Gifford Lectures, published as Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry (1990)." (Lutz §4.b.ii), with three exemplars: "The Ninth Edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica (1875–1889) represents the modern tradition of trying to understand the world objectively without the influence of tradition. The Genealogy of Morals (1887), by Friedrich Nietzsche embodies the post-modern tradition of interpreting all traditions as arbitrary impositions of power. The encyclical letter Aeterni Patris (1879) of Pope Leo XIII exemplifies the approach of acknowledging one’s predecessors within one’s own tradition of enquiry and working to advance or improve that tradition in the pursuit of objective truth." Of genealogy: "MacIntyre finds this mode of enquiry incoherent." (§4.b.ii).
- **Dependence and the virtues** (1999). "MacIntyre’s Carus Lectures, Dependent Rational Animals: Why Human Beings Need the Virtues (1999), put MacIntyre’s theory of rationality into practice to examine the conditions of human action and to argue that the virtues are essential to the practice of independent practical reason." (Lutz §4.c), starting from the claim, as quoted: "“Human identity is primarily . . . bodily and therefore animal identity and it is by reference to that identity that the continuities of our relationships to others are partly defined” (DRA, p. 8)."
- **[Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?](../problems/moral-dilemmas.md)** and **[lying to the murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md).** Lutz (§4.e) on *Ethics and Politics* (2006): "Essays in the second part investigate the apparent problems of moral dilemmas and the real difficulties of determining whether and when it may be more reasonable to deceive or to lie than to tell the truth." Mahon (SEP "The Definition of Lying and Deception", §2.3) cites MacIntyre on Aquinas among the absolutist positions on lying to a would-be murderer: "(Augustine 1952; Aquinas 1972 (cf.  MacIntyre 1995b); Kant 1996 (cf. Mahon 2006);" (full sentence in `raw/sep-lying-definition-fall-2024-murderer-and-kant.md`; the problem page quotes it under Positions taken). Neither MacIntyre text was read here.

## In dialogue with

- **[Aristotle](aristotle.md)** and **[Thomas Aquinas](aquinas.md)** (earlier). MacIntyre (2007): "When I wrote After Virtue, I was already an Aristotelian, but not yet a Thomist," Pasnau (SEP "Thomas Aquinas", §9) lists him among twentieth-century Thomists: "the twentieth century witnessed a flourishing of Thomisms of all kinds, in scholars like Jacques Maritain, Etienne Gilson, Elizabeth Anscombe, and Alasdair MacIntyre." (`raw/sep-aquinas-fall-2024-life-works-natural-law-influence.md`).
- **[Friedrich Nietzsche](nietzsche.md)** (earlier). The alternative of *After Virtue* ch. 9 and ch. 18 (above). Leiter (SEP "Nietzsche's Moral and Political Philosophy", §3.2) names MacIntyre as a holder of one reading of Nietzsche's metaethics: "(This, in fact, is the most familiar reading outside the secondary literature on Nietzsche; one finds this view of Nietzsche’s metaethics, for example, in the sociologist Max Weber and the moral philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre, among many others.)" (`raw/sep-nietzsche-moral-political-fall-2024-leiter-critique-antirealism-politics.md`).
- **[Søren Kierkegaard](kierkegaard.md)** (earlier). Lippitt & Evans (SEP "Søren Kierkegaard", §3.2): "A related debate has arisen from Alasdair MacIntyre’s claim, in his A Short History of Ethics and After Virtue, that the choice between the aesthetic and the ethical is arbitrary or “criterionless”, (MacIntyre 1981 [1984])." and "MacIntyre subsequently amended his position, while nevertheless re-stating the “criterionless choice” charge in a different form (see MacIntyre 2001, esp. 344)." (`raw/sep-kierkegaard-fall-2024-life-pseudonyms-spheres-fear-and-trembling.md`).
- **[Carl Gustav Jung](jung.md)** (contemporary, as subject). MacIntyre's 1967 encyclopedia entry judges: "At the root of the problem lies an ambiguous set of ontological claims." (`raw/macintyre-1967-encyclopedia-of-philosophy-jung.md`, "Criticism").
- **[R. M. Hare](hare.md)** (contemporary). Lutz (§3.b.i.3) gives Hare's claim: "A central claim of Hare’s The Language of Morals (1952), renewed in Freedom and Reason (1963), is that moral judgments are descriptive—not merely emotive—because they are both universalizable and prescriptive." and MacIntyre's 1957 reply: "MacIntyre lists six kinds of moral valuations that are neither universalizable nor prescriptive and concludes that the theory of universal prescriptivism is inadequate for the same reason that emotivism is inadequate; it is reductive."
- **[Karl Barth](barth.md)** (earlier). The early fideism drew on "the fideism of Karl Barth and Wittgenstein’s concept of a form of life" (Lutz §1).
- **[John Rawls](rawls.md)** (contemporary). Bell (SEP "Communitarianism", preamble) lists MacIntyre among critics of Rawls: "Drawing primarily upon the insights of Aristotle and Hegel, political philosophers such as Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Sandel, Charles Taylor and Michael Walzer disputed Rawls’ assumption that the principal task of government is to secure and distribute fairly the liberties and economic resources individuals need to lead freely chosen lives." (`raw/sep-communitarianism-fall-2024-sandel-taylor-against-rawls.md`). For MacIntyre's own rejection of the label see Reception.
- **[G. E. M. Anscombe](anscombe.md)** and **[Philippa Foot](foot.md)** (contemporaries, grouped by encyclopedists). Hursthouse & Pettigrove (SEP "Virtue Ethics", §3): "Following in the footsteps of Anscombe (1958) and MacIntyre (1985), Talbot Brewer (2009) argues that to work with the categories of rightness and wrongness is already to get off on the wrong foot." The same entry (§3): "But McDowell, Foot, MacIntyre and Hursthouse have all outlined versions of a third way between these two extremes." (`raw/sep-philippa-foot-fall-2024-life-applied-ethics-natural-goodness.md`).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's. Critics and
replies are set side by side.

- **Standing.** Lutz (§1): "His most important book, After Virtue (hereafter AV, 1981), has been called one of the most influential works of moral philosophy of the late 20th century." The Notre Dame obituary: "Widely regarded as the most important figure in modern virtue ethics,".
- **On Aristotle without metaphysics.** Critics, as Lutz reports them (§4.a.iv): "Thus one group of critics rejects MacIntyre’s Aristotelianism because they hold that any Aristotelian account of the virtues must first account for the truth about virtue in terms of Aristotle’s philosophy of nature, which MacIntyre had dismissed in AV as “metaphysical biology” (AV, pp. 162, 179)." and "Another group of critics, including materialists, dismissed MacIntyre’s attempt to recover an Aristotelian account of the virtues because they took those virtues to presuppose an indefensible metaphysical doctrine of nature."
  Defenders (§4.a.iv): "Contemporary scholars have defended MacIntyre’s unconventional Aristotelianism by challenging the conventions that MacIntyre is said to violate." Lutz names his own *Tradition in the Ethics of Alasdair MacIntyre* (2004) and Kelvin Knight's *Aristotelian Philosophy* (2007). Lutz (§5.b): "This peculiar form of Aristotelianism, which MacIntyre described in the “Postscript to the 2nd ed.” of AV as “an historicist defense of Aristotle” (AV, p. 277), remains controversial among Aristotelian and Thomistic metaphysicians."
- **On relativism.** The charge, as Lutz reports it: "For this reason, some of MacIntyre’s critics have argued that tradition-constituted rationality entails an absolute relativism in philosophy." (§4.b); "Aristotelian and Thomist critics complained, however, that MacIntyre’s Aristotelianism, which sought its foundation in teleological activity rather than teleological metaphysics, remained open to the challenge that it was relativistic." (§4.b.i). Hursthouse & Pettigrove (SEP §3) cite him for the cultural-relativity objection to virtue ethics: "Is it not the case that different cultures embody different virtues, (MacIntyre 1985) and hence that the v-rules will pick out actions as right or wrong only relative to a particular culture?"
  MacIntyre's reply (2007): rival traditions' claims "presuppose the falsity of relativism." (Prologue). Baghramian & Carter (SEP "Relativism", §4.3.1) report "Alasdair MacIntyre’s observation that relativism may have been refuted a number of times too often, whereas genuinely refutable doctrines only need to be refuted once (MacIntyre 1985: 5)." Relativists' rejoinder, as Gowans reports it (SEP §5): "They may say that the Davidsonian account cannot assure sufficient common ground to resolve conflicts between moral frameworks (or to ensure that there is really only one framework), and that MacIntyre’s approach is likely to work at best only in some cases."
- **On community.** Bell (SEP "Communitarianism", §1), critical: "In After Virtue, Alasdair MacIntyre defended the Aristotelian ideal of the intimate, reciprocating local community bound by shared ends, where people simply assume and fulfill socially given roles (MacIntyre 1984). But this pre-modern Gemeinschaft conception of an all-encompassing community that members unreflectively endorse seemed distinctly ill-suited for complex and conflict-ridden large-scale industrialized societies."
  MacIntyre (2007), on being called a communitarian: "something that I have never been. I see no value in community as such" (Prologue); and in 1999, as Lutz quotes him: "MacIntyre says, “It is . . . a mistake, the communitarian mistake, to attempt to infuse the politics of the state with the values and modes of participation in local community” (DRA, p. 142)." (§4.c). On the charge of nostalgia he writes: "I have been accused of nostalgia and of idealizing the past." and denies it (Prologue).
- **On the Kierkegaard reading.** Lippitt & Evans (SEP §3.2): "MacIntyre’s view has been much discussed since, though Kierkegaard scholars have been almost unanimous in their rejection of it." (full sentence in the Kierkegaard raw file).
- **On the label "virtue ethicist".** Lutz (§5.a): "MacIntyre is sometimes categorized as a “virtue ethicist,” and AV is counted among the principle texts in virtue ethics, but this label may be misleading for MacIntyre." (Lutz's assessment; "principle" as in the source.)

## Vocabulary

- *Practice*, *goods internal to practices*, *tradition*: technical terms of *After Virtue* chs. 14–15 (above); no vocabulary pages yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Emotivism*: for MacIntyre both a metaethical theory (Stevenson's) and, as Lutz reports, the name of a culture — "the culture of emotivism" (§4.a.i).
- *Virtue*: in the first-level definition, a quality relative to practices (AV, p. 191, as Lutz quotes it), wider in the second and third levels; not a normative definition of this implant.
- *Enquiry*: MacIntyre's spelling, used in the titles *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* and in "traditions of enquiry".

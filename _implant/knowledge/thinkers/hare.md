## Facts a claim depends on

- **Birth and name:** "Richard Mervyn Hare was born at Backwell Down, outside Bristol, on 21 March 1919." "He was to be known professionally as R.M. Hare, and personally as Dick Hare." (Price §1).
- **Education:** "He was awarded a scholarship to Balliol College in 1937, where he read two years of Greats before the outbreak of war." (§1).
- **War:** "He was finally taken prisoner when Singapore fell in February 1942." "He was eventually imprisoned with fellow officers in Singapore, whence he was released after exactly three and a half years when the War ended." (§1). Price reports the march up the River Kwai and the work on the railway from Siam into Burma (§1).
- **Balliol pupils:** "four of whom, Bernard Williams, David Pears, Richard Wollheim, and John Lucas, were to join him both as professional philosophers, and as Fellows of the British Academy." (§1). Chappell & Smyth (SEP "Bernard Williams", §1) record that Williams in 1990 "succeeded his old tutor Richard Hare as White’s Professor of Moral Philosophy at Oxford" (`raw/sep-williams-bernard-fall-2024-integrity-objection.md`).
- **Marriage:** "his marriage in 1947 to Catherine Verney" (§1).
- **Posts:** "It was still during his time there, in 1964, that he was elected a Fellow of the British Academy." "However, ineluctable promotion eventually removed him, in 1966, to the White’s Chair of Moral Philosophy at Corpus Christi." (§1).
- **Florida and applied ethics:** "All this made less improbable his early retirement from Oxford in 1983, and his appointment as Graduate Research Professor of Philosophy at the University of Florida at Gainesville." "When a Society for Applied Philosophy was formed, he became its first President." (§1).
- **Death:** "He died suddenly but peacefully on 29 January 2002." (§1).
- **Religion** (his own description, as Price reports it): "He called himself a “Christian empiricist”, but thought the question whether he was really a Christian terminological." (§8). John Hare's account, as Price quotes it: "John Hare (2002: 307) connects the inhibitions that held his father back from belief not just with modern skepticism, but with “a philosophical doctrine about meaning which he inherited from Carnap and the logical positivists”;" (§8).

## Problems addressed

- **The meaning of moral language (prescriptivism).** Price (§2): "Philosophically, Hare came under two influences that together led him to views that he could always retain. One was emotivism." "The other influence was Kantian." "The Language of Morals introduced a distinction between prescriptive and descriptive meaning." "Prescriptive meaning is defined in relation to imperatives: a statement is prescriptive if it entails, if necessary in conjunction with purely factual statements, at least one imperative; and to assent to an imperative is to prescribe action." Price adds: "Hare never said that ethical statements are imperatives;" On emotivism: "It became a recurrent theme that emotivism went wrong by connecting the meaning of moral language with its perlocutionary, rather than its illocutionary, force (1997a: 112–14)," (§2).
  van Roojen (§2.2): "By contrast current versions of prescriptivism, most developed in the works of R. M. Hare, have attempted to vindicate moral thinking as a rational enterprise." "The main idea here is that while moral sentences do in fact express a species of prescription much as ordinary imperatives do, they express prescriptions of a special universal sort." "In this way, Hare believes, calling an action wrong commits the speaker to judging wrong any relevantly similar action done at any time and any place by any person."
- **Is and ought.** Prescriptivism "at once gave a clear sense to his endorsement of Hume’s denial that one can derive an “ought” from an “is”." (Price §2). Cohon (SEP "Hume's Moral Philosophy", §5): "A number of present-day philosophers, including R. M. Hare, endorse this putative thesis of logic, calling it “Hume’s Law.”" (`raw/sep-hume-moral-fall-2024-four-theses-is-ought-readings.md`; see [Hume](hume.md)).
- **Moral judgement and motivation.** Price names "what is everyone’s first objection to prescriptivism, that, intuitively, “I ought to φ” does not entail “I will φ” (expressing intention)." (§2); he reports Hare's replies in *Freedom and Reason* (1963) and *Weakness of the Will* (1992). van Roojen (§3.3): "Hare’s most famous argument for the action-guiding character of moral judgments is the Missionaries and the Cannibals Argument." "The argument thus supports a version of moderate internalism." van Roojen's assessment: "But, insofar as Hare also suggests that accepting a command directed at oneself requires an intention to act accordingly (Hare 1952, 20), he seems committed to a closer connection between moral judgment and motivating states than the Missionaries and Cannibals Argument vindicates."
- **Supervenience.** van Roojen (§3.4): "Hare seems to have introduced the term ‘supervenience’ to the philosophical literature (Hare 1952, 145) and he suggested that his own theory, universal prescriptivism, was uniquely placed to explain it." van Roojen's assessment: "But the feature of his theory that did the explanatory work was not its non-cognitivism – rather it was that it required the judgments to be universal in the ways he specified."
- **[Ross's paradox](../problems/ross-paradox.md) and the logic of imperatives.** Price (§3): "Hare’s attempt to ground a logic of imperatives was innovative, but problematic." (Price's assessment). Among the problem cases: "(Hare discusses the following instance, “Post the letter; so post the letter or burn it”, 1967: 25–34)." The problem page reports, via Vranas (2009), Hare's 1967 reply that permissive presuppositions are conversational implicatures.
  On embedding: "The objection was made strongly by Peter Geach in an article (1965) citing Gottlob Frege; so it has come to be known as the “Frege-Geach” objection." "Hare’s response is to be found in Hare (1970). His solution to the problem as it arises with conditionals derives from Gilbert Ryle’s notion of “inference tickets” (1950)." (§3).
- **Universalizability and the Golden-Rule argument.** "The two features of prescriptivity and universalizability remained the twin pillars of Hare’s theory ever afterwards." (Price §4; the paper *Universalisability*, 1954/5). "The next important development came in a second book, Freedom and Reason (1963), in which the formal features of prescriptivity and universalizability generate a “Golden Rule” form of argument" — illustrated by the debtors' case (1963: 90–1): "suppose that A owes money to B, who owes money to C, and that the law allows creditors to exact their debts by putting their debtors into prison." (§4).
  The exception: "In Freedom and Reason, Hare allows the argument to be evaded by the “fanatic” who is so committed to some impersonal ideal (say that debtors deserve a hard time) that he is willing to disregard his own personal interests" (§4); Price reports a later tightening (first set out fully in 1972c) meant to close this off.
- **Preference utilitarianism from universal prescribing** (*Moral Thinking*, 1981). Price (§4): "The emergent ethical theory is a distinctive variety of utilitarianism, one that identifies the moral good with the maximization not of some subjective state such as happiness, but of the satisfaction of preferences." In the 1981 form: "Hence, the speaker can rationally assent to a particular “ought”-statement only if it is derivable from some universal principle that he will accept if he gives impartial and positive weight to all preferences whose satisfaction would be affected by its observance." "Thus moral reflection generates a universalized prudence." Freedom remains: "Human decision remains free, however rational and informed, because anyone can avoid the constraints of morality by declining to moralize; for this reason, it remains true that no “is” entails an “ought”." Johnson & Cureton (SEP "Kant's Moral Philosophy", §14) on his Kant: "Several 20th century theorists have followed Mill’s suggestion, most notably, R. M. Hare." and "Thus, in his view, the CI is “simply utilitarianism put into other words” (1993, p. 103)." (`raw/sep-kant-moral-fall-2024-equivalence-teleological-hare.md`, `raw/sep-kant-moral-fall-2024-good-will-formulas-autonomy-readings.md`; see [Kant](kant.md)).
- **Two levels of moral thinking.** Price (§7): the theory, "first presented (in different terminology) in “Ethical Theory and Utilitarianism” (1976), and fully explored in Moral Thinking (1981)," "is a distinction between a “critical” level of thinking, conducted by “archangels” with the use of “Golden Rule” arguments, and an “intuitive” level, conducted by “proles” with the use of simple principles (often articulating emotional responses) whose acceptance can be justified at the critical level." "These two levels define not two social castes, but two roles between which each of us learns to alternate as appropriate." Hare's own image, as Price quotes it (1981: 52): "To say that it is impossible to keep critical and intuitive thinking going in the same thought-process is like saying that in a battle a commander cannot at the same time be thinking of the details of tactics, the overall aim of victory, and the principles (economy of force, concentration of force, offensive action, etc.) which he has learnt when learning his trade."
- **[Justification of punishment](../problems/justification-of-punishment.md).** Price (§7) names the sheriff case — "as when an American sheriff might judicially execute one suspect in order to prevent a mass lynching of others." — and "He could now hope to accommodate these at the “intuitive” level of thinking." Duff & Hoskins (SEP "Legal Punishment", §3) cite "Rawls 1955; Hare 1981, chs. 3, 9.7" for the reply that "thus we, and especially our penal officials, will do best if we think and act as if such punishments are intrinsically wrong and unjustifiable" (`raw/sep-legal-punishment-fall-2024-justification-map.md`).
- **[The demandingness objection](../problems/demandingness-objection.md).** Hurka (SEP "Moral Demands and Permissions/Prerogatives", §2) lists Hare 1981 for "“indirect” or “two-level” consequentialism, which distinguishes what’s true—the consequentialist principle—from what’s in practice the best way of deciding how to act." (`raw/sep-moral-demands-permissions-fall-2024-demandingness-objection.md`).
- **[The paradox of hedonism](../problems/paradox-of-hedonism.md).** Dietz (2021, n. 16) lists Hare (1981) among those who deny the acceptability requirement for consequentialist theories (`raw/dietz-2021-how-to-use-the-paradox-of-hedonism.md`; see the problem page).
- **Possible people and [the repugnant conclusion](../problems/repugnant-conclusion.md).** Price (§6): "Hare reasons that, if I am glad that I exist, I tenselessly prescribe, ceteris paribus, that my parents bring me into existence; universalizing the prescription, I must prescribe, ceteris paribus, the bringing into existence of others relevantly like me (1975, 1988b, 1988c)." "A positive answer has implications—though not, Hare argued, very radical ones—for population policy, and the morality of such practices as abortion and IVF." Arrhenius, Ryberg & Tännsjö (SEP, §2.8) list "Hare 1993" (R. Hare, *Possible People*, in *Essays on Bioethics*, per their bibliography) among those for whom "the source of the unacceptability of the conclusion is the fact that we are deluded by our intuition" (`raw/sep-repugnant-conclusion-fall-2024-mere-addition-and-responses.md`).
- **[The Heinz dilemma](../problems/heinz-dilemma.md).** LeBar (SEP "Justice as a Virtue", §2) on Kohlberg: "utilitarians (like R.M. Hare) naturally called into question the objectivity and intellectual fairness of Kohlberg’s account." (`raw/sep-kohlberg-gilligan-fall-2024-heinz-stages-and-care.md`).
- **Apartheid (applied ethics).** "Better known is the last chapter of Freedom and Reason (1963), which addressed the issue, then (it seemed) wholly recalcitrant, of apartheid." (Price §1).

## Reception

Assessments below are their authors'; none is the implant's.

- **Price, on the whole (preamble).** "It was his ambition to have united elements from Aristotle, Kant, and Mill in a logically cogent way that solved the fundamental problems of ethics (though leaving unfinished business), and he usually believed himself to have achieved this." "For much of his career, his “prescriptivism” formed an important part of the curriculum, certainly in Britain." Price's forecast: "and posterity, while unlikely to ratify the logical validity of his theory, will have reason to admire it for its uniting of apparent opposites, freedom and reason, tradition and rationalism, eclecticism and rigor."
- **Price, on universalizability (§4).** On the universal/general distinction: "Hare’s clarity on the matter is his most important non-disputable contribution to philosophy." On the argument from universal prescribing: "The argument excited almost as much skepticism as attention. It seemed implausible that the very activity of prescribing universally should commit a speaker to a substantive ethical position, let alone one so distinctive." On *Moral Thinking*: "This is an extraordinary, and extraordinarily bold, intellectual construction, which invites debate at many points."
- **Price, on Hare's place (§9).** "What makes Hare arguably unique, though at the same time closer in approach to Kant than to the utilitarians whose ally he became, was that he combined this insistence upon the ineluctability of individual choice with an optimistic view of the possibilities of making choices rationally."
- **Williams and Mackie, against the two levels (Price §7).** "This objection was to be raised both by Williams (1988) and by J.L. Mackie (1985: 110–11)" — whether an agent who has internalized both levels can hold absolute intuitive principles that the critical level does not endorse. Price writes "Force still attaches to an objection by Williams (1988: 190):" and quotes him: "Rather, they constitute a way of seeing the situation; and you cannot combine seeing the situation in that way, from the point of view of those dispositions, with seeing it in the archangel’s way, in which all that is important is maximum preference satisfaction, and the dispositions themselves are merely a means to that."
  Hare's anticipating reply, side by side: the commander image (1981: 52, quoted under Problems addressed).
- **Foot, against Hare on "good"** — the cactus passage above (Hacker-Wright §6).
- **van Roojen, on motivation and supervenience** — the two assessments quoted under Problems addressed (§§3.3, 3.4).

## Vocabulary

- *Prescriptive meaning* vs *descriptive meaning*: Hare's distinction (*The Language of Morals*), defined via imperatives and truth-conditions respectively (Price §2); no vocabulary page yet ([vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md)).
- *Universalizability* (Price's spelling; the 1954/5 paper's title is *Universalisability* in its Crossref record): that a moral judgement commits the speaker to the same judgement on any relevantly similar case (van Roojen §2.2).
- *Fanatic*: one who accepts the consequences of an impersonal ideal against his own interests (*Freedom and Reason*; Price §4) — a term of art, not a characterisation of any person.
- *Archangel* and *prole*: the critical and intuitive levels of thinking, "not two social castes, but two roles" (Price §7). Price (§7) reports that Hare "came to deprecate that phrase (1981: 38)" — "rules of thumb" — in favour of "prima facie principles".
- *Paradox* in "Ross's paradox": the implant's normative sense is at [paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md); the problem page reports the uses.

# Human rights

A position page of the [positions](index.md) branch, written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md)
and graded by [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).
"Nickel & Etinson" is James Nickel and Adam Etinson,
[SEP Fall 2024 "Human Rights"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/rights-human/)
(first published 2003, revised 2024; excerpt `raw/sep-rights-human-fall-2024-concept-existence-griffin-relativism.md`).
The Declaration is quoted from the
[UN text](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights)
(`raw/un-1948-universal-declaration-of-human-rights-articles.md`). The kind
of right meant is named as each source names it; see
[Rights](../vocabulary/rights.md).

## Thesis

Nickel & Etinson (§1) state the universality feature: "All living humans—or perhaps we should say all living persons—have human rights." and "People have human rights independently of whether such rights are present in the practices, morality, or law of their country or culture."
Liao and Etinson
([2012, doi:10.1163/17455243-00903008](https://doi.org/10.1163/17455243-00903008),
abstract; `raw/liao-etinson-2012-political-and-naturalistic-conceptions.md`)
give the naturalistic form: "According to one longstanding account, the Naturalistic Conception of human rights, human rights are those that we have simply in virtue of being human."

## Answers the problem

Are there rights that every human holds whatever the law, morality or
culture of their country, and if so what grounds them and which are they?
Nickel & Etinson (preamble): "Human rights are norms that aspire to protect all people everywhere from severe political, legal, and social abuses."
They report that the claims made for them "have frequently provoked" doubt: "The strong claims often made on behalf of human rights (for example, that they are universal, inalienable, or exist independently of legal enactment as justified moral norms) have frequently provoked skeptical doubts and countering philosophical defenses (on these doubts see Lacroix & Pranchère 2016; Mutua 2002; and Waldron 1987)."
No problem page for this question exists yet in the implant.

## Canonical statements

- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights**, "proclaimed by the United Nations General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948 (General Assembly resolution 217 A)" (UN note). Preamble: "Whereas recognition of the inherent dignity and of the equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family is the foundation of freedom, justice and peace in the world,"
- **UDHR Art. 1:** "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. They are endowed with reason and conscience and should act towards one another in a spirit of brotherhood."
- **UDHR Art. 2:** "Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration, without distinction of any kind, such as race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status."
- **UDHR Arts. 3, 5, 18:** "Everyone has the right to life, liberty and security of person." "No one shall be subjected to torture or to cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment." "Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion;"
- **UDHR Arts. 22, 25, 29:** "Everyone, as a member of society, has the right to social security" (Art. 22); "Everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for the health and well-being of himself and of his family," (Art. 25); "Everyone has duties to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible." (Art. 29)
- **Its own status:** the General Assembly "Proclaims this Universal Declaration of Human Rights as a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations," (proclamation clause).
- **Vienna Declaration 1993, Art. 5** (as quoted by Nickel & Etinson §4): "All human rights are universal, indivisible and interdependent and interrelated."

## Varieties

Nickel & Etinson (§2.2) report: "Recent attempts to justify human rights offer a dizzying variety of grounds." The two families most often set side by side:

| Conception | Human rights are | Named holders (as the sources report) |
|---|---|---|
| Naturalistic / "orthodox" / moral | rights "that we have simply in virtue of being human" (Liao & Etinson) | Griffin 2008; Gewirth (Nickel & Etinson §2.2.1) |
| Political / practice-based | norms of "a highly useful political practice that humans have constructed" (Nickel & Etinson §1) | "John Rawls, Charles Beitz, and Joseph Raz" (Liao & Etinson) |

- **Griffin's normative agency** (*On Human Rights*, 2008,
  [doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199238781.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199238781.001.0001); read only as Nickel & Etinson report it). "In the current dispute between “moral” (or “orthodox”) and “political” conceptions of human rights, Griffin strongly sides with those who see human rights as fundamentally moral rights (on this debate see Liao & Etinson 2012)." "Their defining role, in Griffin’s view, is protecting people’s ability to form and pursue conceptions of a worthwhile life—a capacity that Griffin variously refers to as “autonomy”, “normative agency”, and “personhood”." "He describes practicalities as “a second ground” (2008: 37–39 [§2.5]) of human rights." (all §2.2.1)
- **Rawls's law of peoples** (*The Law of Peoples*, 1999; see [Rawls](../thinkers/rawls.md)). Wenar
  ([SEP Fall 2024 "John Rawls"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/rawls/) §5.3; `raw/sep-rawls-fall-2024-law-of-peoples-human-rights.md`): "These core human rights include rights to subsistence, security, personal property, and formal equality before the law, as well as freedoms from slavery, protections of ethnic groups against genocide, and some measure of liberty of conscience (but not, as we have seen, a right to democratic participation)." "The role of human rights in the law of peoples is thus to set limits on international toleration."
- **Beitz** (*The Idea of Human Rights*, 2009,
  [doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199572458.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199572458.001.0001); not read). Nickel & Etinson (§1): "See Beitz 2009 for a defense of the view that not all human rights are rights in a strong sense." **Raz** is named among political-conception proponents by Liao & Etinson and listed by Nickel & Etinson (§1) among critics of human rights as minimal standards (Raz 2010, "Human Rights Without Foundations"; not read).
- **Dignity-based accounts.** Nickel & Etinson (§2.2.2): "Many human rights declarations and treaties invoke human dignity as the ground of human rights."
- **Modes of existence** (Nickel & Etinson §2.1): as law ("The most obvious way in which human rights exist is as norms of national and international law."), as divine gift ("The American Declaration of Independence claims that people are “endowed by their Creator” with natural rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness."), as shared moral norms, and in "true or justified ethical outlooks".

## The case for

Each argument is stated as its proponents state it.

- **Agency as the ground (Griffin).** "Griffin holds that people value this capacity “especially highly, often more highly than even our happiness” (2008: 32 [§2.3])"; and "Griffin thinks that he can explain the universality of human rights by recognizing that normative agency is a threshold concept—once one is above the threshold one has the same rights as everyone else." (Nickel & Etinson §2.2.1).
- **Justified ethical outlooks.** Nickel & Etinson (§2.1) report the view that "Yet another way of explaining the existence of human rights is to say that they exist most basically in true or justified ethical outlooks."; "This approach requires commitment to the objectivity of such reasons."
- **Political function (Rawls, Beitz).** Nickel & Etinson (§1): "These political roles might include providing standards for international evaluations of how governments treat their people and specifying when use of economic sanctions or military intervention is permissible." Wenar (§5.3) on Rawls: "These core human rights are the minimal conditions required for persons to be able to engage in social cooperation in any real sense, so any well-ordered society must protect them."
- **Agreement without shared foundations.** Maritain, in the 1946–47 UNESCO inquiry (as quoted by Nickel & Etinson §4): "“Yes… we agree about the rights but on condition no one asks us why” (Maritain 1949: 9)". The authors connect this to Rawls: "Nonetheless, Maritain’s idea has strong echoes in contemporary philosophical work (see Taylor 1999), including John Rawls’ idea that human rights can have a minimal public or “political” justification which may be accepted from various “comprehensive” religious, moral, and philosophical points of view (Rawls 1999; Beitz 2009)."
- **High priority, modest scope.** Nickel & Etinson (§1): "Maurice Cranston held that human rights are matters of “paramount importance” and their violation “a grave affront to justice” (Cranston 1967: 51, 52)." "Henry Shue suggests that human rights concern the “lower limits on tolerable human conduct” rather than “great aspirations and exalted ideals” (Shue 1996: ix)."

## The case against

Each objection is stated as its holders state it.

- **Bentham: no rights before law** ("Anarchical Fallacies", 1795; `raw/bentham-1795-anarchical-fallacies-nonsense-upon-stilts.md`; see [Bentham](../thinkers/bentham.md)). "That there are no such things as natural rights--no such things as rights anterior to the establishment of government—no such things as natural rights opposed to, in contradistinction to, legal" "But reasons for wishing there were such things as rights, are not rights; — a reason for wishing that a certain right were established, is not that right-want is not supply - hunger is not bread." "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, -nonsense upon stilts."
- **Burke: abstract rights are practically defective** (*Reflections on the Revolution in France*, 1790, [Gutenberg #15679](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15679); `raw/burke-1790-reflections-real-and-pretended-rights-of-men.md`). "Government is not made in virtue of natural rights, which may and do exist in total independence of it,--and exist in much greater clearness, and in a much greater degree of abstract perfection: but their abstract perfection is their practical defect." "The pretended rights of these theorists are all extremes; and in proportion as they are metaphysically true, they are morally and politically false."
- **Marx: the rights of egoistic man** ("On the Jewish Question", 1843, [Marxists Internet Archive](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1844/jewish-question/); `raw/marx-1843-on-the-jewish-question-rights-of-egoistic-man.md`, `raw/marx-1844-on-the-jewish-question-political-emancipation.md`). "Above all, we note the fact that the so-called rights of man, the droits de l’homme as distinct from the droits du citoyen, are nothing but the rights of a member of civil society – i.e., the rights of egoistic man, of man separated from other men and from the community." "But, the right of man to liberty is based not on the association of man with man, but on the separation of man from man." Lutz ([IEP "Alasdair MacIntyre"](https://iep.utm.edu/mac-over/)) reports: "For Marx, “rights” could arise only from laws made by governments."
- **MacIntyre: no such rights** (*After Virtue*, 1981; see [MacIntyre](../thinkers/macintyre.md)), read here only as Bowring ([2008, doi:10.1515/auk-2008-0112](https://doi.org/10.1515/auk-2008-0112), p. 205; `raw/bowring-2008-misunderstanding-macintyre-on-human-rights.md`) reports and quotes it: "He selected Gewirth (1978), and Dworkin (1976), in order to show that there are no such rights, and belief in them is one with belief in witches and in unicorns (MacIntyre 1990, 69)." MacIntyre's target: "“I mean those rights which are alleged to belong to human beings as such and which are cited as a reason for holding that people ought not to be interfered with in their pursuit of life, liberty and happiness.” (MacIntyre 1990, 68–69)" His historical point (Bowring p. 206): "MacIntyre points to the fact that prior to the eighteenth century, where such rights were spoken of as ‘natural rights’ or ‘rights of man’, there was no expression in any ancient or medieval language correctly translated by the modern expression ‘a right’."
- **Cultural relativism: the AAA 1947 Statement** ("Statement on Human Rights", *American Anthropologist* 49(4): 539–543, [doi:10.1525/aa.1947.49.4.02a00020](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.1947.49.4.02a00020); not read, quoted as Nickel & Etinson §4 quote it). "During the drafting in 1947 of the Universal Declaration, the Executive Board of the American Anthropological Association (“AAA”) warned of the danger that the Declaration would be “a statement of rights conceived only in terms of the values prevalent in Western Europe and America”." "But the Board also made the stronger assertion that “standards and values are relative to the culture from which they derive” and thus “what is held to be a human right in one society may be regarded as anti-social by another people” (AAA 1947)." Fagan ([IEP "Human Rights"](https://iep.utm.edu/hum-rts/); `raw/iep-human-rights-fagan-natural-rights-positivism-relativism.md`) reports: "It has been argued by numerous relativists that human rights are unduly biased towards morally individualist societies and cultures, at the necessary expense of the communal moral complexion of many Asian and African societies."
- **"Asian values".** Nickel & Etinson (§4): "In the 1990s, Singapore’s Senior Minister Lee Kuan Yew and others argued that international human rights as found in United Nations declarations and treaties were insensitive to distinctive “Asian values”, such as prizing families and community (in contrast to strong individualism); putting social harmony over personal freedom; respect for political leaders and institutions; and emphasizing responsibility, hard work, and thriftiness as means of social progress (on the Asian Values debate see Bauer & Bell [eds] 1999; Bell 2000; and Sen 1997)." "At the 1993 World Conference on Human Rights in Vienna, countries including Singapore, Malaysia, China, and Iran advocated accommodations within human rights practice for cultural and economic differences."
- **Against the political conception.** Nickel & Etinson (§1), with Tasioulas, imagine one surviving state: "Surely the idea of human rights along with many dimensions of human rights practice could continue in New Zealand, even though there would be no international relations, law, or politics (for an argument of this sort see Tasioulas 2012a)." Liao & Etinson (abstract): "Finally, we argue that a Political Conception of human rights, on its own, lacks the resources necessary to determine the substantive content of human rights."
- **Against agency and dignity accounts.** Nickel & Etinson (§2.2.1) on Griffin's threshold: "Treating agency in this way, however, is a normative policy, not just a fact about concepts." and "To exclude proportional rights, and to explain the egalitarian dimensions of human rights, including their character as universal and equal rights to be enjoyed without discrimination, some additional ground pertaining to fairness and equality seems to be needed." On dignity (§2.2.2): "Another closely associated and increasingly prominent worry is that the Special Worth Thesis is speciesist, arbitrarily ranking the interests, status, and/or value of human beings above that/those of non-human animals (Kymlicka 2018; Meyer 2001)."
- **Inflation.** Nickel & Etinson (§3): "A possible result of this is “human rights inflation”, the devaluation of human rights caused by producing too much bad human rights currency (see Cranston 1973; Orend 2002; Wellman 1995; Griffin 2008)."

## Replies

Objections and replies side by side; each reply is its owner's.

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| No rights before law (Bentham; Marx as Lutz reports) | Henkin (as quoted by Nickel & Etinson §1): "Political forces have mooted the principal philosophical objections, bridging the chasm between natural and positive law by converting natural human rights into positive legal rights. (Henkin 1978: 19)" Nickel & Etinson (§2.1): "Legal enactment at the national and international levels provides a far more secure status for practical purposes." |
| Abstract rights (Burke) | Burke himself does not deny "real" rights: "In denying their false claims of right, I do not mean to injure those which are real, and are such as their pretended rights would totally destroy." and "If civil society be made for the advantage of man, all the advantages for which it is made become his right." |
| No such rights (MacIntyre) | Michael Freeman, as Bowring (p. 206–207) reports: "According to Freeman, “MacIntyre misses his target, and the target he misses is a non-foundationalist defense of rights.”"; Freeman's "answer is that rights are not things, but just claims or entitlements." Bowring's rejoinder (p. 207): "But that is surely not MacIntyre’s objection, which concerns the pretended universality of human rights, when their inception can be specified in both space and time." |
| Relativism (AAA 1947) | Nickel & Etinson (§4): "Still, philosophers have been generally critical of attempts to argue from relativism to a prescription of tolerance (see Williams 1972 [1993] and Talbott 2005)." "Accordingly, a relativist from a tolerant country will be hard-pressed to find a basis for criticizing the citizens and officials of the intolerant country." "Perhaps for these reasons, relativism is not the stance of most anthropologists today." |
| "Asian values" | Nickel & Etinson (§4): "Western representatives tended to view the position of these countries as excuses for repression and authoritarianism." Sen (*Human Rights and Asian Values*, 1997, [Carnegie Council PDF](https://media-1.carnegiecouncil.org/cceia/254_sen.pdf); `raw/sen-1997-human-rights-and-asian-values.md`) sets out to test the claim: "I shall examine the thesis that Asian values are less supportive of freedom and more concerned with order and discipline than are Western values, and that the claims of human rights in the areas of political and civil liberties are, therefore, less relevant in Asia than in the West." The Vienna Declaration answered with Art. 5 (Canonical statements). |
| Against the political conception | Nickel & Etinson (§1): "We can allow, however, that the sorts of political functions that Rawls and Beitz describe are typically served by international human rights today." |
| Inflation | "Prominent philosophers have also advocated smaller lists of human rights (see, for example, Cranston 1967 and 1973; Rawls 1999; and Griffin 2008)." (Nickel & Etinson §3.4) |

## Who holds it

- The UN General Assembly, in the Declaration of 1948. Nickel & Etinson (§4): "Three quarters of the world’s countries have ratified the major human rights treaties, and many countries in Africa, the Americas, and Europe participate in regional human rights regimes that have international courts (see the Georgetown University Human Rights Law Research Guide in the Other Internet Resources below)." and "Ratification does not, of course, guarantee compliance."
- James Griffin, *On Human Rights* (2008); Alan Gewirth (Nickel & Etinson §2.2.1).
- [John Rawls](../thinkers/rawls.md), *The Law of Peoples* (1999); Charles Beitz, *The Idea of Human Rights* (2009); Joseph Raz, "Human Rights Without Foundations" (2010) — for the political conception (Liao & Etinson).
- Earlier, John Locke's natural rights, which Fagan (IEP) reports as predecessors: "At the centre of Locke’s argument is the claim that individuals possess natural rights, independently of the political recognition granted them by the state."

No survey proportions of philosophers recorded.

## Contrasts

- **Legal positivism about rights.** Divergence on whether any right exists before enactment. Fagan (IEP): "For a legal positivist, such as the 19th. Century legal philosopher Jeremy Bentham, there can be no such thing as human rights existing prior to, or independently from legal codification."
- **Cultural relativism.** Divergence on whether standards hold across cultures; Nickel & Etinson (§4) place the difference in metaethics: "Relativists are often subjectivists or noncognitivists and think of morality as entirely socially constructed and transmitted. In contrast, philosophically-inclined human rights advocates are more likely to adhere to or presuppose cognitivism, moral realism, and intuitionism."
- **Natural rights.** Wenar ([SEP "Rights"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/rights/) §1, `raw/sep-rights-fall-2024-wenar-hohfeld-will-interest-natural.md`; via [Rights](../vocabulary/rights.md)) lists the open question "whether human rights are natural rights".
- **Higher law.** [Rule of higher law](rule-of-higher-law.md) reports Finnis (SEP "Natural Law Theories" §1.4; `raw/sep-natural-law-theories-fall-2024-lex-iniusta-nuremberg-higher-law.md`) naming "fundamental human rights" among modern names for higher-law principles.

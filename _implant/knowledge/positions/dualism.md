# Dualism

A position page of the [positions](index.md) branch, written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md)
and graded by [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md):
every statement below is someone's, with its locator; the page does not
say which side wins.

## Thesis

In its classical form: the mind, "a thinking and unextended thing", is
"entirely and truly distinct from my body, and may exist without it"
(Descartes, *Meditations* VI, ¶9, Veitch tr., AT VII 78; excerpt:
`raw/descartes-1641-meditations-thinking-thing-real-distinction.md`). In
the form the 2020 survey groups under "non-physicalism": there is
something about experience that "no amount of purely physical information
includes" ([Jackson 1982](https://doi.org/10.2307/2960077), p. 127;
excerpt: `raw/jackson-1982-epiphenomenal-qualia-mary.md`).

## Answers the problem

[What is consciousness, and how does it relate to the physical?](../problems/consciousness.md)
— the phenomenal question, answered by denying that the physical account
is the whole account. The attribution question is touched by Descartes'
two tests (below), and by the AI extension on
[Could an AI be conscious?](../problems/ai-consciousness.md).

## Canonical statements

Descartes, *Meditations* II and VI (1641; Veitch tr.; excerpt:
`raw/descartes-1641-meditations-thinking-thing-real-distinction.md`):

> "I am therefore, precisely speaking, only a thinking thing, that is, a
> mind (mens sive animus), understanding, or reason" (Med. II, ¶6; AT VII
> 27–28)

> "because, on the one hand, I have a clear and distinct idea of myself, in
> as far as I am only a thinking and unextended thing, and as, on the
> other hand, I possess a distinct idea of body, in as far as it is only
> an extended and unthinking thing, it is certain that I … is entirely
> and truly distinct from my body, and may exist without it." (Med. VI,
> ¶9; AT VII 78)

The excerpt notes that the Meditation VI passage opens from the premise
that whatever is "clearly and distinctly" conceived apart can be made to
exist apart "by the omnipotence of God", and that the AT numbers were not
verified against an AT edition.

Descartes, *Discourse* V (1637; Veitch tr., AT VI 56–57; excerpt:
`raw/descartes-1637-discourse-two-tests-for-machines.md`) — the two
tests "whereby to know that they were not therefore really men":

> "the first is that they could never use words or other signs arranged
> in such a manner as is competent to us in order to declare our thoughts
> to others … The second test is, that although such machines might
> execute many things with equal or perhaps greater perfection than any
> of us, they would, without doubt, fail in certain others from which it
> could be discovered that they did not act from knowledge, but solely
> from the disposition of their organs" (Part V)

"by means of these two tests we may likewise know the difference between
men and brutes" (Part V) — animals fall with the machines.

Huxley, "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata" (1874; *Collected
Essays* I; excerpt: `raw/huxley-1874-animals-automata-steam-whistle.md`):

> "The consciousness of brutes would appear to be related to the mechanism
> of their body simply as a collateral product of its working, and to be
> as completely without any power of modifying that working as the
> steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is
> without influence upon its machinery." (p. 240)

> "We are conscious automata" (pp. 243–244)

## Varieties

- **Substance dualism** — the real distinction of *Meditations* VI: mind
  "may exist without" body. The label "substance dualism" is the one
  Strawson uses for the view he says has "never been any good argument"
  for ([Strawson 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199267422.003.0003),
  pp. 25–26; excerpt: `raw/strawson-2006-realistic-monism.md`).
- **Epiphenomenalism** — consciousness as "a collateral product" of brain
  mechanism with no power to modify it (Huxley 1874, p. 240), and, in
  Huxley's extension from animals to humans, "all states of
  consciousness in us, as in them, are immediately caused by molecular
  changes of the brain-substance" (pp. 243–244). Jackson's 1982 title
  thesis: he argues "that the view that qualia are epiphenomenal is a
  perfectly possible one" (Jackson 1982, p. 127). The SEP entry "Qualia:
  The Knowledge Argument" (Fall 2024), §5, reports that Jackson "was one
  of the very few philosophers who embraced epiphenomenalism" and that he
  "changed his mind" (see Replies).
- **The hard-problem form (Chalmers 1995).** The excerpts held do not
  contain the label "naturalistic dualism". What they contain is the
  claim that the hard problem "is the problem of experience", that asking
  why information-processing is "experienced" is "not making a conceptual
  mistake" but "a nontrivial further question"
  ([Chalmers 1995](https://consc.net/papers/facing.html), §2; excerpt:
  `raw/chalmers-1995-facing-up-hard-problem.md`), and the excerpt's
  context note that the paper goes on to "reject reductive explanation
  for experience and to sketch a 'nonreductive' theory". SEP "Zombies"
  (Fall 2024), §1, reports that "the most systematic use of the zombie
  idea against physicalism is by David Chalmers 1996" (same excerpt).
  Chalmers has a [thinker page](../thinkers/chalmers.md).

## The case for

- **The argument from clear and distinct conception** (Descartes, Med.
  VI, ¶9): a distinct idea of mind as unextended and of body as
  unthinking yields their real distinction. Thinker page:
  [Descartes](../thinkers/descartes.md).
- **The modal argument** — as reported by SEP "The Mind/Brain Identity
  Theory" (Fall 2024), §8, reporting Kripke 1980: true identities are
  necessary, and while "heat = molecular motion" survives the apparent
  contingency because molecular motion "might have existed without being
  felt as heat", Kripke "suggests that the answer is 'No'" to whether
  pain could exist "without being felt as pain"; "the apparent
  contingency … cannot be explained by some sort of qualitative
  analogue" (Kripke 1980, pp. 151–152, via SEP; excerpt:
  `raw/kripke-1980-naming-and-necessity-via-sep.md`). SEP "Zombies"
  (Fall 2024), §2, gives Kripke's image of God creating the physical
  universe and asking whether "he have to do any more work to provide for
  consciousness". Page: [The zombie argument](../arguments/zombie-argument.md).
- **The knowledge argument** — Mary "had all the physical information.
  Ergo there is more to have than that, and Physicalism is false"
  (Jackson 1982, p. 130). Page:
  [The knowledge argument](../arguments/knowledge-argument.md).
- **The hard problem** — "Why doesn't all this information-processing go
  on 'in the dark', free of any inner feel?" (Chalmers 1995, §2), with
  the DNA/gene contrast: someone who says the functions are explained
  but experience is not is "not making a conceptual mistake" (§2).
- **Huxley's causal claim** (an argument for epiphenomenalism, not for
  substance dualism): from decerebrate frogs that "walk, hop, swim"
  without consciousness, per the excerpt's context, to consciousness as
  "a collateral product" (1874, p. 240).

## The case against

- **Ryle's category-mistake.** The "official doctrine, which hails
  chiefly from Descartes" he calls, "with deliberate abusiveness, 'the
  dogma of the Ghost in the Machine'", and holds it "entirely false, and
  false not in detail but in principle … a category-mistake" (Ryle 1949,
  ch. I, §§1–2; excerpt: `raw/ryle-1949-concept-of-mind-ghost-in-the-machine.md`).
- **Smart's nomological danglers.** That everything "should be explicable
  in terms of physics … except the occurrence of sensations seems to me
  to be frankly unbelievable. Such sensations would be 'nomological
  danglers,' to use Feigl's expression" ([Smart 1959](https://doi.org/10.2307/2182164),
  p. 142; excerpt: `raw/smart-1959-sensations-and-brain-processes.md`).
- **Place's diagnosis.** The apparent impossibility of a brain-process
  account comes, Place argues, from the "'phenomenological fallacy', the
  mistaken idea that descriptions of the appearances of things are
  descriptions of the actual state of affairs in a mysterious internal
  environment" ([Place 1956](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1956.tb00560.x),
  p. 44; excerpt: `raw/place-1956-is-consciousness-a-brain-process.md`).
- **Strawson.** Substance dualism is "a view for which (as Arnauld saw)
  there has never been any good argument" (Strawson 2006, pp. 25–26) —
  said by a writer who takes experience to be "more certain than the
  existence of anything else" (p. 3) and answers with panpsychism, not
  physicalism of the usual kind ([Panpsychism](panpsychism.md)).

## Replies

- **To the causal worry about epiphenomenalism**, Jackson's 1982 paper
  itself sets out to "turn this objection by arguing that the view that
  qualia are epiphenomenal is a perfectly possible one" (p. 127). The
  later record cuts the other way: SEP "Qualia: The Knowledge Argument"
  (Fall 2024), §5, reports that Jackson (1995) "argues that knowledge
  about qualia is impossible if qualia are epiphenomenal and he concludes
  that something must be wrong with the knowledge argument", and that in
  Jackson (2003) he answers it "by endorsing strong representationalism"
  (bibliographic: [Jackson 2003](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1358246100008365)).
- **To the charge that the gap is only epistemic**, the dualist excerpts
  held give no direct reply; the epistemic reading is Levine's own
  ([Physicalism](physicalism.md)). Chalmers' answer in the excerpt is
  the "conceptual mistake" contrast (1995, §2).
- **To Ryle and Place**, no reply from a dualist is held in the excerpts.

## Who holds it

- [Descartes](../thinkers/descartes.md) — *Meditations* II, VI (1641);
  *Discourse* V (1637).
- Huxley — epiphenomenalism, *Collected Essays* I, pp. 240, 243–244
  (1874). The excerpt's context notes he rejected the labels "fatalism,
  materialism and atheism" for himself.
- Jackson — "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982); later gave up the argument
  (SEP, §5, above).
- Kripke — *Naming and Necessity*, Lecture III, pp. 144–155 (1980), as
  reported by SEP; the book was not read for this implant.
- [Chalmers](../thinkers/chalmers.md) — 1995 hard problem; 1996 zombie
  argument (bibliographic, via SEP "Zombies" §1).
- Śaṅkara (Advaita Vedānta), as reported by Dalal, SEP "Śaṅkara" (Fall
  2024), §3.1: consciousness "is not an emergent property of the brain
  and body … because that which is inert cannot give rise to sentiency"
  — while, per the same entry, "the witness is not a separate
  substantial entity at all" (§3.1; excerpt:
  `raw/sep-shankara-fall-2024-witnessing-consciousness.md`). Whether
  that is a dualism is a classification the excerpt does not make.
- **Survey data:** consciousness — dualism 21.96 % (20.00 % exclusive),
  N = 1020; mind — non-physicalism 32.08 % (31.85 %), N = 1733; zombies
  metaphysically possible 24.41 %; a hard problem exists, yes 62.42 %
  ([Bourget & Chalmers 2023](https://doi.org/10.3998/phimp.2109), 2020
  results table; excerpt:
  `raw/bourget-chalmers-2023-philpapers-survey-consciousness-and-amcs-2023-open-letter.md`).
  The sample is English-language philosophy faculty.

## Contrasts

- **[Physicalism](physicalism.md)** — the point of divergence is the
  "is": Place's "'is' of composition" (1956, p. 45) and Smart's "strict
  identity" (1959, p. 145) against Descartes' "may exist without it".
- **[Panpsychism](panpsychism.md)** — Strawson keeps the dualist's
  realism about experience (2006, p. 3) and drops the second substance
  by locating experience in the "ultimates" (p. 25).
- **[Illusionism](illusionism.md)** — denies the datum dualism starts
  from: "experiences do not really have qualitative, 'what-it's-like'
  properties, whether physical or non-physical" (Frankish 2016, §1.2).
- **Chinese *xin* 心** — Wong, SEP "Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese
  Philosophy" (Fall 2024), §1, reports that "the dominant view in
  scholarship is that Chinese thought is not dualistic in any fashion",
  a minority view (Slingerland) that "other forms of dualism may fairly
  be attributed", and concludes (§1.2) that "the Chinese view is a
  genuine alternative to substance dualism", since "unlike Descartes'
  mind, xin's qi can interact with both the body's qi and the wider qi"
  (excerpt: `raw/sep-chinese-mind-fall-2024-heart-mind-and-body.md`).
- **Śaṅkara's witness** — shares the denial that inert matter yields
  sentiency, but the witness "is not a separate substantial entity" and
  is, in the absolute order, "numerically identical with nondual
  existence, which is brahman" (SEP "Śaṅkara" §3) — a monism, on the
  SEP author's account, not a two-substance view.
- **Buddhist not-self** — the opposite starting point: no "substantial,
  independent, and enduring self" (Coseru, SEP "Mind in Indian Buddhist
  Philosophy", Fall 2024, preamble; see [Illusionism](illusionism.md)).

## Vocabulary

Terms this page depends on, each open work in
[vocabulary](../vocabulary/index.md): *substance*, *real distinction*,
*qualia* (Jackson's sense), *epiphenomenal*, *physical information*,
*consciousness* (phenomenal sense, per the hub's adoption of Block's
split). Descartes' "thinking thing" covers doubting, understanding,
willing, imagining and perceiving at once (Med. II, ¶8) — a wider
extension than the phenomenal sense used elsewhere on this branch.

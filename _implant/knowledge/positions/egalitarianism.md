## Thesis

Arneson (SEP, preamble): "Egalitarianism is a trend of thought in political philosophy. An egalitarian favors equality of some sort: People should get the same, or be treated the same, or be treated as equals, in some respect."
He adds a relational reading: "An alternative view expands on this last-mentioned option: People should be treated as equals, should treat one another as equals, should relate as equals, or enjoy an equality of social status of some sort."

Gosepath (SEP "Equality", §6) gives a criterion: "A conception of justice is egalitarian when it views equality as a fundamental goal of justice."

## Canonical statements

- **Temkin 1986**, as quoted by Gosepath (SEP "Equality", §6): "an egalitarian is any person who attaches some value to equality itself (that is, any person that cares at all about equality, over and above the extent it promotes other ideals)".
  And (same block quotation, Temkin 1986, p. 100): "Egalitarians have the deep and (for them) compelling view that it is a bad thing – unjust and unfair – for some to be worse off than others through no fault of their own."
- **Strict equality**, as stated by Lamont & Favor (SEP "Distributive Justice", §2): "One of the simplest principles of distributive justice is that of strict, or radical, equality. The principle says that every person should have the same level of material goods (including burdens) and services. The principle is most commonly justified on the grounds that people are morally equal and that equality in material goods and services is the best way to give effect to this moral ideal."
- **Background idea**, per Arneson (preamble): "Egalitarian doctrines tend to rest on a background idea that all human persons are equal in fundamental worth or moral status. So far as the Western European and Anglo-American philosophical tradition is concerned, one significant source of this thought is the Christian notion that God loves all human souls equally."

The primary texts named below (Cohen 1989, Arneson 1989, Anderson 1999,
Frankfurt 1987, Parfit 1997, Temkin 2003) were not read for this page;
their bibliographic data were verified by DOI content negotiation
(records: `raw/egalitarianism-doi-records.md`), and what they say is reported only as the SEP authors report it.

## Varieties

Arneson (preamble) calls the doctrine "protean": "Egalitarianism is a protean doctrine, because there are several different types of equality, or ways in which people might be treated the same, or might relate as equals, that might be thought desirable."

- **Luck egalitarianism.** Gosepath (§3.6): "They hold that it is bad – unjust or unfair – for some to be worse off than others through no fault or choice of their own (Temkin 1993, 13) and therefore strive to eliminate involuntary disadvantages, for which the sufferer cannot be held responsible (Cohen 1989, 916)."
  Lamont & Favor (§3): "Indeed, ‘luck egalitarianism’ as a distinct approach in the distributive justice literature really developed in critical response to Rawls’ theory of distributive justice."
  - *Ronald Dworkin* (equality of resources; *Sovereign Virtue*, 2000, per Arneson's bibliography). Lamont & Favor (§4): "In retrospect, Dworkin’s theory is often identified as one of the earliest in the luck egalitarian literature, though Dworkin himself called his theory Resource Egalitarianism." and "Dworkin proposed that people begin with equal resources but be allowed to end up with unequal economic benefits as a result of their own choices."
    Arneson (§3.5): "In this exercise, we seek to undo the effect of brute luck but not option luck, that former being luck that falls on you in ways beyond your power to control and the latter being luck that you can avoid, and maybe reasonably avoid, by voluntary choice."
  - *Richard Arneson* (equal opportunity for welfare; "Equality and equal opportunity for welfare", *Philosophical Studies* 56(1) (1989): 77–93, DOI [10.1007/BF00646210](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00646210), verified; not read). Arneson (SEP §3.6): "In a slogan, one might assert an ideal of equality of opportunity for welfare."
  - *G. A. Cohen* ("On the Currency of Egalitarian Justice", *Ethics* 99(4) (1989): 906–944, DOI [10.1086/293126](https://doi.org/10.1086/293126), verified; not read). Cited by Gosepath at p. 916 in the passage above.
- **Relational (democratic, social-relations) equality.** Arneson (§4): "One is the idea that in an egalitarian society people should relate to one another as equals or should enjoy the same fundamental status (and also perhaps the same rank and power)."
  Gosepath (§4): "The principle of democratic equality (as asked for by Anderson 1999) requires us to eliminate social hierarchies that prevent a democratically organized society, a society in which we cooperate and decide upon state action as equals."
  Elizabeth Anderson, "What Is the Point of Equality?", *Ethics* 109(2) (1999): 287–337, DOI [10.1086/233897](https://doi.org/10.1086/233897) (verified; not read).
- **Intrinsic, instrumental, constitutive.** Gosepath (§6.1): "It is apparent that there are three sorts of egalitarianism: intrinsic, instrumental and constitutive."
  He reports "Only some egalitarians hold inequality to be bad per se. Most of today’s egalitarians are pluralistic, recognizing other values besides equality." (§3.6).

## The case for

Each point is a proponent's argument as an SEP author reports it.

- **Moral equality of persons.** Lamont & Favor (§2) report strict equality as "most commonly justified on the grounds that people are morally equal" (full sentence under Canonical statements); Arneson (preamble) reports the "background idea" of equal fundamental worth.
- **Unchosen disadvantage is unfair.** The luck-egalitarian argument from the arbitrariness of endowments, as Gosepath (§3.6) presents it: "The kind and the extent of one’s natural abilities are due to a lottery of nature; considered from a moral standpoint, their distribution is purely arbitrary (Rawls, 1971, § 48)."
  Arneson (§3.5), on Dworkin's version: "The Dworkin proposal is noteworthy for its integration of themes of equality and personal responsibility in a single conception." (Arneson's assessment.)
- **A society of equals.** For relational egalitarians, per Arneson (§4): "From the standpoint of the relational equality versions of egalitarianism, equality of condition doctrines get the moral priorities backward."
- **Equality as required by justification to all.** Constitutive egalitarians, per Gosepath (§6.2): "In the view of the constitutive egalitarians, all the judgments of distributive justice should be approached relationally, by asking which distributive scheme all concerned parties can universally and reciprocally agree to."

## The case against

- **The levelling-down objection** (attributed by Gosepath, §6.1, to Nozick 1974, Raz 1986 and Temkin 1993: "(For such an objection, cf. Nozick 1974, p. 229, Raz 1986, chap. 9, p. 227, 235, Temkin 1993, pp. 247–8.)"; Arneson, §6, attributes the diagnosis to Parfit 1991, Temkin 1993 and Holtug 2010).
  Arneson (§6): "What has been called the “leveling-down objection” suggests a reason to answer in the negative: everyone's being equal is not valuable for its own sake."
  "If equality is non-instrumentally valuable, then the situation that obtains after leveling down must be in at least one respect better than the preceding situation: the new situation is more equal. The objection simply is that it is counterintuitive to claim that in any respect increasing equality by making someone worse off and no one else better off is improving the situation."
  Gosepath's example (§6.1): "But would it be morally good if, in a group consisting of both blind and sighted persons, those with sight were rendered blind because the blind could not be offered sight? That would be morally perverse."
  The objector's diagnosis, per Arneson (§6): "By a halo effect, the goodness of 2 appears to shine on 1." (1 = people being more equal; 2 = the worse off becoming better off.)
- **Comparison is not what matters (sufficientarian critique).** Frankfurt, as quoted by Gosepath (§6.2): "“The fundamental error of egalitarianism lies in supposing that it is morally important whether one person has less than another regardless of how much either of them has” (Frankfurt 1987, p. 34)."
- **Pareto.** Lamont & Favor (§2), against strict equality: "But the most common criticism is a welfare-based one related to the Pareto efficiency requirement: that everyone can be materially better off if incomes are not strictly equal (Carens 1981). It is this criticism which partly inspired the Difference Principle."
- **Against patterned principles.** Gosepath (§6.2): "While the older version of a critique of egalitarianism comes mainly from the conservative end of the political spectrum, thus arguing in general against “patterned principles of justice” (Nozick 1974, esp. pp. 156–157), the critique’s newer version also often can be heard in progressive circles (Walzer 1983, Raz 1986, chap. 9, Frankfurt 1987, 1997, Parfit 1997, Anderson 1999)."
- **Relational egalitarians against luck egalitarianism.** Arneson (§4): "One is that the luck egalitarian wrongly takes the aim of egalitarian justice to be achieving an equal distribution of stuff rather than egalitarian solidarity and respect among members of society."
  "Another is that the policy recommendations implied by luck egalitarian principles are too harsh in their dealings with people who fare badly but are deemed personally responsible for their plight".
  Anderson, as quoted by Arneson (§4): "“the proper negative aim of egalitarian justice is not to eliminate the impact of brute luck from human affairs, but to end oppression, which by definition is socially imposed” (Anderson 1999)".
  Gosepath (§3.6) adds the privacy objection: "Against such a procedure, one could object that it subjects the citizens to the tutelage of the state and harms their private sphere (Anderson 1999, also Hayek 1960: 85–102)."

## Replies

- **To levelling down: "in one respect better".** Arneson (§6): "As stated, the leveling down objection invites the reply by the egalitarian that the situation after leveling down is indeed in one respect better than the situation previously obtaining: after all, we now have greater equality or a closer approximation to a condition of equality. We may have a standoff of conflicting moral intuitions."
  Gosepath (§6.1) on the pluralist form of the reply: "Then intrinsic egalitarians could say there is something good about the change, namely greater equality, although they would concede that much is bad about it."
  And on the scope of the objection: "Such leveling-down objections would of course only be valid if there were indeed no better and equally egalitarian alternatives available, but there are nearly always such alternatives: e.g. those who can see should have to help the blind, financially or otherwise."
  Larry Temkin's defence of this position, "Egalitarianism Defended", *Ethics* 113(4) (2003): 764–782, DOI [10.1086/373955](https://doi.org/10.1086/373955) (verified; not read), is recorded here bibliographically only.
- **To the relational critique.** Arneson (§4), for the luck egalitarian: "The luck egalitarian critic of this relational equality ideal is committed only to rejecting it as a proposal for the domain of moral right."
  Arneson (§4) points to Dworkin 2003 and 2010 and Arneson 2004 "for responses to criticisms"; not read.

## Who holds it

- Luck egalitarianism: Ronald Dworkin and G. A. Cohen, named by Arneson (§4) as the view's "foremost exponents" in the passage "Elizabeth Anderson and Samuel Scheffler both affirm versions of relational equality and from this standpoint criticize the family of views that Anderson calls “luck egalitarian,” whose foremost exponents are perhaps G. A. Cohen and Ronald Dworkin"; Richard Arneson (1989); Larry Temkin (1986, 1993).
- Relational equality: Gosepath (§4): "Proponents of social relations egalitarianism include Anderson (1999), Miller (1997), Scanlon (1996, 2018), Scheffler (2003, 2005, 2015), Wolff (1998, 2010) and Young (1990)."
- [John Rawls](../thinkers/rawls.md): Wenar (SEP "Rawls", preamble) describes justice as fairness as a society "cooperating within an egalitarian economic system" (`raw/sep-rawls-fall-2024-life-justice-as-fairness-political-liberalism.md`); Cohen's "egalitarian ethos" critique of the difference principle is on the Rawls page (`raw/sep-justice-distributive-fall-2024-rawls-difference-principle-critics.md`).
- Critics named in the sources: [Robert Nozick](../thinkers/nozick.md) (Nozick 1974, cited by Gosepath §6.1 and §6.2), [Derek Parfit](../thinkers/parfit.md) (Parfit 1991, 1997), Harry Frankfurt (1987), Joseph Raz (1986).
- Survey proportions: none recorded in the sources read.

## Contrasts

- **Prioritarianism.** Arneson (§6.2): "Suppose that for any person, the lower her lifetime well-being score—the amount of well-being she gains or is now expected to gain over the course of her life—the more morally valuable it would be to secure an incremental gain of well-being for her (or to avoid a small loss). This is the root idea of prioritarianism."
  Point of divergence, per Gosepath (§6.2): "According to prioritarians, benefiting people is more important the worse off those people are. This prioritising will often increase equality, but they are two distinct values, since in an important respect equality is a relational value while priority is not."
  Arneson (§6.2): "Prioritarianism is not vulnerable to the leveling down objection, so if one finds that objection decisive against any position that takes equality of any sort to be non-instrumentally valuable, priority looks to be an ethically attractive alternative."
  Gosepath (§6.2): "It is thus a matter of debate whether prioritarianism is a sort of egalitarianism or a (decent) inegalitarianism."
  Derek Parfit, "Equality and Priority", *Ratio* 10(3) (1997): 202–221, DOI [10.1111/1467-9329.00041](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9329.00041) (verified; not read).
- **Sufficientarianism.** Frankfurt, as quoted by Gosepath (§6.2): "“What is important from the moral point of view is not that everyone should have the same but that each should have enough. If everyone had enough, it would be of no moral consequence whether some had more than others” (Frankfurt 1987, p. 21)."
  Point of divergence: comparative versus absolute standard; Arneson (§6.1): "Perhaps the problem is not that the poor have less than the rich, but rather that the poor do not have enough—a sufficient level of resources to provide a good life or a reasonable prospect of a good life."
  Arneson's objection to it (§6.1): "A potential problem for the sufficiency doctrine is that it may not be possible non-arbitrarily to specify a line of sufficiency such that moving a person just across that line has great moral significance."
  Harry Frankfurt, "Equality as a Moral Ideal", *Ethics* 98(1) (1987): 21–43, DOI [10.1086/292913](https://doi.org/10.1086/292913) (verified; not read).
- **Rawls's difference principle (maximin).** Arneson (§6.2): "For example, if benefit is identified with the Rawlsian idea of a primary social good, and one takes prioritarianism beyond its limit to maximin, one then has the Rawlsian conception, that justice in most general terms is bringing about the best possible outcome for the very worst off."
- **Desert.** Arneson (§6): "The values that might be confounded with equality include sufficiency, priority, and desert."

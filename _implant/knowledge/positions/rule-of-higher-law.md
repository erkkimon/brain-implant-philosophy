## Thesis

Wikipedia's lead (pointer only): "The rule according to a higher law is a philosophical concept that no law may be enforced by the government unless it conforms with certain universal principles (written or unwritten) of fairness, morality, and justice."

In the natural law tradition the thesis is usually carried by the slogan
*lex iniusta non est lex*. Finnis (preamble) states the two-sided view of
law it presupposes: "Natural law theory accepts that law can be considered and spoken of both as a sheer social fact of power and practice, and as a set of reasons for action that can be and often are sound as reasons and therefore normative for reasonable people addressed by them. This dual character of positive law is presupposed by the well-known slogan “Unjust laws are not laws.”"

Structural note (this implant): the sources below differ on what follows
from a conflict with higher law — (a) the rule binds no one in conscience,
(b) the rule loses its legal validity, or (c) judges may set it aside.
Each holder is placed under Varieties by the claim the source attributes.

## Answers the problem

What makes a rule law, and does a rule's injustice bear on its legal
validity or on the duty to obey it? Green & Adams (preamble) frame the
opposing thesis as "Legal positivism is the thesis that the existence and content of law depends on social facts and not on its merits."

## Canonical statements

- **Cicero, *De re publica* III (conventionally III.33)**, surviving as Lactantius quotes it (*Divine Institutes* VI.8, tr. Fletcher 1886; [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/07016.htm); `raw/cicero-de-re-publica-3-33-true-law-via-lactantius-fletcher.md`): "There is indeed a true law, right reason, agreeing with nature, diffused among all, unchanging, everlasting, which calls to duty by commanding, deters from wrong by forbidding" and "It is not allowable to alter the provisions of this law, nor is it permitted us to modify it, nor can it be entirely abrogated. Nor, truly, can we be released from this law, either by the senate or by the people"
- **[Augustine](../thinkers/augustine.md), *De libero arbitrio* I.5**, as [Aquinas](../thinkers/aquinas.md) quotes it (the Augustine text itself was not read): "a law that is not just, seems to be no law at all."
- **Aquinas, *ST* I-II q. 95 a. 2** (tr. English Dominicans 1920; [text](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2095.htm); `raw/aquinas-st-1-2-q95-a2-q96-a4-unjust-law-perversion-of-law.md`): "Consequently every human law has just so much of the nature of law, as it is derived from the law of nature. But if in any point it deflects from the law of nature, it is no longer a law but a perversion of law."
- **Radbruch 1946**, "Gesetzliches Unrecht und übergesetzliches Recht", *Süddeutsche Juristen-Zeitung* 1: 105–108 ([scan](https://archive.org/details/Radbruch); `raw/radbruch-1946-gesetzliches-unrecht-formula.md`; English tr. *Oxford Journal of Legal Studies* 26 (2006) 1–11, [doi:10.1093/ojls/gqi041](https://doi.org/10.1093/ojls/gqi041), not read). The formula, in German: "Der Konflikt zwischen der Gerechtigkeit und der Rechtssicherheit dürfte dahin zu lösen sein, daß das positive, durch Satzung und Macht gesicherte Recht auch dann den Vorrang hat, wenn es inhaltlich ungerecht und unzweckmäßig ist, es sei denn, daß der Widerspruch des positiven Gesetzes zur Gerechtigkeit ein so unerträgliches Maß erreicht, daß das Gesetz als »unrichtiges Recht« der Gerechtigkeit zu weichen hat." Second clause: "wo Gerechtigkeit nicht einmal erstrebt wird, wo die Gleichheit, die den Kern der Gerechtigkeit ausmacht, bei der Setzung positiven Rechts bewußt verleugnet wurde, da ist das Gesetz nicht etwa nur »unrichtiges Recht«, vielmehr entbehrt es überhaupt der Rechtsnatur."
  Gloss (this implant's literal reading, not a published translation): positive law keeps priority even when unjust, unless its contradiction with justice reaches an intolerable degree; where equality is deliberately denied, the statute lacks the nature of law altogether.
- **Martin Luther King, Jr., "Letter from a Birmingham Jail" (16 April 1963)** ([Wayback copy of the UPenn text](https://web.archive.org/web/20250101071208/https://www.africa.upenn.edu/Articles_Gen/Letter_Birmingham.html); `raw/king-1963-letter-from-birmingham-jail-unjust-law.md`): "I would agree with St. Augustine that "an unjust law is no law at all."" and "To put it in the terms of St. Thomas Aquinas: An unjust law is a human law that is not rooted in eternal law and natural law."

## Varieties

- **Divine and natural law above human law (Aquinas).** Murphy (§1.1) reports the layering: "The fundamental thesis affirmed here by Aquinas is that the natural law is a participation in the eternal law (ST IaIIae 91, 2)." Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 96 a. 4) separates two cases. Laws "contrary to human good" are "acts of violence rather than laws" which "do not bind in conscience, except perhaps in order to avoid scandal or disturbance"; laws "opposed to the Divine good" are such that "laws of this kind must nowise be observed, because, as stated in Acts 5:29, "we ought to obey God rather than man.""
- **Classical natural law of reason (Cicero).** The higher law is "right reason, agreeing with nature", and Cicero (as Lactantius quotes him) names "God, the framer, arbitrator, and proposer of this law".
- **The Radbruch formula (1946).** A threshold version: positive law prevails over injustice except at an "unerträgliches Maß" or where equality is "bewußt verleugnet". Radbruch opens by naming the maxims he answers: "»Befehl ist Befehl« und »Gesetz ist Gesetz«."
- **Finnis's two-sense reading.** Finnis (§4) holds that a seriously unjust rule can be described both ways: "If a course of reflection or discourse makes it appropriate to acknowledge the rule’s “settled” or “posited” character as cognizable by reference to social-fact sources, one can say that it is legally valid though too unjust to be obeyed or applied." He reports of the textual source: "Aquinas never says simply “unjust law is not law” but rather “unjust law is not straightforwardly or unqualifiedly [simpliciter] law” or “is a perversion of law”, and similar statements."
- **Higher law in modern legal names.** Finnis (§1.4) lists, for principles found in virtually all legal systems, "In modern law they are picked out, in principle, by names such as “the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations” (Statute of the International Court of Justice, art. 38), ius cogens erga omnes" and "“higher law”, or “fundamental human rights.”" (see [Human rights](human-rights.md)).
- **Procedural version (Fuller).** Waldron (§3.6) reports Fuller's claim that a regime's disregard of legality can cost it the name of law: "when all these things have become true of a dictatorship, it is not hard for me, at least, to deny to it the name of law." (Fuller 1958: 660; [doi:10.2307/1338226](https://doi.org/10.2307/1338226)).
- **Substantive rule of law.** Waldron (§5.3): "It is also widely believed—though not necessarily by the same people who associate legality with property—that a system of positive law that fails to respect fundamental human rights should not be dignified with the term “the Rule of Law”."

## The case for

- **Judgment of regimes under color of law (Nuremberg).** Finnis (§3.1.1) reports the tribunal's ruling: "The judges held that the defendants had at all relevant times been bound by (and in many instances had acted in violation of) the principles or rules specified in the London Charter". He sets out exclusive positivism, inclusive positivism and natural law as three accounts of it, and gives his own assessment: "Still, (iii) natural law theory’s account seems the most explanatory: the moral rules applied were also rules of the “higher law” applicable in all times and places"
- **Judges avoiding grave injustice.** Finnis (§3.2): "When judges, in order to avoid grave injustice, depart from the settled understanding of the law (and perhaps from the clear terms of a decree) and apply an alternative, morally mandated interpretation, regarding themselves as licensed to do so by the higher law of reason, nature and humanity, they need not be lying if they say that in doing so they are both rectifying and applying the law (of their state)."
- **"Gesetz ist Gesetz" left jurists defenceless (Radbruch).** Radbruch: "Mittels zweier Grundsätze wußte der Nationalsozialismus seine Gefolgschaft, einerseits die Soldaten, andererseits die Juristen, an sich zu fesseln: »Befehl ist Befehl« und »Gesetz ist Gesetz«."
- **Grounds for conscientious disobedience (King).** "One has not only a legal but a moral responsibility to obey just laws. Conversely, one has a moral responsibility to disobey unjust laws." King adds a constraint: "One who breaks an unjust law must do so openly, lovingly, and with a willingness to accept the penalty."
- **Law's point is justice (Finnis, §4).** Finnis holds that the slogan concedes that the rule is in some respects a law, and argues: "The meaning of “an unjust law is not a law” is essentially identical to Hart’s “This is law but too iniquitous to be applied or obeyed” (or availed of as a defense)."
- **Legality restrains wickedness (Fuller).** Waldron (§3.6) reports Fuller's claim that "even in the most perverted regimes there is a certain hesitancy about writing cruelties, intolerances, and inhumanities into law" and adds "Fuller acknowledged that this link between legality and justice was tentative. It was certainly controversial."

## The case against

- **Existence and merit are separate questions (Austin, Bentham).** Austin (1832, as Green & Adams quote him): "The existence of law is one thing; its merit and demerit another. Whether it be or be not is one enquiry; whether it be or be not conformable to an assumed standard, is a different enquiry." Green & Adams (§1) report that "its first full elaboration is due to Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) whose account Austin adopted, modified, and popularized." [Bentham](../thinkers/bentham.md) (*Anarchical Fallacies*, 1795; `raw/bentham-1795-anarchical-fallacies-nonsense-upon-stilts.md`) denies "no such things as natural rights opposed to, in contradistinction to, legal" and writes "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, -nonsense upon stilts." (see [Rights](../vocabulary/rights.md)).
- **Clarity about law serves moral critique (Hart 1958).** Hart, "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals", *Harvard Law Review* 71(4): 593 ([doi:10.2307/1338225](https://doi.org/10.2307/1338225); the article itself was not read), as Green & Adams (§2) quote him, warns of "an enormous overvaluation of the importance of the bare fact that a rule may be said to be a valid rule of law, as if this, once declared, was conclusive of the final moral question: “Ought this law to be obeyed?”" Finnis (§3.1) gives Hart's formula as "This is law; but it is too iniquitous to be applied or obeyed".
- **Positivism does not demand obedience.** Green & Adams (§2): "No legal positivist argues that the systemic validity of law establishes its moral validity, i.e., that it should be obeyed by subjects or applied by judges." and "Bentham and Austin, as utilitarians, hold that such questions always turn on the consequences, and both acknowledge that disobedience is therefore sometimes fully justified."
- **Legality is compatible with injustice (against Fuller).** Waldron (§3.6) reports the positivists' "conventional wisdom": "The conventional wisdom of the legal positivists held that laws could be impeccably drafted and even-handedly administered and still be hideously unjust: antebellum slave law in the United States and apartheid law in South Africa were often cited as examples." Green & Adams (§3): "there is little, if anything, to be said in favor of a clear, consistent, prospective, public and impartially administered system of racial segregation, for example." Hart (1965), per Waldron (§3.6), on Fuller's principles: "They seemed to be more like instrumental principles for effective legislation, and on Hart’s view, they were only as moral as the enterprise they made possible."
- **Natural law theory accommodates positivism.** Green & Adams (§3) on Finnis: "it becomes hard to see how the natural law theory he develops rivals rather than accommodates the truth of legal positivism (see Gardner 2001, 225–227)."
- **Legal certainty (Kant, as Finnis reports).** Finnis (§4): "Some theories have adopted certain main tenets of natural law theory, and professed to be natural law theories, but have asserted that even the most unjust laws create an obligation to obey which is both legal and moral." He names Kant's theory as one, citing Alexy 2002.

## Replies

- **To Hart on the slogan.** Finnis (§4): "Hart’s argument that use of the slogan must tend to discourage or confuse the moral critique of law seems historically and logically indefensible." (his assessment).
- **To the charge that positivism is quietist — a positivist reply to the case for.** Green & Adams (§2), as above, deny that positivists hold law must be obeyed; Finnis (§3.1) in turn reads contemporary positivists as commending "This is law; but it is too iniquitous to be applied or obeyed" rather than Bentham's "Under a government of laws … obey punctually and … censure freely"
- **Fuller's question to positivism.** Green & Adams (§3) quote Fuller asking how "an amoral datum called law could have the peculiar quality of creating an obligation to obey it" (Fuller 1958: 656); their own reply follows in the same section.
- **The formulation is still open.** Finnis (§4): "But precisely how the classic position itself should be formulated, explained and applied today is debated between Alexy, Finnis and others (Alexy 2013; Finnis 2014; Crowe 2019)."

## Contrasts

- **Legal positivism.** The point of divergence, as Green & Adams (§4.2) put the positivist side: "it rejects any dependence of the existence of law on its merits (Gardner 2001)." Finnis (§4) holds that the slogan, rightly read, says the same as Hart's formula, so that the divergence is over the sense of "law", not over obedience.
- **Formal rule of law.** Waldron (§5.3): "Though many jurists follow Raz 1977 in thinking that the Rule of Law is a purely formal/procedural ideal, others believe in adding a more substantive dimension." The divergence is whether respect for fundamental rights is part of the ideal.
- **Natural law ethics without a legal thesis.** Murphy (preamble) separates the two: "We will be concerned only with natural law theories of ethics: while such views arguably have some interesting implications for law, politics, and religious morality, these implications will not be our focus here."

## Vocabulary

- *Higher law* — a pointer term for whatever standard the cited holder places above positive law (eternal, natural, divine law, right reason, *ius gentium*, fundamental human rights).
- *Positive law* — law "posited" by social-fact sources (Green & Adams §1; Finnis preamble).
- *Justice* — used in each holder's own sense; see [Justice](../vocabulary/justice.md).
- *Rights* — natural versus legal rights in Bentham's dispute; see [Rights](../vocabulary/rights.md).

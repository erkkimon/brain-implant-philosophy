## Thesis

Rothbard's statement: "The libertarian creed rests upon one central axiom: that no man or group of men may aggress against the person or property of anyone else."
(*For a New Liberty*, p. 27). He defines the key term: "This may be called the “nonaggression axiom.” “Aggression” is defined as the initiation of the use or threat of physical violence against the person or property of anyone else."
(p. 27). Rand's statement (below) adds that force is permitted "only in retaliation".

Van der Vossen & Christmas (SEP, preamble) summarise libertarianism in
the same terms: "The content of libertarian political philosophy, broadly speaking, is a claim that the initiation of interpersonal force is unjust."

## Answers the problem

No problem page for this question exists in the implant yet. The question
the principle answers, as Zwolinski's 2016 abstract frames it, is what
grounds "the libertarian opposition to redistributive taxation, legal paternalism, and perhaps even the state itself"
([Zwolinski 2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/s026505251600011x), abstract;
`raw/zwolinski-2016-christmas-2017-nonaggression-principle-abstracts.md`)
— that is, the limits of permissible coercion by persons and by states.

## Canonical statements

- **Rothbard 1973.** The axiom and its definition (p. 27, above), and:
  "Aggression is therefore synonymous with invasion." (p. 27). On scope:
  "The libertarian, in short, insists on applying the general moral law to everyone, and makes no special exemptions for any person or group."
  (p. 28).
- **Rand.** "The basic political principle of the Objectivist ethics is: no man may initiate the use of physical force against others."
  and "Men have the right to use physical force only in retaliation and only against those who initiate its use."
  ("The Objectivist Ethics", *The Virtue of Selfishness*, p. 32, read as
  quoted in the [Ayn Rand Lexicon](http://aynrandlexicon.com/lexicon/physical_force.html);
  `raw/rand-1963-objectivist-ethics-initiation-of-force-via-lexicon.md`).
  Zwolinski (IEP, §6) annotates that Rand, "While rejecting the label “libertarian,”"
  advocates "a minimal state that uses force only in retaliation".
- **Nozick 1974, side constraints.** "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)."
  (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, Preface, p. ix) and "Side constraints express the inviolability of other persons."
  (p. 32; `raw/nozick-1974-anarchy-state-utopia-rights-entitlement-wilt-utopia.md`).
  Nozick does not use the phrase in the excerpts held; the parallel is a
  structural note the implant adds (manifest [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)).

## Varieties

- **Foundations.** Rothbard lists "three broad types of foundation for the libertarian axiom, corresponding to three kinds of ethical philosophy: the emotivist, the utilitarian, and the natural rights viewpoint."
  (p. 30) and adopts natural rights (pp. 30–31). Van der Vossen & Christmas (preamble)
  report that "Within the discipline of philosophy, libertarianism is most famously deontological (Rothbard 1978, 1982, Nozick 1974, Mack 1981, 1989, 1990, 1998, 1999, 2010, 2018, Steiner 1994), taking a cue from both John Locke and Immanuel Kant."
- **Non-axiomatic.** Zwolinski (IEP, §3.b) reports Hayek's route: institutions that "leave individuals free to act as they wish so long as they do not act aggressively toward others",
  but "For Hayek, however, this principle is not a moral axiom but rather follows from his beliefs regarding the limits and uses of knowledge in society."
- **Minimal state versus anarcho-capitalism.** Zwolinski (IEP, §1): the "minimal state" theories of Nozick and Rand "hold that states may legitimately provide police, courts, and a military, but nothing more."
  Rothbard (p. 28) names the State as "one central, dominant, and overriding aggressor";
  Zwolinski (IEP, §6) annotates Childs 1994 [1969] as arguing "that a proper respect for those rights requires anarcho-capitalism."
- **Absolute or moderate.** Zwolinski 2016 (abstract) proposes "this more moderate and defensible version of the NAP" against the absolutist one (see the case against).

## The case for

Stated as proponents state it, from the cited texts.

- **Consistency of one moral law.** Rothbard (p. 28) holds that the
  libertarian "insists on applying the general moral law to everyone, and makes no special exemptions for any person or group."
  Zwolinski (IEP, §1) lists among libertarian commitments "that governments are bound by essentially the same moral principles as individuals;".
- **The difference between attack and defence.** Rand (p. 32, via
  Lexicon): "The ethical principle involved is simple and clear-cut: it is the difference between murder and self-defense."
- **Self-ownership.** Rothbard argues that denying self-ownership leaves
  "only two alternatives: either (1) a certain class of people, A, have the right to own another class, B; or (2) everyone has the right to own his own equal quotal share of everyone else."
  (p. 34). Zwolinski (IEP, §2.c) reports this as "Murray Rothbard’s claim that the only alternatives to self-ownership are slavery or communism (Rothbard 1973, 29)".
- **Inviolability.** Nozick (p. 32): "Side constraints express the inviolability of other persons."
  Mack (SEP "Robert Nozick's Political Philosophy", §2.3) reports: "For Nozick, rights express the moral inviolability of individuals;"
  (`raw/sep-nozick-political-fall-2024-life-rights-entitlement-utopia.md`).
- **Reach of the principle to harms like pollution.** Rothbard (p. 319):
  "Air pollution that injures others is aggression pure and simple."

## The case against

Stated as opponents state it, from the cited texts. Zwolinski, the first
critic below, writes as a libertarian: his abstract defends a moderate
NAP that "still has important libertarian implications".

- **Moralized aggression depends on a prior theory of property.**
  Zwolinski 2016 (abstract): "First, insofar as libertarians employ a moralized understanding of aggression, their principle is really about the protection of property rights rather than the prohibition of aggression."
  Van der Vossen & Christmas (SEP, §2) report the critique: "A common critique of approaches to libertarianism grounded in a non-aggression principle (NAP) is that is assumes without argument that property rights violations constitute aggressions against the person, when the connection of property to person is precisely the point of contention (Zwolinski 2016)."
- **Absolutism.** Zwolinski 2016 (abstract): "Second, the absolutist prohibition on aggression, which libertarians typically endorse and which is necessary to generate strongly libertarian conclusions, is grossly implausible."
  He concludes "that a full defense of libertarianism cannot rely upon appeals to nonaggression alone."
- **Pollution and minor infringements.** Van der Vossen & Christmas (SEP,
  §1) report an objection stated against full self-ownership (Railton
  2003; Sobel 2012): "Full self-ownership might seem to condemn as wrongful even very minor infringements of the personal sphere, such as when innocuous particles of pollution fall upon an unconsenting person."
  "Thus, this objection goes, self-ownership places such strong protections for our liberty, it makes us mutually unable to act on our liberty."
  The entry states this for self-ownership, not under the name "NAP".
- **The anarchist challenge to the minimal state.** Zwolinski (IEP, §4),
  on minimal-state libertarians who allow taxation and a monopoly of
  protection: "Once the bright line of non-aggression has been crossed, it is difficult to find a compelling substitute."
  Childs, as annotated by Zwolinski (IEP, §6): "Argues that Ayn Rand’s defense of a minimal state is incompatible with her more basic views regarding men’s natural rights against the initiation of force, and that a proper respect for those rights requires anarcho-capitalism."
- **Modus tollens.** Zwolinski (IEP, §4): "If respect for robust rights of self-ownership and property in external goods, as libertarians understand them, entail anarcho-capitalism, why not then reject these rights rather than embrace anarcho-capitalism?"
  and "Rothbard, Nozick and other natural rights libertarians are notoriously lacking in foundational arguments to support their strong belief in these rights."
  Mack (SEP, §2.2) reports: "It is a commonplace to say that in ASU Nozick provides no foundation for his affirmation of such Lockean natural rights (Nagel 1975)."
- **Other grounds against slavery.** Zwolinski (IEP, §2.c) reports that Rothbard's claim and Nozick's eye-transplant
  concern "have been met with the response that a denial of the permissibility of slavery, communism, and eye-transplants can be made—and usually better made—on grounds other than self-ownership."

## Replies

- **Property tied to the person.** Van der Vossen & Christmas (SEP, §2):
  "Connecting external property rights to personal or bodily rights vindicates the NAP-based approaches (Christmas 2018b)."
  That is the assessment of the entry's authors, one of whom (Christmas)
  wrote the cited reply. Christmas ([2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/mopp-2017-0007),
  abstract): "A commitment to property rights and to non-aggression can both be grounded in a commitment to non-interference."
- **Pollution.** Van der Vossen & Christmas (SEP, §1) answer: "This objection, however, presupposes an implausible conception of self-ownership that its defenders need not endorse."
  "As Eric Mack (2015) puts it, a good theory of self-ownership offers people some “elbow room.”"
  Rothbard's text classifies emissions that injure as aggression: "All such emanations which injure person or property constitute aggression against the private property of the victims."
  (p. 319); the excerpt held does not address non-injurious particles.
- **Anarchist challenge.** (none recorded in the excerpts held; Nozick's
  invisible-hand argument is on [Robert Nozick](../thinkers/nozick.md).)

- **Murray Rothbard** — *For a New Liberty* (1973), p. 27. Van der Vossen
  & Christmas (SEP, preamble): "Rothbard was the major voice of libertarianism in the United States during and after his lifetime."
- **Ayn Rand** — "The Objectivist Ethics", *The Virtue of Selfishness*,

- **Billy Christmas** — defends the NAP ([2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/mopp-2017-0007)).
- **Matt Zwolinski** — defends a moderate version only ([2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/s026505251600011x)).
- Zwolinski (IEP, §1) cautions that there is "probably no single principle or set of principles on which all libertarians can agree."
  No survey figure was found.

## Contrasts

- **Hayek's consequentialist route** (Zwolinski, IEP, §3.b): the same
  practical rule, but "not a moral axiom".
- **Negative liberty.** Zwolinski (IEP, §1) lists the commitment "that liberty, understood as non-interference, is the only thing that can be legitimately demanded of others as a matter of legal or political right;"
  and annotates Rothbard's "Isaiah Berlin on Negative Freedom" (1982) as "a libertarian criticism of this distinction and Berlin’s argument for it."
  See [Liberty, positive and negative](../vocabulary/liberty-positive-and-negative.md).
- **Kantian inviolability.** Mack (SEP, §2.2): "Nozick follows suit in appropriating this Kantian language (see Cohen 1995: 238–243)."
  See [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md).
- **Nozick's side constraints and Rothbard's axiom.** The point of
  divergence in the texts held: Nozick leaves open an exception for
  "catastrophic moral horror" (p. 30 n.), while Rothbard speaks of "the absolute right to be “free” from aggression"
  (p. 27); and Rothbard's State is "one central, dominant, and overriding aggressor"
  (p. 28), while Nozick holds the minimal state "justified" (p. ix).
  The juxtaposition is a structural note the implant adds (manifest
  [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)).

# Golden Rule

A [positions](index.md) page under [Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md),
graded by [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).
Religious attributions are the texts' and the cited scholars' claims.

## Thesis

Puka (IEP, "The Golden Rule", preamble): "The most familiar version of the Golden Rule says, “Do unto others as you would have them do unto you.”"
Gensler's reformulation: "Treat others only as you consent to being
treated in the same situation." (Gensler, harryhiker.com page and slides;
excerpts: `raw/iep-golden-rule-puka-silver-rule-singer-hare.md`,
`raw/gensler-golden-rule-consistency-masochist-judge.md`).

## Answers the problem

How one ought to treat others: the rule is offered as a single principle
of conduct. Puka (§6) reports that
Singer (1963) and Hare (1975) treat it as a principle for
"generalization across cases, situations, and all varieties of societies".

## Canonical statements

Quoted from public-domain translations; locators are those of the named
edition (excerpts: `raw/golden-rule-texts-analects-leviticus-hillel-mahabharata-shaw.md`,
`raw/legge-analects-ren-li-junzi-xiao-reciprocity-names.md`,
`raw/kjv-gospels-sermon-on-the-mount-golden-rule-great-commandments.md`).

- **[Confucius](../thinkers/confucius.md), Analects 15.23 (Legge's
  numbering; the Legge excerpt notes that his chapter divisions
  occasionally differ by one from other editions):** to the question "Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?" the
  Master answers: "Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others."
- **Analects 12.2 (Legge):** part of the answer on "perfect virtue":
  "not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself".
- **Analects 5.11 (Legge):** the disciple states it and is told he has not
  reached it: "Tsze-kung said, 'What I do not wish men to do to me, I also wish not to do to men.' The Master said, 'Ts'ze, you have not attained to that.'"
- **Leviticus 19:18 (KJV):** "19:18 Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD."
- **Hillel, Babylonian Talmud Shabbat 31a (Rodkinson tr. 1903, Vol. I
  pp. 49–50; folio per the Soncino page):** to a Gentile who asked to be
  taught "the whole Torah while I stand on one foot": "What is hateful to thee, do not unto thy fellow; this is the whole law. All the rest is a commentary to this law; go and learn it."
- **[Jesus](../thinkers/jesus.md) as reported in Matthew 7:12 (KJV):**
  "7:12 Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets."
- **Luke 6:31 (KJV):** "6:31 And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise."
- **Mahabharata, Anusasana Parva, Section CXIII (Ganguli tr.), Vrihaspati
  to Yudhishthira:** "One should never do that to another which one regards as injurious to one's own self. This, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness."

Gensler's page reports the reach of the rule as its holders see it: "The golden rule is endorsed by all the great world religions; Jesus , Hillel, and Confucius used it to summarize their ethical teachings."
Puka (§3) reports the same as a reputation: "The golden rule is much-reputed for being the most culturally universal ethical tenet in human history."

## Varieties

- **Positive and negative forms.** Puka (§1): "Its corollary, the so-called “silver rule,” focuses on restraint and non-harm: “do nothing to others you would not have done to you.”"
  Structural note (manifest G4): of the statements above, Analects 5.11,
  12.2, 15.23, Hillel and the Mahabharata passage are worded negatively
  ("do not do"), Matthew 7:12 and Luke 6:31 positively ("do ye").
- **The rule and the love command.** Puka (§1) separates them: "Yet the “love thy neighbor” maxim far exceeds the golden rule in its moral expectations. It stresses loving identification with others while the golden rule merely advises equal treatment."
  On Leviticus 19:18 he writes (§3): "No fair reading of Levitticus XIX: 18 would term its statement the golden rule, not in our modern sense, first stated in Matthew 7:12."
- **The literal rule.** Gensler's name for the unqualified form: "If you want X to do something to you, then do this same thing to X."
- **The consistency reading (Gensler).** "The golden rule is best seen as a consistency principle." It "only prescribes consistency - that we not have our actions (toward another) be out of harmony with our desires (toward a reversed situation action)."
  The "same situation" clause includes the other's preferences: "To apply it, you'd imagine yourself on the receiving end of the action in the exact place of the other person (which includes having the other person's likes and dislikes)."
- **The procedural reading (Marcus G. Singer).** Puka (§6) reports: "Singer argues further that the golden rule is a procedural principle, directing us through a process—perspective-taking, either real or imaginary, for example—to generate morally salient action directives."
  (Singer, "The Golden Rule", *Philosophy* 38 (146), 1963, pp. 293–314,
  [doi:10.1017/s0031819100058290](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0031819100058290); not read here.)
- **The universalizability reading ([Hare](../thinkers/hare.md)).** Price
  (SEP "Richard Mervyn Hare", Fall 2024, §4; excerpts:
  `raw/sep-hare-fall-2024-golden-rule-argument-debtors-fanatic.md`): in *Freedom and Reason*
  (1963) "the formal features of prescriptivity and universalizability generate a “Golden Rule” form of argument"
  "(as stated in Luke 6:31 (KJV), this ran, “And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise”)".
  Puka (§6): "R. M. Hare basically places the golden rule in the company of the Kantian and Utilitarian theories, or his own “universal prescriptivism.”"
- **The historical and religious reading (Wattles).** Gensler describes it: "Jeff Wattles's The Golden Rule ( Oxford 1996 ) emphasizes historical and religious aspects and thus complements my logical-rational approach."
  (Jeffrey Wattles, *The Golden Rule*, Oxford University Press, 1996,
  [doi:10.1093/oso/9780195101874.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195101874.001.0001);
  bibliographic data verified by DOI; not read here.)

## The case for

- **A summary of the law.** The texts themselves present it so: Hillel's
  "this is the whole law" (Shabbat 31a), Matthew 7:12's "for this is the
  law and the prophets", the Mahabharata's "the rule of Righteousness", the
  Analects' "rule of practice for all one's life" (15.23).
- **A consistency test (Gensler).** The rule "only prescribes consistency"
  (quoted above); Hare's *Freedom and Reason* "greatly influenced my thinking on the golden rule".
- **Hare's Golden-Rule argument.** The debtors' case as Price reports it (SEP §4, citing Hare 1963: 90–1): a
  creditor who says he ought to imprison his debtor commits himself to a
  universal principle, and "B is unlikely to be willing to prescribe a likely implication of this, “Let C put me into prison”, since that would frustrate his own interests."
- **Wide reach across traditions.** Gensler's claim, quoted under
  Canonical statements.

## The case against

- **[Kant](../thinkers/kant.md), *Groundwork* 4:430, footnote** (Abbott
  tr.; Akademie page per korpora.org; excerpts:
  `raw/kant-mill-tolstoy-on-love-commandment-golden-rule-nonresistance.md`):
  "Let it not be thought that the common "quod tibi non vis fieri, etc." could serve here as the rule or principle."
  Kant gives the reasons in the same footnote: "it cannot be a universal law, for it does not contain the principle of duties to oneself, nor of the duties of benevolence to others (for many a one would gladly consent that others should not benefit him, provided only that he might be excused from showing benevolence to them), nor finally that of duties of strict obligation to one another, for on this principle the criminal might argue against the judge who punishes him, and so on."
- **Differing tastes (G. B. Shaw).** In "Maxims for Revolutionists" (1903),
  under the heading "THE GOLDEN RULE": "Do not do unto others as you would that they should do unto you. Their tastes may not be the same."
  And: "The golden rule is that there are no golden rules."
- **The masochist.** Gensler states the case against the literal rule:
  "Ima Masochist: I want to be hurt. Does GR tell me to hurt others?"
- **The judge.** Kant's "the criminal might argue against the judge"
  (above); Gensler poses the same kind of case: "A nurse giving a shot to a baby, a judge sentencing a dangerous criminal to jail."
- **Vagueness of "as you would have".** Puka (§6): "For philosophers, however, even a clarified or unbiased depiction of the golden rule cannot overcome its shortfalls in specificity and decisiveness."
  Puka (§7): "The greatest help that the golden rule’s common sense might seek from philosophy is a conceptual analysis of the “as you would have” notion (Matthew 7:12)."
- **Against the generalizing readings.** Puka (§6), of Singer's and
  Hare's: "These interpretations are highly unlikely judging from the rule’s strikingly ethnic origin and design function, as a bottom-up approach makes clear."
  Of Hare's switch from "would have" to "wish" or "glad": "Notably, no grounding is offered for this claim—for the switch from “would have” to “wish” or “glad,” as if these were obviously the same ideas."

## Replies

- **To the masochist (Gensler).** "Have her imagine herself in the place of her non-masochist victim."
  And: "Criticize Ima’s irrational desires." His slides add that satisfying
  GR-consistency does not guarantee that an action is right.
- **To differing tastes (Gensler).** His formula puts the agent in the
  other's place "which includes having the other person's likes and
  dislikes" (quoted under Varieties).
- **To the judge (Gensler).** The judge case is placed under the question
  whether one is willing that, in the same situation, this be done to
  oneself; he labels the opposite reading: "The soft GR fallacy assumes that we should always do what the other person wants."
- **Hare on the fanatic.** Price (SEP §4) reports that in *Freedom and
  Reason* Hare "allows the argument to be evaded by the “fanatic”" and that "A later tightening of the argument (first set out fully in 1972c), hoped to close off this possibility."
- **To Kant.** None recorded in the sources held.

## Who holds it

- **Texts:** those under Canonical statements (Puka, §3, disputes
  reading Leviticus 19:18 as the Golden Rule).
- **[Mill](../thinkers/mill.md)**, *Utilitarianism* ch. II (excerpt in
  `raw/kant-mill-tolstoy-on-love-commandment-golden-rule-nonresistance.md`):
  "In the golden rule of Jesus of Nazareth, we read the complete spirit of the ethics of utility."
- **[R. M. Hare](../thinkers/hare.md):** *Freedom and Reason* (1963);
  "Abortion and the Golden Rule" (1975), per Puka's reference list
  (*Philosophy and Public Affairs* 4 (3): 201–222).
- **Singer** (1963, per Puka); **Wattles** (1996); **Gensler**, *Ethics
  and the Golden Rule* (Routledge, 2013,
  [doi:10.4324/9780203154373](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203154373);
  verified by DOI, not read) and the web essays quoted. Survey
  proportions: none recorded.

## Contrasts

- **Kant's formula of humanity / categorical imperative** (sibling page
  [Categorical imperative](categorical-imperative.md)): Kant's footnote
  calls the negative rule "only a deduction from the former, though with
  several limitations" — "the former" being the formula of humanity
  (context note in the Kant excerpt). Point of divergence, per Kant: the
  rule lacks duties to oneself, duties of benevolence and strict duties.
- **Love of neighbour** (Leviticus 19:18; Matthew 22:39): see Puka under
  Varieties.
- **Retaliation:** Gensler's slides set the two apart: "“Treat others as you want to be treated” ≠ “Treat others as they treat you.”"

*Golden Rule* (positive form) and *silver rule* (negative form) as Puka
names them; *reciprocity* as Legge renders the Analects' word (15.23);
*universalizability* in Hare's sense. No vocabulary entry for these

# Categorical imperative (universalisability, Kingdom of Ends)

A position page of the [positions](index.md) branch, written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md)
and graded by [How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md).
The proponent is [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md); the objections are their
holders' and the replies theirs.

## Thesis

Johnson & Cureton (SEP "Kant's Moral Philosophy", Fall 2024, preamble):
"Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) argued that the supreme principle of morality is a principle of practical rationality that he dubbed the “Categorical Imperative” (CI)."
They gloss it (§1): "Kant characterized the CI as an objective, rationally necessary and unconditional principle that we must follow despite any natural desires we may have to the contrary."
Kant's contrast (Groundwork, Abbott tr., 4:414): "Now all imperatives command either hypothetically or categorically. The former represent the practical necessity of a possible action as means to something else that is willed (or at least which one might possibly will). The categorical imperative would be that which represented an action as necessary of itself without reference to another end, i.e., as objectively necessary."

## Canonical statements

Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Abbott tr. 1895
([Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5682), public domain).
Akademie pages located in the German text at
[korpora.org](https://korpora.org/kant/aa04/) or as the SEP authors give them.

- **Universal law (4:421):** "There is therefore but one categorical imperative, namely, this: Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
  Law-of-nature variant (4:421): "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature."
  Section I form (4:402): "I am never to act otherwise than so that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law."
- **Humanity (4:429):** "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only."
- **Autonomy (4:432, as Johnson & Cureton §7 give it):** "The third formulation of the CI is “the Idea of the will of every rational being as a will that legislates universal law.” (G 4:432)."
  Kant's definition (4:440): "Autonomy of the will is that property of it by which it is a law to itself (independently of any property of the objects of volition)."
- **Kingdom of Ends (4:433–434):** "By a kingdom I understand the union of different rational beings in a system by common laws." "It is certainly only an ideal." (4:433).
  "A rational being belongs as a member to the kingdom of ends when, although giving universal laws in it, he is also himself subject to these laws. He belongs to it as sovereign when, while giving laws, he is not subject to the will of any other." (4:433–434).
  "Morality consists then in the reference of all action to the legislation which alone can render a kingdom of ends possible." (4:434).
  "In the kingdom of ends everything has either value or dignity. Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity." (4:434; German "Preis", rendered "value" by Abbott).
- **Kingdom of Ends formula (4:439):** "Act according to the maxims of a member of a merely possible kingdom of ends legislating in it universally,"
- **On the formulas' unity (4:435, Gregor tr. as Johnson & Cureton §9 quote it):** these "are basically only so many formulations of precisely the same law, each one of them by itself uniting the other two within it".

(Excerpts: `raw/kant-1785-groundwork-4-402-4-422-universal-law-lying-promise-abbott.md`,
`raw/kant-1785-groundwork-4-414-4-440-imperatives-kingdom-of-ends-abbott.md`.)

## Varieties

- **Universal law as a decision procedure.** Johnson & Cureton (§5): "O’Neill (1975, 1989) and Rawls (1980, 1989), among others, take this formulation in effect to summarize a decision procedure for moral reasoning"
  Its steps: "First, formulate a maxim that enshrines your proposed plan of action."
  "Second, recast that maxim as a universal law of nature governing all rational agents, and so as holding that all must, by natural law, act as you yourself propose to act in these circumstances."
  "Third, consider whether your maxim is even conceivable in a world governed by this new law of nature."
  "If it is, then, fourth, ask yourself whether you would, or could, rationally will to act on your maxim in such a world."
  Failing step three yields a perfect duty "admitting “of no exception in favor of inclination”" (G 4:421, as quoted in §5); "If your maxim fails the fourth step, you have an “imperfect” duty requiring you to pursue a policy that can admit of such exceptions."
  The authors add: "Again, Kant’s interpreters differ over exactly how to reconstruct the derivation of these duties."
- **Three readings of the "contradiction".** Korsgaard ([1985](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0114.1985.tb00240.x), ms. p. 2): "There are three different interpretations of the kind of contradiction Kant has (or ought to have) in mind found in the literature."
  Logical: "if the maxim were universalized, the action or policy that it proposes would be inconceivable." (held, she reports, by Dietrichson, Kemp and Wood "among others", ms. p. 6).
  Teleological: "it would be contradictory to will your maxim as a law for a system of nature teleologically conceived" (ms. p. 2).
  Practical: "the contradiction is that your maxim would be self-defeating if universalized: your action would become ineffectual for the achievement of your purpose if everyone (tried to) use it for that purpose." (ms. p. 3).
  Korsgaard's own preference, in her words: "The argument that shows this seems to me to be an almost decisive one in favor of this interpretation." (ms. p. 21; excerpt `raw/korsgaard-1985-formula-of-universal-law-contradiction-interpretations.md`).
- **The Kingdom of Ends formula as the social form.** Johnson & Cureton (§8): "This formulation has gained favor among Kantians in recent years (see Rawls, 1971; Hill, 1972)." "Many see it as introducing more of a social dimension to Kantian morality."
  Their gloss: "The intuitive idea behind this formulation is that our fundamental moral obligation is to act only on principles which could earn acceptance by a community of fully rational agents each of whom have an equal share in legislating these principles for their community."
  (Excerpt: `raw/sep-kant-moral-fall-2024-decision-procedure-kingdom-of-ends-disability.md`.)
- **Teleological and deontological readings.** Johnson & Cureton (§14): "Perhaps the first philosopher to suggest a teleological reading of Kant was John Stuart Mill." "Several 20th century theorists have followed Mill’s suggestion, most notably, R. M. Hare."
  For the other side they report: "But a powerful argument for the deontological reading is Kant’s own apparent insistence that the authority of moral demands must come simply from their being the demands of a rational will, quite apart from the value that will may have (see Schneewind 1996; Johnson 2007, 2008; and Reath 1994)."
- **Constructivist and realist readings.** Johnson & Cureton (§15) list constructivists (Rawls, Korsgaard, O'Neill, Reath, Hill and others) and add: "Other commentators interpret Kant as a robust moral realist (Ameriks 2003; Wood 1999; Langton 2007; Kain 2004)."
- **Universalisability as a broader thesis.** Jollimore (SEP "Impartiality", Fall 2024, §4.2): "On many deontological views, particularly Kantian ones, the significance of moral impartiality is seen as arising from the fact that a core role is given to the concept of universalizability (Gert 1998; Hare 1981; Kant 1964 [1785]; Kohlberg 1979)."
  "The requirement that moral judgments be universalizable is, roughly, the requirement that such judgments be independent of any particular point of view."
  [Hare](../thinkers/hare.md), as Price reports (SEP "Richard Mervyn Hare", §4): "The two features of prescriptivity and universalizability remained the twin pillars of Hare’s theory ever afterwards." In *Freedom and Reason* (1963) they "generate a “Golden Rule” form of argument" (excerpt `raw/sep-hare-fall-2024-prescriptivism-universalizability-levels.md`).
  Marcus G. Singer's "Generalization in Ethics" ([Mind 1955](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lxiv.255.361); book 1961) is placed by Hooker (SEP "Rule Consequentialism", §6.2) among early compliance formulations of rule-consequentialism: "For example, they said an act is morally wrong if and only if it is forbidden by rules the compliance with which will maximize the good (or the expected good). (See Austin 1832; Brandt 1959; M. Singer 1955, 1961.)" Singer's text was not read (excerpt `raw/sep-impartiality-rule-consequentialism-fall-2024-universalizability-singer.md`).

## The case for

As proponents and sympathetic commentators state it:

- **Universalisation exposes making an exception of oneself.** Kant on the lying promise (4:422): "the promise itself would become impossible, as well as the end that one might have in view in it, since no one would consider that anything was promised to him, but would ridicule all such statements as vain pretences."
  Johnson & Cureton's reconstruction (§5): "Hence, it is inconceivable that I could sincerely act on my maxim in a world in which my maxim is a universal law of nature."
  Jollimore (§4.2) on the agent whose judgments are universalisable: "Such an agent will not make an exception of herself by allowing herself to break a rule she regards as binding for others, or to perform any other action which she would not accept if performed by another agent."
- **Unconditional authority.** Johnson & Cureton (§14) quote Kant that a categorical imperative "commands a certain line of conduct directly, without assuming or being conditional on any further goal to be reached by that conduct" (G 4:416), and report the deontological argument quoted under Varieties.
- **Humanity and dignity.** Johnson & Cureton (§6): "Most philosophers who find Kant’s views attractive find them so because of the Humanity Formulation of the CI."
  Kant (4:434) distinguishes what has "value" from what has "dignity": what "admits of no equivalent, has a dignity".
- **Autonomy.** Johnson & Cureton (§10): "At the heart of Kant’s moral theory is the idea of autonomy." "As with Rousseau, whose views influenced Kant, freedom does not consist in being bound by no law, but by laws that are in some sense of one’s own making."

## The case against

As opponents and critical commentators state it:

- **Mill: no contradiction, only consequences.** Mill (*Utilitarianism*, ch. I; [Gutenberg #11224](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11224); see [Mill](../thinkers/mill.md)):
  "But when he begins to deduce from this precept any of the actual duties of morality, he fails, almost grotesquely, to show that there would be any contradiction, any logical (not to say physical) impossibility, in the adoption by all rational beings of the most outrageously immoral rules of conduct."
  He continues (as Korsgaard 1985, ms. p. 5, quotes him): "All he shows is that the consequences of their universal adoption would be such as no one would choose to incur."
  Korsgaard's gloss: "Mill thinks that Kant's view really amounts to an appeal to utility, to what we would now call rule-utilitarianism."
- **Hegel: empty formalism.** [Hegel](../thinkers/hegel.md), *Philosophy of Right* §135 Note (Dyde tr. 1896):
  "Yet if this merely moral standpoint does not pass into the conception of the ethical system, this philosophical acquisition is reduced to empty formalism, and moral science is converted into mere rhetoric about duty for duty's sake."
  "The rule that there should be no private property contains of itself no contradiction, nor does the proposition that this or that particular nation or family should not exist, or that no one should live at all."
  §135 Addition: "But in the Kantian theory the rule is not to hand, and the criterion that there should be no contradiction produces nothing."
  (Excerpt `raw/hegel-1820-philosophy-of-right-dyde-preface-punishment-formalism-ethical-life.md`.) Brooks (SEP "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy", Fall 2024): "Hegel’s criticisms centre on Kant’s Formula of Universal Law stating we should “act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law” (Kant 1997, 4:421)."
  Korsgaard (1985, ms. p. 12) reports the 1803 essay's point: "The interesting question, Hegel goes on, is just why there should be property, and about this Kant says nothing."
- **Too strong (Bradley).** Korsgaard (ms. p. 13): "The other Hegelian objection pulls in the opposite direction: instead of showing the test to be empty, it shows it to be too strong." Bradley, as she quotes him: "In short, every duty which presupposes something to be negated is no duty; it is an immoral rule, because self-contradictory."
- **Counterexamples ("false positives and negatives", in Perkins's phrase).** Korsgaard (ms. p. 8), building on Dietrichson's case: "We can make the maxim one of killing children that tend to cry at night more than average, in order to get enough sleep." "Either Dietrichson's maxim or mine could clearly be a universal law without a logical contradiction."
  Perkins (2008, abstract) names the general problem: "In this paper, I discuss some counterintuitive results of Kant’s Formula of Universal Law (FUL), known as false positives and negatives." He reports drawing them from Wood: "To do this I investigate examples of false results drawn from the work of Allen W. Wood and identify flaws in their maxims." McCarty's "False Negatives of the Categorical Imperative" ([Mind 2015](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzu151)) is on the same topic; only its record was read (excerpt `raw/perkins-2008-mccarty-2015-false-positives-negatives-records.md`).
- **Rigorism on lying.** Korsgaard ([1986](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139174503.006), p. 327): "Unsympathetic readers are inclined to take them as evidence of the horrifying conclusions to which Kant was led by his notion that the necessity in duty is rational necessity" — see [the murderer at the door](../problems/murderer-at-the-door.md).
- **Who is in the Kingdom of Ends.** Johnson & Cureton (§11): "One of the most important criticisms of Kant’s moral theory concerns human beings with severe cognitive disabilities who lack the moral capacities and dispositions that, according to Kant, are needed for people to have dignity, be ends in themselves, possess moral rights, legislate moral laws, be a member of the kingdom of ends, or otherwise have basic moral status (Kittay 2005, Vorhaus 2020, Barclay 2020; cf. the SEP entry cognitive disability and moral status )."
- **A hypothetical imperative in disguise.** [Schopenhauer](../thinkers/schopenhauer.md) (*The Basis of Morality*, II.vi, Bullock tr.): "From this analysis it is abundantly clear that Kant's famous leading principle is not--as he maintains with tireless repetition--a categorical, but in reality a hypothetical Imperative;" (excerpt `raw/schopenhauer-1840-basis-of-morality-compassion-kant-animals-bullock.md`).

## Replies

- **To Mill.** Johnson & Cureton (§9): "The universal law formula is not itself derived, as some of Kant’s interpreters have suggested, from the principle of non-contradiction." Korsgaard (1985, ms. p. 26) on her practical reading: "But on the Practical Contradiction Interpretation it is the failure of efficacy, not the non-existence, that really matters."
- **To Hegel and Bradley.** Korsgaard (ms. p. 23) says the practical reading "shows even more clearly why those objections miss the moral point of a universalization test."
  Brooks (SEP, Morality) assesses: "But, of course, as Kant’s defenders are correct to point out, Hegel’s criticism is unfair and misses its mark (Wood 1989)." He also grants: "Undoubtedly, Kant does offer Hegel some ammunition for this criticism." (Both are Brooks's assessments; excerpt `raw/sep-hegel-social-political-fall-2024-emptiness-charge-against-kant.md`.)
- **To the counterexamples.** Korsgaard (ms. p. 12) takes them as the hardest case for one reading only: "In my view, it is the difficulty about natural actions which is most damaging to the Logical Contradiction Interpretation." Perkins (2008, [abstract](https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/sophia/article/view/11991)) states: "I argue that the cause of at least some of these false results is the improper formation of maxims." (abstract read; paper not read.)
- **To rigorism.** Korsgaard (1986, p. 330): "It is permissible to lie to deceivers in order to counteract the intended results of their deceptions, for the maxim of lying to a deceiver is universalizable." She adds (p. 337): "This result impugns Kant's belief that the formulas are equivalent."
- **To the disability objection.** Johnson & Cureton (§11) report two replies: "One approach is simply to bite the bullet by admitting that people with certain severe cognitive disabilities lack the basic moral status that others of us share (Wood 1998, Sussman 2001."; and "A second approach to addressing the problem of moral status for those with significant cognitive disabilities is to emphasize passages in which Kant says all human beings have dignity or are ends in themselves (G 4:428–29; MM 6:410)"

## Who holds it

- [Kant](../thinkers/kant.md): *Groundwork* (1785) Second Section, 4:414–440.
- Interpreters and defenders named above, each with the work cited: Korsgaard (1985, 1986; *Creating the Kingdom of Ends*, 1996), O'Neill and [Rawls](../thinkers/rawls.md) (the decision-procedure reading, per Johnson & Cureton §5), Wood (Brooks's citation of Wood 1989), Hill (1972, per §8).
- Universalisability without Kant's formulas: [Hare](../thinkers/hare.md) (*Freedom and Reason*, 1963); M. G. Singer (1955, 1961), as Hooker reports. Hare's reading of Kant, per Johnson & Cureton (§14): "Thus, in his view, the CI is “simply utilitarianism put into other words” (1993, p. 103)."
- No survey proportion for this position is held in the implant's sources.

## Contrasts

- **Golden Rule.** Kant (4:430 footnote, Abbott tr.) denies that "quod tibi non vis fieri" can serve as the principle: "it cannot be a universal law, for it does not contain the principle of duties to oneself, nor of the duties of benevolence to others" (excerpt `raw/kant-mill-tolstoy-on-love-commandment-golden-rule-nonresistance.md`); see [Golden Rule](golden-rule.md). Hare's universalisability, by contrast, yields a "Golden Rule" form of argument (Price §4).
- **Utilitarianism.** The point of divergence, per Johnson & Cureton (§14), is whether the formula tests "the consequences of universal adoption of a maxim" (Mill's reading) or the form of a rational will (the deontological reading).
- **Rawls's original position.** Freeman (SEP "Original Position", §2): "Unlike Kant’s categorical imperative procedure, the original position is designed to represent the predominantly social bases of justice." (excerpt `raw/sep-original-position-fall-2024-veil-maximin-critics.md`).
- **Hegel's ethical life.** Hegel (§135 Note) says the moral standpoint must "pass into the conception of the ethical system", else it is "reduced to empty formalism".
- **Autonomy.** See [Autonomy](../vocabulary/autonomy.md) for the term.

## Vocabulary

*Maxim*: "The form of a maxim is “I will A in C in order to realize or produce E” where “A” is some act type, “C” is some type of circumstance, and “E” is some type of end to be realized or achieved by A in C." (Johnson & Cureton §5).
*Hypothetical imperative*: "A hypothetical imperative is thus a command in a conditional form." (§4).
*Perfect / imperfect duty*, *contradiction in conception / in the will*: as in Varieties.
*Universalisability* is used here in Jollimore's rough sense quoted under Varieties; spelling follows the Outline of ethics, quotations keep their sources' "universalizability".
*Kingdom of Ends* translates "Reich der Zwecke" (4:433). See also [moral patient](../vocabulary/moral-patient.md) for the status question raised in §11.

## Thesis

Mill, *On Liberty* ch. I: "That principle is, that the sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection. That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant."

Stanton-Ife (§4) calls it "Far and away the best-known proposal for a principled limit to the law is the “harm principle” from John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty." and reads it as excluding three other grounds: "At least as far as Mill’s canonical statement is concerned, moralism, offence and paternalism are ruled out as legitimate bases for legal coercion."

## Answers the problem

What may a society, by law or by public opinion, legitimately compel an
individual to do or not do? Mill states the scope as "the dealings of society with the individual in the way of compulsion and control, whether the means used be physical force in the form of legal penalties, or the moral coercion of public opinion" (ch. I).
Stanton-Ife (§4) frames it as the question of a "principled
limit to the law". No problem page for this question exists yet in the
implant.

## Canonical statements

- **Mill, ch. I:** "The object of this Essay is to assert one very simple principle, as entitled to govern absolutely the dealings of society with the individual in the way of compulsion and control, whether the means used be physical force in the form of legal penalties, or the moral coercion of public opinion."
- **Mill, ch. I:** "The only part of the conduct of any one, for which he is amenable to society, is that which concerns others. In the part which merely concerns himself, his independence is, of right, absolute." "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."
- **Mill, ch. I (scope):** "It is, perhaps, hardly necessary to say that this doctrine is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties."
- **Mill, ch. IV:** "As soon as any part of a person's conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion."
- **Feinberg 1984** (*Harm to Others*, p. 26, as quoted by Stanton-Ife §4): "Harm to others: It is always a good reason in support of penal legislation that it would be effective in preventing (eliminating, reducing) harm to persons other than the actor (the one prohibited from acting) and that there is probably no other means that is equally effective at no greater cost to other values. (Feinberg 1984: 26)"

Feinberg's work is *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law*, 4 vols. (New
York: Oxford University Press): vol. 1 *Harm to Others*
([doi:10.1093/0195046641.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0195046641.001.0001)),

## Varieties

Stanton-Ife (§4) tabulates four accounts by which grounds they admit for
legal coercion, and states that "No one of them has an account with
conclusions identical to any of the others":

| Account | Harm to others | Offence to others | Harm to self | Legal moralism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mill | Yes | No | No | No |
| Feinberg | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Hart | Yes | Yes, can be | Yes, can be | No |
| Raz | Yes | No | Yes, can be | No |

His comment on the table: "What unites them all is the rejection of legal moralism." He judges that "The dramatic differences between major proponents of the harm principle makes it misleading to speak of the harm principle as one principle, although one suspects the term is too far embedded in the debate to be dislodged."

- **Feinberg's "Liberal Position"** (as quoted by Stanton-Ife §4): "The Liberal Position: The harm and offense principles, duly clarified and qualified, between them exhaust the class of good reasons for criminal prohibitions." His offense principle: "Offense to others: It is always a good reason in support of a proposed criminal prohibition that it is probably necessary to prevent serious offense to persons other than the actor and would probably be an effective means to that end if enacted. (Feinberg 1984: 26)"
- **Weak and strong sufficiency.** Brink (§3.6) separates two readings of how far harm justifies regulation: "Weak Sufficiency: Harm to others is a pro tanto justification of regulation. Strong Sufficiency: Harm to others is a conclusive justification of regulation." Brink's own assessment: "Once we distinguish these options, there is a pretty compelling case for thinking that Mill rejects strong sufficiency but embraces weak sufficiency."
- **Three conceptions of harm** (Stanton-Ife §§5.1–5.3): harm as other-regarding conduct (from Mill ch. I); harm as setback to interests — "Feinberg’s defines harm as “those states of set-back interest that are the consequence of wrongful acts or omissions by others” (Feinberg 1984: 215)."; harm as setback to autonomy, Raz's "“To harm a person is to diminish his prospects, to affect adversely his possibilities” (Raz 1986: 414;" ([*The Morality of Freedom*, doi:10.1093/0198248075.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/0198248075.001.0001)).

## The case for

Each argument is stated as its proponents state it.

- **Self-knowledge of one's own good.** Mill, ch. IV: "He is the person most interested in his own well-being". Brink (§3.7) reconstructs this as Mill's second reason against paternalism: "Second, even well intentioned rulers will misidentify the good of citizens."
- **Public error.** Mill, ch. IV: "But the strongest of all the arguments against the interference of the public with purely personal conduct, is that when it does interfere, the odds are that it interferes wrongly, and in the wrong place." Brink (§3.7) adds Mill's first reason: "First, state power is liable to abuse."
- **Utility, not abstract right.** Mill, ch. I: "It is proper to state that I forego any advantage which could be derived to my argument from the idea of abstract right, as a thing independent of utility." "I regard utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions; but it must be utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being."
- **Against legal moralism (Hart on Devlin).** Hart rejects Devlin's claim that society may enforce its shared morality to avoid disintegration; Devlin, he says, moves "from the acceptable proposition that some shared morality is essential to the existence of any society to the unacceptable proposition that a society is identical with its morality as that is at any given moment of its history, so that a change in its morality is tantamount to a destruction of society. (Hart 1963 [1982: 51])" (*Law, Liberty and Morality*, 1963, quoted by Stanton-Ife §3).

## The case against

Each objection is stated as its holders state it.

- **Legal moralism (Devlin).** Devlin holds that immorality by common standards gives society a "prima facie right to legislate" (Devlin 1965: 11, *The Enforcement of Morals*, as quoted by Stanton-Ife §1), and "that it is not possible to set theoretical limits to the power of the State to legislate against immorality. It is not possible to settle in advance exceptions to the general rule or to define inflexibly areas of morality into which the law is in no circumstances to be allowed to enter. (Devlin 1965: 12–13)" Stanton-Ife (§3) reports his ground: "If a society’s morality is not sufficiently enforced the society will “disintegrate”; and the society accordingly has a right to defend itself against attacks on that morality (Devlin 1965: 10)." Stanton-Ife (§1) adds that "While many of the responses to Devlin have been heavily critical, the approach is alive and well and continues to develop in the hands, among others, of self-declared legal moralists, such as Michael Moore and Antony Duff".
- **No conduct is purely self-regarding.** Mill states the objection himself (ch. IV): "The distinction here pointed out between the part of a person's life which concerns only himself, and that which concerns others, many persons will refuse to admit." "No person is an entirely isolated being; it is impossible for a person to do anything seriously or permanently hurtful to himself, without mischief reaching at least to his near connections, and often far beyond them." Hart reports critics (Stanton-Ife §5.1 names James Fitzjames Stephen 1873 as likely among them) who "have urged that the line which Mill attempts to draw between actions with which the law may interfere and those with which it may not is illusory. “No man is an island”; and in an organised society it is impossible to identify classes of actions which harm no one or no one but the individual who does them. (Hart 1963 [1982: 5])"
- **What counts as harm.** Stanton-Ife (§5): "Proponents of the harm principle often proceed without supplying any definition or understanding of “harm”." On the interest conception: "This Mill-inspired move of Rees and Feinberg, of defining harm in terms of interests, smacks of defining one unclear concept in terms of another unclear concept (Miller 2010: 119)." On Feinberg's wrongful-harm qualification (§4): "For example, it might be claimed that it is the wrongs that are doing the essential explanatory work, while harm is a redundant or near-redundant addition." On Raz's autonomy conception (§5.3), Stanton-Ife argues that animals and persons lacking the capacity for autonomy can be harmed, which it does not cover.
- **Paternalism.** Hart, as Stanton-Ife (§4) reports: "Hart indeed accuses Mill of carrying “his protests against paternalism to lengths that may now appear to us fantastic” (Hart 1963 [1982: 32])." Raz, likewise: "Raz in turn speaks of his “ready embrace of various paternalistic measures”, asserting it is “senseless to formulate either a general pro- or a general anti-paternalistic conclusion”". Brink (§3.7), assessing Mill's two reasons: "In particular, these arguments provide no principled objection to paternalism—no objection to successful paternalistic restrictions on B’s liberty that do in fact benefit B."
- **Offence.** Mill (ch. V) allows that public acts may be prohibited as "offences against others": "Of this kind are offences against decency; on which it is unnecessary to dwell, the rather as they are only connected indirectly with our subject, the objection to publicity being equally strong in the case of many actions not in themselves condemnable, nor supposed to be so." Brink (§3.8) judges: "It seems impossible to square this with Mill’s blanket prohibition on offense regulation."
- **Sufficiency.** Brink (§3.6): "But notice that if Mill rejects strong sufficiency then this compromises his one very simple principle."
- **Utilitarian foundation.** Stanton-Ife (§6.1): "At first sight, the principle of utility looks like a straightforward rival to the harm principle, not its sturdy foundation."

## Replies

Objections and replies side by side; each reply is its owner's.

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| Legal moralism (Devlin) | Hart's disintegration reply (case for, above). Stanton-Ife (§3): "Since Hart’s critique, few have found the relativist aspect of Devlin’s account convincing (Hart 1963 [1982: 17–24])." He adds an objection of his own: the survival of an illegitimate society (his example: apartheid South Africa) would not make its enforced morality right. Devlin for his part requires "toleration of the maximum individual freedom that is consistent with the integrity of society" (Devlin 1965: 16, via Stanton-Ife §2.2). |
| No conduct is purely self-regarding | Mill (ch. IV) concedes the spill-over and draws the line at obligation: "When, by conduct of this sort, a person is led to violate a distinct and assignable obligation to any other person or persons, the case is taken out of the self-regarding class". Hart keeps the distinction for sexual morality, finding it "“prima facie plausible that there are actions immoral by accepted standards and yet not harmful to others” (Hart 1963 [1982: 5])" (Stanton-Ife §5.1). |
| What counts as harm | Brink (§3.6) reads Mill: "To constitute a harm, an action must be injurious or set back important interests of particular people, interests in which they have rights (I 12; III 1; IV 3, 10, 12; V 5)." Feinberg restricts the relevant harms to wrongful ones; Stanton-Ife (§4): "It is the harms that are produced by “morally indefensible conduct” that matter (Feinberg 1984: 215)." |
| Paternalism | Feinberg limits his anti-paternalism to the hard form (Stanton-Ife §4): "It is the “hard” variety that Feinberg sets himself against, the forcible coercion of a person against her will for her own good." Mill's bridge case (ch. V) already permits intervention to check what a person wants: "If either a public officer or any one else saw a person attempting to cross a bridge which had been ascertained to be unsafe, and there were no time to warn him of his danger, they might seize him and turn him back, without any real infringement of his liberty; for liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river." G. Dworkin ([SEP Fall 2024 "Paternalism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/paternalism/) §2.1; excerpt `raw/sep-paternalism-fall-2024-definition-soft-paternalism.md`) uses this example for soft paternalism: "Soft paternalism is the view that the only conditions under which state paternalism is justified is when it is necessary to determine whether the person being interfered with is acting voluntarily and knowledgeably." |
| Offence | Mill confines the exception to acts "done publicly", which "coming thus within the category of offences against others may rightfully be prohibited" (ch. V). Feinberg makes offence a separate ground beside harm (Varieties, above). |
| Sufficiency | Mill (ch. IV) makes harm a ground of jurisdiction, not a conclusive warrant ("becomes open to discussion"); Brink's weak-sufficiency reading (Varieties). |
| Utilitarian foundation | Stanton-Ife (§6.1): "The path to reconciling the harm principle with utilitarianism is often thought to be some form of indirect utilitarianism (Gray 1996; Mulgan 2007: 117–119)." |

## Who holds it

- [John Stuart Mill](../thinkers/mill.md), *On Liberty* (1859), ch. I.
- Joel Feinberg, *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law* (1984–1990), with the offense principle added; Brink (§3.9): "Other Millian liberals, such as Feinberg, treat the rejection of legal moralism as a constitutive commitment of liberalism."
- H. L. A. Hart, *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963), and Joseph Raz, *The Morality of Freedom* (1986), in forms that admit some paternalism. Stanton-Ife (§9): "Adherents of the harm principle also manifest a strong divide between those who broadly inherit Mill’s principled anti-moralism without his anti-paternalism, such as Hart and Raz and those who inherit both Mill’s principled anti-moralism and his anti-paternalism, such as Feinberg."

No survey proportions recorded.

## Contrasts

- **Legal moralism** (Devlin; Moore, Duff). The point of divergence is
  whether immorality without harm is a reason for coercion. Stanton-Ife
  (§1): "In the 1960s Herbert Hart applied the label “legal moralist” to his intellectual sparring partner, Lord Patrick Devlin, and the label stuck (Hart 1963; Devlin 1965)." Brink (§3.9) on the debate's shared premise: "For the most part, both sides conceded that these activities were immoral but harmless and debated whether it could be permissible to regulate them as a matter of enforcing sexual morality."
- **Legal paternalism.** Divergence on harm to self. G. Dworkin (SEP
  preamble): "Paternalism is the interference of a state or an individual with another person, against their will, and defended or motivated by a claim that the person interfered with will be better off or protected from harm."
- **Offence principle.** Divergence on whether offence without harm is a
  ground (Feinberg yes, Mill's canonical statement no — Stanton-Ife §4).

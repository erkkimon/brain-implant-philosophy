# Straw man

Family: misrepresentation. Written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md):
each classification belongs to the theory that makes it. Sources are
excerpted in `raw/` under the names given.

## Also known as

- *Straw man fallacy* — the textbook name; presented in the SEP entry as "a
  variation of *ignoratio elenchi*", the irrelevant conclusion
  ([Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/),
  §1 item 8; excerpt: `raw/sep-fallacies-fall-2024-excerpts.md`).
- *Weak man* — the name for the *selection* form: answering the weakest
  real version of an opposing view as if it were the view. The form is
  introduced in [Talisse & Aikin 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10503-006-9017-8)
  (*Argumentation* 20, 345–352; excerpt: `raw/talisse-aikin-2006-weak-man.md`),
  which calls it "the selection form" throughout; the label "weak man" is
  first attached to it in Aikin & Casey 2011 (p. 88: "They call this the
  selection form of straw man, or the weak man"). The gap between popular
  attribution and the text is a finding of this implant's reading.
- *Hollow man* — Aikin & Casey's name for the case where the position
  answered is held by no one at all: "fabricating an imaginary opponent
  with an imaginary and impossibly weak argument, and then defeating the
  argument" ([Aikin & Casey 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10503-010-9199-y),
  *Argumentation* 25, 87–105, p. 92; excerpt:
  `raw/aikin-casey-2011-hollow-man.md`).
- *Strawmanning* — the verb in everyday and online use; the opposite move
  is *steelmanning*, treated in
  [Steelman a position](../../skills/steelman-a-position.md).

## Mechanism

As described by named sources, in their own vocabulary:

- **Substitution.** The SEP entry: the move "occurs when an opponent's
  point of view is distorted in order to make it easier to refute", and
  "two things went wrong: the proponent does not hold (b), and even if she
  did, the falsity of (b) does not imply the falsity of (a)" (§1 item 8).
  The mechanism is a swap of the target followed by a real refutation of
  the substitute, with the audience left to transfer the refutation back.
- **Two forms.** Talisse & Aikin start from the "widely accepted
  characterization" — "one commits the straw man fallacy when one
  misrepresents an opponent's position in a way that imputes to it
  implausible commitments, and then refutes the misrepresentation instead
  of the opponent's actual view" (2006, p. 345) — and add a second form:
  a proponent "can misrepresent the current dialectical situation not with
  regard to what her current opponent B says, but with regard to the
  variety and strength of opposition to her view, of which B may only be
  a questionably relevant representative" (p. 346). "Hence there are two
  forms of the straw man: the representation form, which is captured in
  the standard analysis of the fallacy, and the selection form" (p. 347).
  In the selection form the proponent "correctly presents B's argument
  and legitimately refutes it, but she fails to countenance stronger
  objections from other sources" (p. 348).
- **A family on a spectrum.** Aikin & Casey 2011 arrange the three: "the
  straw man, where the opponent is real but the argument is distorted, or
  the weak man, where both the opponent and the argument are real but the
  overall state of play in the dialogue is distorted, with the hollow
  man, the argument and sometimes the opponent are completely fake"
  (p. 92). Their abstract adds that "there can be non-fallacious cases of
  any of these species of straw man arguments" (p. 87).
- **Meta-argument.** Aikin & Casey, as reported by the SEP entry, treat the straw man as a fallacy "of meta-argumentation": it happens "when someone
  in the course of argumentation inaccurately and unfavourable distorts a
  ground-level argument in an attempt to refute it or discredit its
  source" (Hansen, SEP Fall 2024, §4.3, on Finocchiaro's ground-level /
  meta-argument distinction; the book is
  [Aikin & Casey 2022](https://doi.org/10.5040/9781350065031)).
  The fault is not in any premise-to-conclusion step but in the
  argument *about* the opponent's argument.

## Example

The SEP entry's own (§1 item 8): against a proponent's view that "(a)
industrialization is the cause of global warming, an opponent might
substitute the proposition that (b) all ills that beset mankind are due to
industrialization and then, having easily shown that (b) is false, leave
the impression that (a), too, is false."

A constructed selection-form case, labelled as constructed: a debater
answers "the case for a carbon tax" by quoting the least informed comment
on a public thread and refuting it.

## Classified as a fallacy by

- **The standard treatment** — the SEP entry lists it among the core
  informal fallacies, as a variation of *ignoratio elenchi* (§1).
- **Walton** — among the "informal paralogisms", the class of fallacies
  that are errors rather than deliberate deceptions (the sophisms), on his
  1995 division as reported by the SEP entry (§3.6).
- **Talisse & Aikin** — classify both forms as fallacies (2006, p. 347);
  their contribution is that the standard analysis misses the second,
  which they judge "among the most prevalent forms of fallacious
  argumentation at work in contemporary popular political discourse"
  (p. 349).
- **Aikin & Casey** — classify all three as "a family of related forms of
  misrepresentation of the opposition in a critical dialogue" which,
  "when fallacious, function as impediments to further critical
  engagement with opponents" (2011, p. 104), and in the 2022 book as
  fallacies of meta-argumentation.
- **Pragma-dialectics** — on this theory "each of the core fallacies can
  be assigned a place as a violation of one of the rules of a critical
  discussion", and the rules "are not just rules of logic or epistemology,
  but rules of conduct for rational discussants" (Hansen, SEP Fall 2024,
  §3.5, on [van Eemeren & Grootendorst 1987](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00136779)).
  Which rule the straw man breaks is not stated in the excerpt this
  implant holds; the theory's own rule lists include one requiring an
  attack to bear on the standpoint actually advanced, and the implant has
  not yet excerpted that rule's primary wording.

## Classified as legitimate by

No source on this page classifies misrepresenting an opponent as a
legitimate move. Two neighbouring moves *are* defended, and are the ones a
straw-man charge is most often answered with:

- **Reconstruction.** Informal logic reconstructs arguments — supplies
  missing premises, sharpens wording — as routine practice; the
  discipline is to do it under the principle of charity
  ([Groarke, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/logic-informal/),
  §3.1; excerpt: `raw/sep-informal-logic-fall-2024-charity.md`). A
  reconstruction that the opponent would not accept is where charity ends
  and the representation form begins; where exactly that line is drawn is
  contested case by case.
- **Answering the argument actually made.** Answering a weak argument
  that someone did make is not classified as a fallacy by any source
  here; on Talisse & Aikin's account the proponent in the selection form
  "correctly presents B's argument and legitimately refutes it" and the
  error is that "she fails to countenance stronger objections from other
  sources" (2006, p. 348). Aikin & Casey go further: "there are formally
  similar maneuvers in dialogue that contribute positively to rational
  resolution of a dispute" (2011, p. 104) — each of the three forms has
  cases they classify as non-fallacious (p. 87).

## Critique

Published objections, attributed:

- **Of the standard definition's coverage.** Talisse & Aikin 2006 is
  itself a critique: the "standard analysis" catches only
  misrepresentation and so misses the weak man, which they describe as
  the more common form in public political argument. Aikin & Casey extend
  the same critique to the hollow man.
- **Of "fallacy" as the frame.** Hamblin's charge against the standard
  treatment as a whole (as quoted in Hansen, SEP Fall 2024, §2.10) applies
  here: the textbook list gives no criterion for when a paraphrase becomes
  a distortion. The meta-argument analysis is one answer to that; the
  pragma-dialectical rule is another.
- **Of the reasonableness of charity.** The SEP informal-logic entry
  presents charity as attributing the "logical minimum" needed to connect
  premises to conclusion (§3.1). Critics in that literature hold that
  maximal charity can misrepresent in the other direction — building a
  better argument than the arguer made; the implant has not excerpted a
  primary source for this objection and flags it as reported at second
  hand.
- **A structural note the implant adds**, and owns as its own comparison
  ([manifest G4](../../vision/manifest.md)): every theory on this page
  agrees the move is bad and disagrees only about *what it is a bad
  instance of* — an irrelevant conclusion (standard treatment), a
  breached discussion rule (pragma-dialectics), an inaccurate meta-argument
  (Aikin & Casey), a hasty generalisation over arguments (the selection
  form). The disagreement matters for how to answer it, below.

## How to spot it

Observable markers, from the sources:

- The refuted claim is stronger, cruder or more absolute than the claim
  the opponent made — "all ills" for "the cause" (SEP §1 item 8).
- The opponent is quoted selectively, paraphrased with "so what you're
  saying is…", or characterised rather than quoted.
- The weakest available proponent stands in for the position — a
  commenter, a marginal advocate, an early draft — while stronger statements
  of the same position go unmentioned: "a relatively weak version of, or
  inept spokesman for, the opposition" (Talisse & Aikin 2006, p. 347).
- No named proponent at all: Aikin & Casey's own marker is "a vague
  phrase such as 'some say'", which "often (but not always) indicates
  that no one can be found for attribution" (2011, p. 93).
- The conclusion drawn is about the *position* or its *holders*, not about
  the argument answered — the transfer step the SEP entry identifies as
  the second thing that "went wrong" (§1 item 8).

## How to answer it

Counter-moves the literature describes or implies:

- **Restate the original.** Put the actual claim beside the substituted
  one
  and ask which is under discussion; the SEP entry's diagnosis — "the
  proponent does not hold (b)" — is the answer in its shortest form.
- **Ask for the strongest version.** Under the principle of charity the
  reconstruction should make the argument "as credible as possible"
  ([Groarke, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/logic-informal/),
  §3.1); the [Steelman a position](../../skills/steelman-a-position.md)
  skill performs this for either side.
- **Separate the argument from the position.** Against the selection form,
  grant the weak argument's defeat and ask whether it bears on the
  position — the reply Talisse & Aikin's analysis licenses is that a
  proponent who "fails to countenance stronger objections from other
  sources" (2006, p. 348) has answered an argument, not the opposition.
- **Name the rule.** Under pragma-dialectics, where fallacies are rule
  violations in a discussion (§3.5), identify the standpoint actually
  advanced and note that the attack is on another one.
- **When you are the one reconstructing**, say so: label paraphrase as
  paraphrase, quote where the wording matters, and let the opponent
  correct the reconstruction before it is refuted — the
  [Debate](../../skills/debate.md) skill's rule.

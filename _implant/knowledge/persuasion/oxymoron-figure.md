# Oxymoron (the figure of speech)

Family: figure. "Oxymoron" here has the sense fixed by
[Oxymoron](../vocabulary/oxymoron.md) (two ordinarily opposed terms joined
on purpose); the loose sense "contradiction in terms" is reported as its
users' claim ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[template](index.md); sibling: [Framing](framing.md)). Excerpts: `raw/`.

## Also known as

- *Oxymoron*, *oxymora verba* — Lewis & Short gloss the Latin adjective
  *oxymōrus* as "acutely silly" and *oxymora verba* as "expressions which at first sight appear absurd , but which contain a concealed point"
  ([Lewis & Short 1879](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=oxymorus);
  excerpt: `raw/lewis-short-1879-oxymorus-and-strenua-inertia.md`).
- *Synoeciosis* (also *syneciosis*), *contrapositum*, *crosse copling* —
  Silva Rhetoricae lists "oxymoron" and "crosse copling" as alternative
  names of synoeciosis, "A coupling or bringing together of contraries, but not in order to oppose them to one another (as in antithesis )."
  ([Burton, *Silva Rhetoricae*](http://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/S/synoeciosis.htm);
  excerpt: `raw/silva-rhetoricae-synoeciosis-antithesis-paradox.md`).
- *The Crosse-couple* — Puttenham, *The Arte of English Poesie* (1589)
  III.19, sidenote "Synteiosis, or the Crosse copling"; table of Book III
  "Sineciosis, or the crossecoople", p. 172. He does not use the word
  "oxymoron"; Silva Rhetoricae makes the identification by quoting his
  verses under synoeciosis ([Gutenberg #16420](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16420);
  `raw/puttenham-1589-crosse-couple-and-figures-as-ornament.md`).
- *Acutifatuum*, "wise-folly", "A compressed paradox" — Silva Rhetoricae,
  [oxymoron](http://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/O/oxymoron.htm)
  (`raw/oxymoron-and-dilemma-definitions-silva-rhetoricae-wiktionary-etymonline.md`).
- *A paradox with a point* — Jebb's gloss on ὀξύμωρον
  ([Jebb 1900, on *Antigone* 74](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0023:text=comm:commline=74);
  excerpt: `raw/jebb-1900-antigone-74-oxymoron-paradox-with-a-point.md`).
- Not the same as *antithesis*: Silva Rhetoricae defines antithesis as "Juxtaposition of contrasting words or ideas (often, although not always, in parallel structure)."
  and lists oxymoron among its related figures; Puttenham names
  *antitheton* "the Quarreller" for its "contentious nature" (III.19,
  p. 175). Richard Lanham's handlist is not quoted here (not read).

## Mechanism

As described by named sources, in their own vocabulary:

- **Contraries made to agree.** Puttenham: the figure "takes me two contrary words, and tieth them as it were in a paire of couples, and so makes them agree like good fellowes"
  (III.19); Silva Rhetoricae: "not in order to oppose them".
- **A concealed point.** Lewis & Short: expressions that "at first sight
  appear absurd" but "contain a concealed point". Jebb: in an oxymoron
  "the qualification (“ ὅσια ”) seems contrary to the essence of the thing qualified."
  His limit: Milton's "by transgressing most truly kept the law" "is not an oxymoron, because the words, ‘most truly,’ suggest an explanation"
  — the reconciliation is left to the reader.
- **Arrest.** Fowler defines the figure as "The combining in one
  expression of two terms that are ordinarily contradictory, & whose
  exceptional coincidence is therefore arresting"
  ([Fowler 1926, p. 613](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofmode22fowl);
  excerpt: `raw/fowler-1926-modern-english-usage-oxymoron.md`).
- **Two opposite qualities producing a third.** Gellius explains
  Augustus's σπεῦδε βραδέως as recommending "at once the promptness of energy and the delay of carefulness, and it is from these two opposite qualities that maturitas springs."
  (10.11.5, Rolfe 1927; `raw/suetonius-augustus-25-4-and-gellius-10-11-speude-bradeos.md`).
- **Structural note (the implant's, [G4](../../vision/manifest.md)).** The
  figure is not the logical form *P and not-P*. That form is inconsistent
  in every truth-table row, and from it anything follows:
  `logic.py check --premises "p & ~p" --conclusion "q"` prints `VALID`,
  `premises are jointly inconsistent — argument is vacuously valid`. Every
  source above describes the terms of an oxymoron as *apparently*
  contrary, read so that both apply.

## Classified as a fallacy by

No read source classifies the figure as a fallacy (it makes no argument).
Recorded instead:

- **Figures as "trespasses in speach".** Puttenham opens his chapter on
  figures in general: "As figures be the instruments of ornament in euery language, so be they also in a sorte abuses or rather trespasses in speach, because they passe the ordinary limits of common vtterance,"
  and are used "to deceiue the eare and also the minde, drawing it from plainnesse and simplicitie to a certaine doublenesse, whereby our talke is the more guilefull & abusing" (III.7).
- **The worn oxymoron as a fault of style.** Fowler writes that the
  oxymoron in Whistler's *The Gentle Art of Making Enemies* "was what made
  it effective", but that journalists, "failing to see how he did it",
  have "reduced it to a Battered ornament" (p. 213), and lists "such
  oxymorons as The gentle art of doing something ungentle" among phrases
  at which "the average adult" is "not amused" (pp. 735–736).
- **The loose sense as a usage error.** Wiktionary's usage note: "The common vernacular use of oxymoron as simply a contradiction in terms is considered incorrect by some speakers and writers, and is perhaps best avoided in certain contexts." (wiki markup removed; the raw excerpt keeps the source text as read)
  Its sense 2 is labelled "(loosely, sometimes, proscribed)"; Etymonline:
  "Now often used loosely to mean "contradiction in terms.""
  (`raw/wiktionary-festina-lente-and-jumbo-shrimp.md`). This objection
  targets the word's use, not the figure.

## Classified as legitimate by

- **Classical and Renaissance rhetoric.** The figure is catalogued as
  ornament: Puttenham's Crosse-couple sits in Book III, "Of Ornament",
  among the "Figures sententious, otherwise called Rhetoricall"; Silva
  Rhetoricae records it among the figures, with Quintilian 9.3.81
  (*contrapositum*) as a primary source for synoeciosis.
- **Lexicography and commentary.** Lewis & Short (1879) and Jebb (1900)
  define it by its "concealed point"; Fowler (1926, p. 613) by its
  "arresting" coincidence (see Mechanism).
- **Current dictionaries.** Merriam-Webster: "a combination of
  contradictory or incongruous words (such as cruel kindness )"
  (`raw/merriam-webster-oxymoron-definition-and-faq.md`); Wiktionary sense
  1 adds "used together intentionally for effect".

**The loose sense, as its users employ it.** In it a speaker asserts that
a phrase's terms cannot both apply — recorded as that speaker's claim.
Merriam-Webster records the extension
("broadly : something (such as a concept) that is made up of contradictory
or incongruous elements") and quotes Mark Coleman in *Rolling Stone*
(1996): "The phrase "Broadway rock musical" is an oxymoron ." English
Wikipedia reports that the OED's 1902 edition records "A general meaning of
"contradiction in terms"", and that Richard Lederer (1990) calls such cases
"opinion oxymoron", describing "war games", "peacekeeping missile",
"United Nations", and "airline food" "as opinion-based, because some may disagree that they contain an internal contradiction."
(`raw/wikipedia-oxymoron-article-lederer-carlin-oed.md`; Lederer's article
itself was not reachable). The primary comic instance, George Carlin on
*Saturday Night Live* (11 October 1975): "it’s like Military Intelligence – the words don’t go together, man."
(`raw/carlin-1975-snl-jumbo-shrimp-military-intelligence.md`).

## Critique

- **Of the loose sense.** Wiktionary's usage note (above) reports that the
  "contradiction in terms" use "is considered incorrect by some speakers
  and writers"; Merriam-Webster, of calling a person an oxymoron: "While we are loath to place restrictions on language use, oxymoron usually refers to a set of contradictory words (such as bittersweet ) rather than to a contradictory person."
- **Of extending the word to accidents.** English Wikipedia separates the
  "narrow sense" from "inadvertent or incidental contradictions" ("barely clothed").
- **Of the opinion oxymoron.** Wikipedia on Lederer: the humour "derives from implying that an assumption (which might otherwise be expected to be controversial or at least non-evident) is so obvious as to be part of the lexicon." (wiki markup removed)
- **Of the boundary with paradox.** Merriam-Webster: "A paradox is a statement or argument that seems to be contradictory or to go against common sense, but that is yet perhaps still true";
  for the logical sense see [Paradox](../vocabulary/paradox.md).

## How to spot it

- Two terms "ordinarily contradictory" (Fowler) or "ordinarily opposing"
  (Silva Rhetoricae) placed together, typically adjective and noun
  ("darkness visible", "sweet sorrow") or adverb and verb ("make haste
  slowly").
- The phrase does not explain itself: Jebb's test is that a phrase which
  supplies its own reconciliation ("most truly") "is not an oxymoron".
- Deliberateness: Wiktionary's "intentionally for effect". Loose sense:
  "is an oxymoron" said of a phrase or concept (Merriam-Webster's example).

## How to answer it

- **To the figure:** no read source prescribes a rebuttal. Lewis & Short's
  "concealed point" and Gellius's gloss ("the promptness of energy and the
  delay of carefulness") model reading it: naming the respect in which each
  term holds.
- **To the loose sense:** treat *X is an oxymoron* as the claim that the
  two terms cannot both apply, and ask whether they can; Lederer's
  criterion, as Wikipedia reports it, is whether "some may disagree that
  they contain an internal contradiction". Whether a claimed contradiction
  is a formal one (*P and not-P*) can be checked with `logic.py`
  ([Classical logic](../methods/classical-logic.md)).
- **To a worn one:** Fowler's "Battered ornament" (above).

# Framing

Family: presentation. Written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md):
each classification belongs to the theory that makes it. Sources are
excerpted in `raw/` under the names given.

## Also known as

- *Decision frame* — Tversky & Kahneman's term: "the decision-maker's
  conception of the acts, outcomes, and contingencies associated with a
  particular choice"
  ([Tversky & Kahneman 1981](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683), p. 453;
  excerpt: `raw/tversky-kahneman-1981-framing-asian-disease.md`).
- *Gain/loss framing*, *risky-choice framing*, *attribute framing*, *goal
  framing* — the three types in the typology of
  [Levin, Schneider & Gaeth 1998](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2804)
  (excerpt: `raw/levin-schneider-gaeth-1998-framing-typology.md`).
- *Media framing*, *issue framing* — the communication-studies sense, in
  which a frame selects "some aspects of a perceived reality" and makes
  them more salient ([Entman 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x),
  p. 52; excerpt: `raw/entman-1993-framing-definition.md`); surveyed for
  political science by [Chong & Druckman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054).
- *Spin* — the everyday name; not a term of art in any source on this page.

## Mechanism

As described by named sources, in their own vocabulary:

- **Prospect theory.** Tversky & Kahneman distinguish "an initial phase in
  which acts, outcomes, and contingencies are framed, and a subsequent
  phase of evaluation" (1981, p. 453). Because "outcomes are commonly
  perceived as positive or negative in relation to a reference outcome
  that is judged neutral", and the value function is "concave for gains,
  convex for losses, and steeper for losses than for gains, shifts of
  reference can change the value difference between outcomes and thereby
  reverse the preference" (p. 456). The frame is set "partly by the
  formulation of the problem and partly by the norms, habits, and personal
  characteristics of the decision-maker" (p. 453) — so the persuader
  controls one of the two inputs.
- **Salience.** Entman's definition locates the mechanism in selection and
  emphasis: to frame is to make some aspects of a situation "more salient
  in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular
  problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or
  treatment recommendation" (1993, p. 52). No claim about value functions
  is made; the frame works by what it puts in front of the audience.
- **Typology.** Levin, Schneider & Gaeth argue that the mixed literature
  is mixed "because different studies have employed different operational
  definitions of framing and thus have tapped different underlying
  processes" (1998, p. 149), and separate three: *risky choice* framing,
  "the form most closely associated with the term 'framing'", in which
  "the outcomes of a potential choice involving options differing in
  level of risk are described in different ways"; *attribute* framing,
  "in which some characteristic of an object or event serves as the focus
  of the framing manipulation"; and *goal* framing, "in which the goal of
  an action or behavior is framed" (p. 150). In attribute framing "the
  framed stimulus label is like a prime, but it is part of, rather than
  peripheral to, the description of the target stimulus" (p. 166).

## Example

The canonical case (Tversky & Kahneman 1981, p. 453). Two groups read the
same cover story — an outbreak "expected to kill 600 people" — and chose
between two programmes:

- Gain frame (N = 152): "If Program A is adopted, 200 people will be saved.
  [72 percent] If Program B is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that 600
  people will be saved, and 2/3 probability that no people will be saved.
  [28 percent]"
- Loss frame (N = 155): "If Program C is adopted 400 people will die. [22
  percent] If Program D is adopted there is 1/3 probability that nobody
  will die, and 2/3 probability that 600 people will die. [78 percent]"

"The only difference between them is that the outcomes are described in
problem 1 by the number of lives saved and in problem 2 by the number of
lives lost. The change is accompanied by a pronounced shift from risk
aversion to risk taking. We have observed this reversal in several groups
of respondents, including university faculty and physicians" (p. 453).

## Classified as manipulation by

- **Tversky & Kahneman** do not use the word, but they treat frame-driven
  preference reversals as inconsistencies a rational agent would want to
  remove: discovering that "the relative attractiveness of options varies
  when the same decision problem is framed in different ways … will
  normally lead the decision-maker to reconsider the original
  preferences" (p. 457). On this reading a frame that changes a choice
  without changing the facts is a defect in the choice.
- **The decision-theoretic invariance norm.** The paper measures framing
  against the requirement, stated in its opening paragraph, that rational
  "choice requires that the preference between options should not reverse
  with changes of frame" (p. 453); the reversal is offered as a violation
  of it. Under that norm, deliberately choosing the frame that produces
  the wanted choice is exploiting a violation. The paper's last section
  adds that the coherence criterion "enjoins the decision-maker to resolve
  inconsistencies but offers no guidance on how to do so" (p. 458).
- **Communication studies (critical reading).** Entman presents framing as
  what texts do to promote "a particular problem definition, causal
  interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation"
  (p. 52); the critical media literature he surveys reads the power to
  frame as a power over the audience's conclusions.

## Classified as legitimate by

- **Rhetoric.** Selection and emphasis are what any speech does; on
  Aristotle's account the orator's task is to find "the available means of
  persuasion" in each case
  ([*Rhetoric* I.2.1, 1355b26](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0060:book=1:chapter=2),
  Freese; excerpt: `raw/aristotle-rhetoric-1-2-1356a-three-proofs.md`),
  and choosing under which description to present the facts is one of
  them. No source on this page calls a *true* description under a chosen
  emphasis a fallacy.
- **Entman's neutral definition.** The definition itself is descriptive:
  "to frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make
  them more salient" (p. 52). Every text frames; the question the field
  asks is *which* frame, not *whether*.
- **Equivalence versus emphasis.** The political-science survey separates
  *equivalency* frames (logically identical statements, the Asian-disease
  type) from *emphasis* frames (different considerations brought forward);
  Chong & Druckman 2007 treat the second as ordinary political
  communication and reserve the concern about manipulation mainly for the
  first. This page reports that distinction without deciding where the
  line is.

## Critique

Published objections, attributed:

- **Of the effect's size and generality.** Kühberger's meta-analysis of
  risky-choice framing — "136 empirical papers … nearly 30,000
  participants … 230 effect sizes" — finds "that the overall framing
  effect between conditions is of small to moderate size and that
  profound differences exist between research designs"; "framing is a
  reliable phenomenon", but reference-point manipulations must be
  distinguished from outcome-salience manipulations, and "procedural
  features of experimental settings have a considerable effect on effect
  sizes" ([Kühberger 1998](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2781),
  abstract, p. 23; excerpt: `raw/kuhberger-1998-framing-meta-analysis.md`;
  only the abstract was read, and it gives no numeric effect size).
  Levin, Schneider & Gaeth's own comparison: in risky-choice framing "a
  choice shift (but not necessarily a reversal) typically occurs"
  (p. 181), whereas for attribute framing "we know of no case in which a
  negative attribute frame produces more favorable evaluations than a
  positive attribute frame" and its table "presents the clearest evidence
  of a homogeneous phenomenon" (p. 160).
- **Of the "same problem" premise.** The reversal is a violation of
  invariance only if the two descriptions are equivalent; the
  authors themselves say the problems are "effectively identical" on the
  assumption that 200 saved *means* 400 dead (p. 453). Readings on which
  "200 will be saved" is heard as "at least 200" and "400 will die" as "at
  least 400" — so that the descriptions are not equivalent and the choice
  is not inconsistent — are discussed in the framing literature Kühberger
  reviews; the implant reports the existence of this line of objection
  and has not excerpted its primary sources.
- **Of the concept's coherence.** Entman's paper is titled "Toward
  Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm" because, on his account, the
  field lacked a shared definition; his own fixes "at least four
  locations in the communication process: the communicator, the text,
  the receiver, and the culture" (1993, p. 52). The Levin typology exists
  for the same reason on the decision side.
- **A structural note the implant adds**, and owns as its own comparison
  ([manifest G4](../../vision/manifest.md)): the decision-science and
  communication literatures share the word and little else. The first
  concerns logically equivalent redescriptions of a fixed choice; the
  second concerns selection among non-equivalent aspects of an open
  situation. A page that does not say which sense it means is already
  framing.

## How to spot it

Observable markers, from the sources:

- Gains and losses stated from a chosen reference point — "saved" versus
  "die", "gain" versus "forgo", "fat-free" versus "fat" — where the
  complementary description is available and absent (Tversky & Kahneman
  1981; Levin et al. 1998).
- A sure outcome set against a gamble with the same expected value — the
  risky-choice structure, where the frame predicts which way the audience
  will lean (gain frame → the sure thing; loss frame → the gamble).
- Consequences of acting described as what is *lost by not acting* — goal
  framing, the type Levin et al. associate with health and compliance
  messaging.
- A problem definition, cause, moral evaluation or remedy supplied by the
  text rather than argued for — Entman's four functions (p. 52); the
  marker is that they arrive as description.
- What is missing: the reference point, the base rate, the other
  attribute, the other side's considerations. A frame is defined by what
  it makes less salient as much as by what it makes more.

## How to answer it

Counter-moves the sources describe or imply:

- **Reframe and compare.** The 1981 paper's own remedy: notice that the
  preference "varies when the same decision problem is framed in different
  ways" and "reconsider the original preferences" (p. 457). In practice,
  restate the offer in the complementary frame — lives lost beside lives
  saved, the percentage fat beside the percentage lean — and see whether
  the choice survives.
- **Fix the reference point.** Ask what outcome is being treated as
  neutral (p. 456) and whether that is the one the audience would choose
  on reflection.
- **Convert to totals.** Where the frame is an equivalency frame, write
  down the full outcome distribution (600 people: 200 live, 400 die, with
  the probabilities) so that no description is privileged.
- **Name the omitted aspect.** Under Entman's definition, the counter to a
  frame is another frame that makes the omitted aspect salient; the
  communication literature treats *counter-framing* as the ordinary
  response, not a rule violation.
- **Ask which sense is in play.** If the disagreement is over an emphasis
  frame, the answer is an argument about which considerations matter; if
  over an equivalency frame, the answer is the reframe-and-compare test.
  Chong & Druckman's distinction is the tool.

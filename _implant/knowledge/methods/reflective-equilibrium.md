# Reflective equilibrium

## What it does

Reflective equilibrium is the method of mutual adjustment between general
principles (theories, rules, moral principles) and particular judgements
(intuitions about cases, data points, considered verdicts). When a principle
conflicts with a judgement, either may be revised; the aim is a stable state
in which principles and judgements cohere. The method does not guarantee
truth; it guarantees coherence among the agent's commitments.

The implant reports reflective equilibrium as a method used by many
philosophers and as a description of how reasoning actually proceeds in
ethics, epistemology, and political philosophy. It does not adopt reflective
equilibrium as its own justificatory method; the implant's own justification
rests on the givens in the [manifest](../../vision/manifest.md), not on
coherence among intuitions.

## Origins

- **Goodman 1955:** coined the term in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*
  (pp. 63–64) to describe the mutual adjustment between rules of inference
  and accepted inferential practices. Goodman's context was induction, not
  ethics.
- **Rawls 1971:** adopted reflective equilibrium as the justificatory method
  for *A Theory of Justice*. Distinguished narrow reflective equilibrium
  (adjusting principles to match existing judgements) from wide reflective
  equilibrium (also considering alternative moral conceptions and background
  theories) (§4, §9; see also Daniels, "Wide Reflective Equilibrium and
  Theory Acceptance in Ethics", *Journal of Philosophy* 76(5), 1979).
- **Nelson Goodman's predecessors:** the method resembles Aristotle's
  endoxic method (reasoning from reputable opinions, *Topics* I.1) and
  Sidgwick's method of reconciling common-sense morality with utilitarian
  principles (*The Methods of Ethics*, 1874), though neither used the term.

## Presuppositions

- **Coherence as a virtue.** Reflective equilibrium assumes that coherence
  among beliefs is at least a pro tanto reason for accepting them. Foundationalists
  deny this: justification flows from basic beliefs upward, not laterally.
- **Revisability of both poles.** The method assumes that neither principles
  nor intuitions are sacrosanct. Intuitionists who hold that moral intuitions
  are self-evident and unrevisable reject this assumption (Ross, *The Right
  and the Good*, 1930).
- **No external anchor.** Reflective equilibrium is internalist; it does not
  require correspondence to mind-independent facts. Realists who demand such
  correspondence object that coherence alone cannot justify (Enoch, *Taking
  Morality Seriously*, 2011).
- **Agent-relative starting points.** Different agents may reach different
  equilibria from different starting points. Whether this is a feature
  (pluralism) or a bug (relativism) is contested.

## The case for

- **Descriptive accuracy.** Philosophers and ordinary reasoners do adjust
  principles and judgments mutually; reflective equilibrium describes what
  we already do rather than imposing an alien standard.
- **Avoids foundationalism's regress.** Foundationalism requires basic beliefs
  that are justified without inference; identifying such beliefs has proved
  difficult. Reflective equilibrium offers a non-foundational alternative.
- **Accommodates theory change.** Scientific and moral progress often involve
  revising both data interpretations and theoretical frameworks simultaneously;
  reflective equilibrium models this dynamic.
- **Democratic / public justification.** Wide reflective equilibrium, by
  requiring consideration of alternative conceptions, models public reason
  in pluralistic societies (Daniels 1979; Rawls, *Political Liberalism*,
  1993).

## The case against

- **Conservatism bias.** Starting from existing intuitions privileges the
  status quo; radical moral reform may require abandoning intuitions that
  reflective equilibrium would preserve. Singer criticises reflective
  equilibrium for entrenching parochial moral intuitions ("Ethics and
  Intuitions", *Journal of Ethics* 9(3–4), 2005).
- **No convergence guarantee.** Different agents with different starting
  points may reach different equilibria; the method provides no independent
  criterion for choosing between them. This is the relativism objection.
- **Garbage in, garbage out.** If initial intuitions are systematically
  distorted (by culture, ideology, cognitive bias), the equilibrium will
  codify the distortion. Empirical moral psychology suggests widespread
  intuition distortion (Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*, 2012; Greene, *Moral
  Tribes*, 2013).
- **Circularity.** Principles are adjusted to fit judgements that were
  themselves shaped by prior principles; the mutual adjustment may be
  circular rather than genuinely justificatory. Brandt, *A Theory of the
  Good and the Right* (1979), ch. 1.
- **Foundationalist alternatives.** Ross-style intuitionism, Kantian
  constructivism, and natural-law theories offer non-coherentist
  justificatory structures. Reflective equilibrium is one option among
  several, not the default.

## Where it is used in this implant

The implant does not use reflective equilibrium as its own justificatory
method. It is documented here because:

- Many positions in [ethics](../positions/index.md) and [epistemology](../positions/index.md)
  appeal to reflective equilibrium as their justificatory structure.
- The [steelman-a-position](../../skills/steelman-a-position.md) skill may
  reconstruct a position's reflective equilibrium when that is how the
  position's proponents justify it.
- Understanding the method's presuppositions and limits is necessary for
  evaluating positions that rely on it.

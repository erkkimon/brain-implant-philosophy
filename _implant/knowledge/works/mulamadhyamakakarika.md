# Mūlamadhyamakakārikā

Abbreviation: MMK. English titles vary by translator, e.g. *The Fundamental
Wisdom of the Middle Way* (Garfield) and *Nāgārjuna's Middle Way*
(Siderits & Katsura).

## Author and date

Nāgārjuna. Westerhoff (SEP, "Nāgārjuna", Fall 2024, §1) reports that he
"was a Buddhist monk who lived some time between 150 and 250 CE, probably
mainly in southern India". Westerhoff says this "is rarely disputed". He
also says that "it is not easy to come up with a precise list of texts
Nāgārjuna composed". One reason he gives is that "different authors
bearing the name “Nāgārjuna” might have lived during different periods".
He lists the MMK among works whose ascription "is largely uncontested".

## Locator scheme

The MMK is cited by chapter and verse, e.g. MMK 24.18. Westerhoff gives the
work "450 stanzas" (SEP §1). Beyond that, the implant has not verified the

## Structure

Verse, commented on "by a large number of later authors" (Westerhoff, SEP
§1). The section headings of the SEP entry follow the MMK's topics:

## Cited for

- The central concept is **emptiness of svabhāva**. Westerhoff: "The
  central concept around which all of Nāgārjuna’s philosophy is built is the
  notion of emptiness (śūnyatā)". What things are empty of is svabhāva,
  rendered as "“inherent existence” and “intrinsic nature”". Other
  renderings are "“substance” and “essence”" (SEP §2).
- The [consciousness problem](../problems/consciousness.md) has a Buddhist
  row. It rests on SEP "Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy", not on the MMK.
  The MMK is listed here as the root text of the Madhyamaka school that the
  row's later sources belong to.

## Interpretations

- **Nihilist reading.** Westerhoff reports "the accusation that Madhyamaka
  is really a nihilistic doctrine, a doctrine that nothing exists". He says
  this reading has "a relatively long history, beginning in ancient India
  and continuing to find supporters nowadays". He cites Spackman 2014 and
  Westerhoff 2016 (SEP §2).
- **Middle-way reading.** Westerhoff reports "powerful systematic and
  historical reasons against" the nihilist reading. One is that "the
  Mādhyamikas themselves are very clear that their position avoids both of
  the extreme views" (SEP §2). He writes as a participant in this debate.
  The implant quotes both readings from his report and ranks neither.
- **Prāsaṅgika.** Westerhoff reports that a "specific reading of
  Nāgārjuna’s thought, called Prāsaṅgika-Madhyamaka, became the official
  philosophical position of Tibetan Buddhism" (SEP preamble).

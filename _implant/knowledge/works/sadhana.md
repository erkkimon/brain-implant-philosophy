# Sadhana: A Way to God

This page reports who said what about the book. It does not assert that
its author is a philosopher or that its exercises or claims are true or
false ([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md);
[How claims are graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md);
[Works](index.md)).

## Author and date

[Anthony de Mello](../thinkers/de-mello.md), S.J. Full title *Sadhana: A
Way to God. Christian Exercises in Eastern Form*. First edition St. Louis:
Institute of Jesuit Sources / Anand: Gujarat Sahitya Prakash, 1978, xi +
134 pp., per the Internet Archive record of that edition; the copyright
line reads "Copyright © 1978 Anthony de Mello, S.J., Poona, India" and the
imprimatur, by C. Gomes, SJ, Bishop of Ahmedabad, is dated January 24, 1978
(`raw/de-mello-1978-sadhana-introduction-and-exercises.md`). Jesuits in
Ireland call it "His first book, Sadhana: A Way to God, which was published in 1978"
(`raw/jesuits-ireland-de-mello-biography.md`).

## Locator scheme

## Structure

As the table of contents of the 1984 edition sets it out
(`raw/de-mello-1978-sadhana-introduction-and-exercises.md`;
`raw/de-mello-1978-sadhana-exercises-1-22-35.md`):

1. **Introduction.** De Mello writes: "I have spent the past fifteen years of my life as retreat master and spiritual director helping people to pray."
   One of his stated convictions: "Another is that prayer is to be made less with the head than with the heart."
   On method: "Contrary to common belief, there is such a thing as group contemplation."
   And: "I have written out the exercises here almost exactly in the form and in the language in which I propose them to groups."
   The dedication: "I dedicate this book to the Blessed Virgin Mary, who has always been for me a model of contemplation."
2. **Awareness, Exercises 1–14** (1 The Riches of Silence, 2–3 Body
   Sensations, 5 Breathing Sensations, 6 God in My Breath, 7 Breath
   Communication with God … 13 Finding God in All Things, 14 Awareness of
   the Other), with an unnumbered section "Awareness and Contemplation"
   after Exercise 5 and four short closing sections on its benefits.
   Exercise 1 opens: "We are accustomed to think of Scripture as the revelation of God. And so it is. I want you now to discover the revelation that silence brings."
   In Exercise 3 he reports: "I once made a Buddhist retreat at which we spent as many as twelve to fourteen hours each day concentrating on our breathing, on the air coming in and going out of our nostrils."
   From "Awareness and Contemplation" (p. 34): "And so we are led to the seemingly disconcerting conclusion that concentration on your breathing or your body sensations is very good contemplation in the strict sense of the word."
3. **Fantasy, Exercises 15–32** (15 There and Here, 19 The Sorrowful
   Mysteries, 22 Ignatian Contemplation, 24 Healing of Hurtful Memories,
   28 Your Funeral, 32 Awareness of Persons, among others).
   Exercise 15 opens: "There is an unsuspected and untapped source of power and life in our fantasy."
   Exercise 22: "This is a form of fantasy prayer recommended by Saint Ignatius of Loyola in his Spiritual Exercises and frequently used by many of the saints."
4. **Devotion, Exercises 33–47** (33 The "Benedictine" Method, 35 The
   Jesus Prayer, 36 The Thousand Names of God, 40 The Prayer of
   Intercession, 41 Petition, 47 The Prayer of Praise, among others).
   Exercise 35: "The ceaseless repetition of the name of Jesus is a form of prayer very dear to Greek and Russian Orthodox Christians"
   Exercise 47, the last: "If I had to choose the one form of prayer that has made the presence of Christ most real in my life and given me the deepest sense of being supported and surrounded by the loving providence of God, I would unhesitatingly choose this, the last form of prayer I propose in this book, the Prayer of Praise."

Structural note (G4): the count of 47 exercises and the part boundaries
are read off the table of contents; the implant has not summarised what
any exercise teaches beyond the quoted openings.

## Cited for

- [Anthony de Mello](../thinkers/de-mello.md): his first book, and the
  primary source for his retreat method. Jesuits in Ireland: it "outlines spiritual exercises based on those of Saint Ignatius, but blends with them Eastern traditions and practices."
  (`raw/jesuits-ireland-de-mello-biography.md`). The Institute of Jesuit
  Sources describes it as blending "insights and prayer techniques from scripture, modern psychology, and spiritual sources including yoga, Hinduism, and Zen Buddhism."
  (`raw/de-mello-1978-sadhana-introduction-and-exercises.md`, source 2).
- **The 1998 CDF documents.** The Notification of 24 June 1998, signed by
  Joseph Card. Ratzinger, names no book, but distinguishes early and later
  writings: "Especially in his early writings, Father de Mello, while revealing the influence of Buddhist and Taoist spiritual currents, remained within the lines of Christian spirituality."
  And: "But already in certain passages in these early works and to a greater degree in his later publications, one notices a progressive distancing from the essential contents of the Christian faith."
  (`raw/cdf-1998-notification-writings-de-mello.md`). Its accompanying
  Explanatory Note names this book, citing the 1978 edition:
  "In his work Sadhana: A Way to God, published for the first time in 1978, Jesus occupies a central place, particularly in the last part ("Devotion," 99-134)."
  It continues: "But already in this work he develops his theory of contemplation as awareness, which seems to be not lacking in ambiguity."
  It quotes the Exercise 1 passage on Lao-tse and Scripture, and the "Awareness and Contemplation" passage:
  "But this intuition, without images or form, is that of a void: "But what do I gaze into when I gaze silently at God? An imageless, formless reality. A blank!" (ibid., 26)."
  (`raw/cdf-1998-explanatory-note-de-mello.md`). Related de Mello works
  in the implant: [The Song of the Bird](the-song-of-the-bird.md) and
  [Awareness](awareness.md).
- **Jung's psychology applied to a practice.** Choe (2025) reads "the
  Sadhana practice" through [Jung](../thinkers/jung.md)'s analytical
  psychology: "First, based on Jung's theory, Sadhana pursues wholeness through the union of opposites, such as extraversion and introversion."
  (`raw/choe-2025-sadhana-practice-religious-psychological.md`; on the
  terms see [Psychological Types](psychological-types.md) and
  [Individuation](../vocabulary/individuation.md)).

## Interpretations

**Sources of the method.** Barnes (Thinking Faith, 2014), who made a
retreat under de Mello, reports him saying that the guiding practice
"was indeed taken from the Theravada Buddhist tradition of Vipassana or ‘Insight’ meditation."
Barnes's reading of its purpose: "Tony intended the Buddhist mindfulness exercises as no more than an introduction to prayer, a preparation which would build up the right dispositions for listening to the Word of God."
(`raw/barnes-2014-thinking-faith-de-mello.md`). Choe (2025, §1) names two
cores: "The core of the Sadhana practice lies in two key aspects: first, the introduction of psychological methods such as Gestalt therapy into the process of the Spiritual Exercises;"
the second being an integration of Christian prayer with Vipassana; and
(§2) "Christian traditional prayer methods like meditative and contemplative prayer, along with awareness and breathing, can be described as the three central pillars of the Sadhana practice."
Choe's evaluation (abstract): "Anthony de Mello's Sadhana practice is evaluated as a novel case that presents new possibilities for contemporary Christian spirituality."
(`raw/choe-2025-sadhana-practice-religious-psychological.md`).

**The "blank" passage.** Two readings are recorded, of the same sentence.

- The CDF Explanatory Note (1998) reads it as describing "a void" and
  links note 2 to the CDF letter *Orationis formas* (1989), which it
  quotes: "Still others do not hesitate to place that absolute without image or concepts, which is proper to Buddhist theory, on the same level as the majesty of God revealed in Christ, which towers above finite reality"
  (`raw/cdf-1998-explanatory-note-de-mello.md`).
- Fárek (Religions, 2022, §3) reads it as apophatic: "I think that De Mello's claims about "imageless, formless reality", which is perceived by a believer while "gazing silently at God" (ibid.) can be understood from the perspective of via negativa very well."
  And: "Instead of interpreting them as a presentation of a void concept of God, we should consider such claims to be practical instructions which try to grasp the experience of silent contemplation."
  His conclusion: "Therefore, criticism raised by theologians from CDF shows the problems of their own kataphatic approach, when confronted with the apophatic thought."
  (`raw/farek-2022-de-mello-via-negativa.md`).

De Mello's own context for the sentence, earlier on the same page range:
"Many mystics tell us that, in addition to the mind and heart with which we ordinarily communicate with God, we are, all of us, endowed with a mystical mind and mystical heart, a faculty which makes it possible for us to know God directly, to grasp and intuit him in his very being, though in a dark manner, apart from all thoughts and concepts and images."
(`raw/de-mello-1978-sadhana-introduction-and-exercises.md`).

**Reception.** Fárek (§2) quotes Callanan (2001): "Many consider that Tony de Mello's original book on prayer, Sadhana: A Way to God for Today, has been one of the outstanding contributions and practical aids to orthodox, creative and practical Christian prayer published in the last twenty-five years. In 1997, that book reached its twenty-second edition and is still greatly in demand"
(`raw/farek-2022-de-mello-via-negativa.md`). The standfirst of Barnes's article
calls de Mello "the controversial author of Sadhana"
(context note in `raw/barnes-2014-thinking-faith-de-mello.md`).

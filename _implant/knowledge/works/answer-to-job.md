# Answer to Job

This page reports what the essay says and what named authors say about it. It does not assert that
its theses about God, Job or the unconscious are true or false, nor that the essay is philosophy
([Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md); [How claims are
graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md)). It belongs to the [works](index.md) branch;
the author is [C. G. Jung](../thinkers/jung.md).

## Author and date

[C. G. Jung](../thinkers/jung.md). The CW editors' note: "[First published as a book, Antwort auf Hiob (Zurich, 1952). The present translation was first published, in book form, in London, 1954; for it, Professor Jung made some half-dozen small alterations to the original text and added or authorized an occasional footnote." (CW 11, head of item VI; `raw/jung-1952-answer-to-job-cw11.md`).
Composition: Shamdasani (2010 foreword) reports that "On 29 May 1951, Jung wrote to Aniela Jaffé from his tower at Bollingen, “I have landed the great whale; I mean ‘Answer to Job.’ ”" and quotes von Franz that Jung "“wrote in one burst of energy and with strong emotion, during an illness and after a high fever, and when he finished it he felt well again.”" (`raw/shamdasani-2010-answer-to-job-foreword.md`).
The Prefatory Note was "[Written for Pastoral Psychology (Great Neck, N.Y.), VI: 60 (January, 1956).— EDITORS.]" (CW 11), published there as "Why and How I Wrote My 'Answer to Job'" ([doi:10.1007/bf01683146](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01683146); `raw/answer-to-job-reviews-1955-1956-crossref.md`).

## Structure

As CW 11 divides it (`raw/jung-1952-answer-to-job-cw11.md`):

1. **Prefatory Note** — the occasion of the book. Jung: "In connection with the discussion of these problems and of the doctrine of Redemption, I criticized the idea of the privatio boni as not agreeing with the psychological findings."
2. **Lectori Benevolo** (¶¶553–559) — the standpoint: "“Physical” is not the only criterion of truth: there are also psychic truths which can neither be explained nor proved nor contested in any physical way." (¶553).
3. **Answer to Job**, sections I–XX (¶¶560–758) — Job (I–III), then Old Testament wisdom, the
   incarnation, the Apocalypse, and (XIX) the 1950 dogma of the Assumption; (XX) closes with the
   God-image and the self. Structural note (G4): the topic labels in this item are this page's
   summary of the headings-free sections, from the paragraphs quoted below.

## Cited for

**The question.** Jung, Prefatory Note: "If Christianity claims to be a monotheism, it becomes unavoidable to assume the opposites as being contained in God. But then we are confronted with a major religious problem: the problem of Job." and "It is the aim of my book to point out its historical evolution since the time of Job down through the centuries to the most recent symbolic phenomena, such as the Assumptio Mariae, etc." He describes the form: "I deliberately chose this form because I wanted to avoid the impression that I had any idea of announcing an “eternal truth.”"

**Religious statements as psychic facts.** "These entia are the archetypes of the collective unconscious, and they precipitate complexes of ideas in the form of mythological motifs." (¶557; see [archetype and collective unconscious](../vocabulary/archetype-and-collective-unconscious.md)).

**Job and Yahweh.** "The Book of Job is a landmark in the long historical development of a divine drama." (¶560). Jung announces his manner: "For this reason I shall express my affect fearlessly and ruthlessly in what follows, and I shall answer injustice with injustice, that I may learn to know why and to what purpose Job was wounded, and what consequences have grown out of this for Yahweh as well as for man." (¶563). On God: "Yahweh is not split but is an antinomy—a totality of inner opposites—and this is the indispensable condition for his tremendous dynamism, his omniscience and omnipotence." (¶567). On Job: "The victory of the vanquished and oppressed is obvious: Job stands morally higher than Yahweh. In this respect the creature has surpassed the creator." (¶640).

**The incarnation.** "Yahweh’s decision to become man is a symbol of the development that had to supervene when man becomes conscious of the sort of God-image he is confronted with." and "That is to say, God wants to become man, but not quite." (¶740). "He fills us with evil as well as with good, otherwise he would not need to be feared; and because he wants to become man, the uniting of his antinomy must take place in man." (¶747).

**The 1950 dogma of the Assumption.** "It is psychologically significant for our day that in the year 1950 the heavenly bride was united with the bridegroom." (¶748). "The new dogma expresses a renewed hope for the fulfilment of that yearning for peace which stirs deep down in the soul, and for a resolution of the threatening tension between the opposites." (¶754). Jung ties it to [individuation](../vocabulary/individuation.md): "The metaphysical process is known to the psychology of the unconscious as the individuation process." (¶755). He calls himself "a layman in things theological" (¶750).

**The God-image and the self.** "Strictly speaking, the God-image does not coincide with the unconscious as such, but with a special content of it, namely the archetype of the self. It is this archetype from which we can no longer distinguish the God-image empirically." (¶757).

**The problem of evil.** Ryan (1983, dissertation abstract) writes: "The problem of evil was a central concern of Jung's life and work. It is a major theme in Answer to Job; a point which received a great deal of criticism from religious thinkers." (`raw/ryan-1983-answer-to-job-dissertation-abstract.md`). The privation response that Jung's Prefatory Note criticises is set out, with its case for and against, on [the Epicurean trilemma](../problems/epicurean-trilemma.md).

## Interpretations

**Shamdasani: the theology of the Red Book.** Shamdasani (2010) writes: "No wonder: for it was in Answer to Job that the theology first articulated in Liber Novus—the themes of the progressive incarnation of the God, the necessity for “Christification,” and the replacement of the one-sided Christian God image with one that encompassed evil within it—found its definitive expression and elaboration." and "Answer to Job is faithful to the force of Jung’s theophany, now presented in the form of psychotheological and historical argument." On reception he reports Jung's letter to Hull and adds: "On November 25, 1953, Jung wrote to Richard Hull that “the clouds of dust it has raised at times nearly suffocated me!” To this day, the controversies around this work have not been stilled." (`raw/shamdasani-2010-answer-to-job-foreword.md`).

**Ryan: individuation.** Ryan (1983) reads the book this way: "Answer to Job is a paradigm of the individuation process, the lens through which all of Jung's theories may be viewed." Her dissertation "also examines Jung's objection to the concept of the privatio boni as an explanation for the cause of evil. It attempts to answer his objections in light of Christian tradition." (abstract, same raw file).

**Victor White.** Wikipedia ("Victor White (priest)", rev. 1307111261) reports that White "was initially deeply attracted to Jung's psychology, but when Jung's Answer to Job was published in English, he gave it a very critical review." and that "Jung was quite critical of White, for example, over his commitment to the doctrine of privatio boni as means of understanding the problem of evil." It adds: "White published a review of Answer to Job in the journal Blackfriars in March 1955. It was clear from this review that by this stage, White's feelings on the book were ambivalent, and he even seems to have had mixed feelings about his publication of the review, wondering how Jung would take the review." (`raw/wikipedia-answer-to-job-and-victor-white.md`). The review is White, "Jung on Job", *Blackfriars* 36 (1955), 52–60 ([doi:10.1111/j.1741-2005.1955.tb00631.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-2005.1955.tb00631.x); held bibliographically, not read). Callot writes of White: "He was an English Dominican with whom Jung maintained intense relations for years. Of course these relations deteriorated significantly due to Answer to Job! In a remarkable ten-page letter from April 1954, Jung tirelessly attempts to engage in dialogue and explain himself, yet the rift between the two men widens." (`raw/callot-answer-to-job-jung-and-his-critics.md`).

**Martin Buber.** The 1952 exchange in *Merkur* concerned Jung's psychology of religion in general; no read source ties Buber's essay to *Answer to Job* specifically. Zank and Braiterman (SEP, "Martin Buber", Fall 2026, §7) report: "Buber takes Jung to task for reducing religion to something immanent, at the expense of divine transcendence." and "The debate falters because Buber argues that Jung’s statements about the psychic origin of religious beliefs exceed the strict confines of empirical psychology, while Jung dismisses Buber and other critics beholden to traditional systems of thought as “naturally incapable of understanding” him correctly." (`raw/sep-buber-fall-2026-jung-exchange.md`). Buber's lecture appeared in *Eclipse of God* (1952); Jung's reply, "Answer to Martin Buber" (1952), is placed in CW 18 (CW 11 editorial note). Both are held bibliographically; neither text was read.

**Theologians.** Callot writes that "After all, it asserts that opposites are contained within God, that one cannot ignore His dark side, and even more provocatively, that the divine Wholeness lacks a feminine element!" and that "During these years, both before and after the publication of Answer to Job, Jung engaged in polemics with theologians, especially Protestant ones, regarding what I would call the concept of God and religion." She quotes Jung's letter of February 1952: "I have shown the MS to three theologians and they were shocked." (same raw file).

**Statements of esteem.** Wikipedia ("Answer to Job", rev. 1324649887) reports: "Author Joyce Carol Oates described Answer to Job in her review "Legendary Jung" as Jung's most important work." and attributes "most profound work" to John Shelby Spong (`raw/wikipedia-answer-to-job-and-victor-white.md`). Michael Fordham published "An Appreciation of Answer to Job" (*British Journal of Medical Psychology* 28, 1955, 271–273, [doi:10.1111/j.2044-8341.1955.tb00902.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8341.1955.tb00902.x); held bibliographically, not read).

**Other early reviews (held bibliographically, not read).** Seward Hiltner, *Pastoral Psychology* 6 (1956), 82–83 ([doi:10.1007/bf01683147](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01683147)); E. S. Waterhouse, *Philosophy* 31 (1956), 259–260 ([doi:10.1017/s0031819100057326](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0031819100057326)); Adrian Cunningham, "Victor White and C. G. Jung: the fateful encounter of the White Raven and the Gnostic", *New Blackfriars* 62 (1981), 320–334 ([doi:10.1111/j.1741-2005.1981.tb03298.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-2005.1981.tb03298.x)). Shamdasani's note 9 points to Paul Bishop, *Jung's Answer to Job: A Commentary* (2002), and Lammers and Cunningham (eds), *The Jung-White Letters* (2007).

# Psychological Types

This page reports what the book says and what named authors say about it. It does not assert that
the typology is true or false, nor that the book is philosophy ([Reporting, not
endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md); [How claims are
graded](../../conventions/how-claims-are-graded.md)). It belongs to the [works](index.md) branch.

## Author and date

[C. G. Jung](../thinkers/jung.md). German original *Psychologische Typen*, 1921 (Princeton
University Press writes that the book "appeared in German in 1921 after a “fallow period” of eight
years during which Jung had published little" — `raw/princeton-up-cw6-psychological-types.md`). The
publisher quotes Jung's autobiography on its origin: "This work sprang originally from my need to
define the ways in which my outlook differed from Freud’s and Adler’s." (same raw file).

## Structure

As the 1923 table of contents divides it (pp. v–viii; transcribed in
`raw/jung-1923-psychological-types-baynes.md`):

- Translator's Preface; Foreword; **Introduction** — "The Two Mechanisms: Extraversion and
  Introversion. The Four Psychological Basic Functions" (p. 9).
- **I.** The Problem of Types in the History of Classical and Medieval Thought (p. 15): the
  Gnostics, Tertullian and Origen; theological disputes of the ancient Church; transubstantiation;
  nominalism and realism (the universalia in antiquity and in scholasticism, Abelard's attempt at
  conciliation); Luther and Zwingli on Holy Communion.
- **II.** Schiller's Ideas upon the Type Problem (p. 87): the *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of
  Man*; "A Discussion on Naive and Sentimental Poetry".
- **III.** The Apollonian and the Dionysian (p. 170).
- **IV.** The Type Problem in the Discernment of Human Character (Jordan's types, p. 184).
- **V.** The Problem of Types in Poetry: Spitteler's *Prometheus and Epimetheus* (p. 207), with
  sections on the Brahmanic conception of the reconciling symbol, Chinese philosophy and Meister
  Eckehart.
- **VI.** Psychiatry (p. 337). **VII.** Aesthetics (p. 358).
- **VIII.** The Problem of Types in Modern Philosophy (p. 372): William James's types, the pairs of
  opposites in them, a general criticism.
- **IX.** Biography (p. 397).
- **X.** General Description of the Types (p. 412): the extraverted and the introverted type, each
  function in each attitude, and "The Principal and Auxiliary Functions" (p. 513).
- **XI.** Definitions (p. 520): 57 numbered terms, "Abstraction" to "Will".
- Conclusion (p. 618); Index.

The CW 6 volume also prints later typology lectures cited by Sharp as "A Psychological Theory of
Types" (CW 6 ¶958) and "Psychological Typology" (CW 6 ¶972)
(`raw/sharp-1991-jung-lexicon-psychological-types.md`).

## Cited for

**The two attitudes.** Jung writes that "two types especially became clear to me which I have termed
the Introversion and the Extraversion Types." (Introduction, 1923 p. 9). In the CW translation: "A
further division into two classes is permitted by the predominant trend of the movement of libido,
namely introversion and extraversion." (CW 6 ¶835, via Sharp).

**The four functions.** "there exist thinking , feeling, sensation, and intuition. If one of these
functions habitually prevails, a corresponding type results. I therefore discriminate thinking,
feeling, sensation, and intuitive types." (Introduction, 1923 p. 14). Definition 22 sorts them: "I
distinguish four basic functions in all, two rational and two irrational — viz. thinking and feeling
, sensation and intuition. I can give no a priori reason for selecting just these four as basic
functions ; I can only point to the fact that this conception has shaped itself out of many years’
experience." (1923 p. 547). Jung revises earlier papers that "identified the thinking type with the
introvert and the feeling type with the extravert" (1923 p. 14). On the hierarchy: "Absolute
sovereignty always belongs, empirically, to one function alone, and can belong only to one function"
(CW 6 ¶667, via Sharp).

**Tertullian and Origen.** "Tertullian is a classical representative of the introverted thinking
type." (1923 p. 22); "Origen is a classical example of the extraverted type." (p. 25).

**Nominalism and realism.** Jung defines nominalism as the school holding that universals "are
nothing but nomina (names) or words" (p. 37) and realism as affirming "the existence of the
universalia ante rem" (p. 37). He writes that "the radical difference between nominalism and realism
is not purely a logical and intellectual distinction but also a psychological one" (p. 66), and
later: "As I have already pointed out, realism corresponds with the principle of introversion,
nominalism With extraversion." (p. 374). Links to a universals page await one in the implant (none
recorded).

**Schiller.** "Friedrich Schiller seems to have been the first to have made any considerable attempt
at a conscious discrimination of typical attitudes" (p. 87).

**Nietzsche's Apollonian and Dionysian.** Jung calls the Apollonian "a state of introspection, of
inner contemplation towards the dream world of eternal ideas : it is therefore a state of
introversion ." (p. 180), and adds that resting on the analogy alone "does violence to Nietzsche’s
ideas" (p. 180).

**William James's tender- and tough-minded.** Jung writes: "These associations prompt us to regard
the tender-minded as introverted" (p. 374); he judges the two labels "manifestly but a one-sided
terminology, and at bottom conceal a certain prejudice" (p. 382) and holds that "James’
characterization deals with those same types which I have termed the introverted and the
extraverted." (p. 382).

**Individuation.** The definitions chapter defines it: "In general, it is the process by which
individual beings are formed and differentiated; in particular, it is the development of the
psychological individual as a being distinct from the general, collective psychology." (CW 6 ¶757,
via Sharp). See [individuation](../vocabulary/individuation.md); the term also recurs in [*Answer to
Job*](answer-to-job.md) and the [archetype](../vocabulary/archetype-and-collective-unconscious.md)
page.

## Interpretations

**How Jung's sources describe the method.** Princeton University Press writes that Jung "relied not
so much on formal case data as on the countless impressions and experiences derived from the
treatment of nervous illnesses" (publisher page). Sharp (1991, "Typology") writes that "Jung's model
of typology grew out of an extensive historical review of the type question in literature,
mythology, aesthetics, philosophy and psychopathology."

**Myers-Briggs as a derivative.** Stein and Swan (2019, p. 2) write: "The MBTI is based on Carl
Jung's theory of psychological types (Jung, 1921/1971)." and "Two decades following Jung, Katharine
Cook Briggs, and later her daughter, Isabel Briggs Myers, extended Jung's theory to add a fourth
dimension, judging vs. perceiving (Quenk, 2009, p. 2)."
([doi:10.1111/spc3.12434](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12434);
`raw/stein-swan-2019-mbti-theory-validity.md`).

**Statements against.** Stein and Swan (2019, Table 1): "Jungian theory is not grounded in empirical
data."; (§4.1.1) "Jung's ideas were based on argumentation and anecdotal observation rather than
systematic empirical evidence". Of the MBTI they conclude that its theory "lacks agreement with
known facts and data, lacks testability, and possesses internal contradictions" (abstract). Barbuto
(1997) criticises "the dichotomous nature of the indices" and the MBTI's "operationalization of
Jung's psychological types" (abstract,
[doi:10.2466/pr0.1997.80.2.611](https://doi.org/10.2466/pr0.1997.80.2.611);
`raw/barbuto-1997-capraro-2002-mbti-abstracts.md`).

**Statements for.** Capraro and Capraro (2002), a meta-analysis of MBTI score reliability, report
that "the MBTI and its scales yielded scores with strong internal consistency and test-retest
reliability estimates, although variation was observed." (abstract,
[doi:10.1177/0013164402062004004](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164402062004004); same raw file).
Stein and Swan (2019, p. 2) report that "The Myers‐Briggs Company claims that reports of problems
with the assessment's test–retest reliability are fixed in newer versions (Schaubhut, Herk, &
Thompson, 2009)." Barbuto (1997), while criticising the MBTI, "considers a reconstruction of the
measure of Jung's psychological types" (abstract, as above). Structural note (G4): both sides here
concern the MBTI as an instrument; no read source assesses Jung's philosophical chapters, so that
reception is (none recorded).

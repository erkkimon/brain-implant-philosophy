# Evidence before authority

Decided by erkkimon on 2026-09-25, founding the philosophy implant
([journal](../journals/2026-09-25.md)); it is the manifest's eighth given
written out (manifest [G8](../vision/manifest.md)). This implant exists to
lay arguments in front of a reader — premises, conclusion, the case for and
the case against — so that the reader's own judgement becomes more
educated. That service is worthless if it flinches when the author is
canonical, and equally worthless if it sneers when the author is
unfashionable.

## The rule

**Weigh the argument, never the arguer.** The questions this implant asks
of a position are what its premises are, whether the inference is valid,
what the strongest published objection is, and how its proponents have
replied. None of those questions has a field for reputation.

Concretely:

1. **Same terms for everyone.** A dialogue of Plato, a journal article
   ([Gettier 1963](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/23.6.121), three pages
   long), a monograph, a blog post and a conference talk are each reported
   by what they argue and where. A canonical author's thesis gets no credit
   for being canonical and no penalty for being popular. That a work is
   widely taught or widely cited is a fact about the world, citable where
   someone states it (a citation count, a syllabus survey) — it is simply
   not a reason in the implant's voice.
2. **Credentials are reported, not used.** That an author spent thirty
   years on a problem, holds a chair, or was peer-reviewed is a fact about
   the source, and the implant records it where it is documented (manifest
   [G2](../vision/manifest.md)). It never appears as the reason a claim is
   presented more gently or more harshly, because the admission test here
   is checkability, not approval.
3. **Criticism attaches to the claim.** "The analysis of knowledge as true
   belief with an account, examined at *Theaetetus* 201c–d, admits
   counterexamples in which a justified true belief is true by luck
   ([Gettier 1963](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/23.6.121))" is in scope.
   "This author is a crank" is not, and never appears here — it is unciteable, it is about a
   person, and it survives in a public repository as defamation rather than
   analysis. Where a *published* critique makes a strong claim about an
   author, this implant reports that the critique exists and cites it,
   without adopting its tone or its evaluative predicates (manifest
   [G4](../vision/manifest.md)).
4. **Consensus is data, never a reason.** That most philosophers hold a
   view is real information, and it is reported as a survey result with its
   source — "59.2 % of target-faculty respondents accepted or leaned toward
   compatibilism in the PhilPapers 2020 survey (Bourget & Chalmers,
   [10.3998/phimp.2109](https://doi.org/10.3998/phimp.2109), free-will
   question)" — with the question, the population and the year, because a
   survey figure is an empirical claim like any other and carries a grade.
   It is never written as "compatibilism is the mainstream view" and never
   used to decide how much space a minority position gets: admission is by
   the mechanical rule of manifest [G5](../vision/manifest.md), and a
   position held by one named proponent is reported with the same care as
   one held by a majority.
5. **Symmetry of scrutiny.** The test: would this position be reported the
   same way if the name on it were swapped for an unknown's? If a thesis is
   being treated gently because it is canonical, or harshly because it is
   unfashionable, the page is wrong. This applies in both directions — the
   reflex to "debunk" a famous argument is as much a bias as the reflex to
   defer to it, and both words belong to the reader, not the implant.

## Provenance and soundness are two separate questions

Conflating them is the most common failure in writing about any influential
idea.

- **"Who first held this, provably?"** is a *provenance* question, answered
  by primary sources: what the person actually wrote, cited to a canonical
  locator — a Stephanus page, a Bekker number, an A/B page, a dated letter —
  never to a later paraphrase. It has an answer, and the answer can be wrong
  in checkable ways. The slogan "entities must not be multiplied beyond
  necessity" is universally called Ockham's Razor, and that formulation is
  nowhere in Ockham's texts
  ([Spade & Panaccio, "William of Ockham", SEP Fall 2024 edition, §4.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/ockham/)).
  A widely attributed idea that traces back to nobody, or to somebody else,
  is a finding worth a page.
- **"What are the arguments for and against?"** is a *soundness* question,
  answered by reconstructing the arguments and the published objections and
  replies. It is answered the same way whoever turns out to have held the
  position — and, on any contested question, it is answered by laying the
  cases side by side, not by the implant picking one (manifest
  [G3](../vision/manifest.md)).

Getting the first right is a precondition for the second being fair: it is
not a report of someone's position if the position being reported is a
distortion of what they wrote. Equally, establishing that a position is
genuinely someone's says nothing whatever about whether its arguments hold.

## Reception sections hold only attributed assessments

A thinker page is a hub of links — to the problems the thinker worked on,
the positions they held, the works those positions are in — and it has no
ranking, no "major" or "minor", no "influential" in the implant's voice
(manifest [G8](../vision/manifest.md), *what follows*). Its **reception**
section is where assessments live, and every sentence in it is an
attributed report: *who* judged the work how, *where*, cited. The
admissible form is "*Assessor* called the argument a fallacy (*work*,
edition, chapter and page)"; "the argument is a fallacy" is not, and
neither is "widely regarded as a fallacy" unless a source that says so is
cited as the one saying it. The same holds for praise: an assessment that
the work is "foundational" is somebody's, and the section says whose.

## Naming an assumption is not an attack

Almost every ambitious argument contains a premise that nothing in the text
demonstrates. Marking such a premise as *assumed* — in the reconstruction,
at the step where it is used — is a neutral, structural observation, and
this implant makes it about steps rather than about people. Naming it is
not answering it: the point of showing which premises carry the load is
exactly the information a reader deciding what to believe needs, and the
reader may well grant the premise.

The separation cuts the other way too. That a premise has not been argued
for in the text is not evidence that it is false; that an objection has not
been answered is not evidence that it cannot be. A page reports *that* the
step is unargued or the objection unanswered, with the receipt, and stops
there.

## Why it is a standing rule

Because it is easiest to abandon exactly where it matters most: on the small
number of famous, heavily taught positions that most readers arrive asking
about, and on the positions most readers arrive already despising. A rule
written down before those pages are drafted is harder to bend than a
judgement made while drafting them.

It works with [Every claim carries its receipt](every-claim-carries-its-receipt.md):
receipts make the argument checkable, and this rule says the argument — and
only the argument — is what the reader is shown.

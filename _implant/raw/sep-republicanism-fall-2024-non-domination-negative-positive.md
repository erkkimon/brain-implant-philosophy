# SEP Republicanism (Fall 2024) — Lovett on negative, positive and republican liberty

Source: Frank Lovett, "Republicanism", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (Fall 2024 Edition,
  ed. Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman); first published 19 Jun 2006, substantive revision 29 Jun 2022;
  preamble, §§1.1–1.3, 3.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/republicanism/ (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-09 (archive HTML fetched with curl; section text read)

> "According to republicans in this second sense (sometimes called ‘civic republicans’ or ‘neo-republicans’), the paramount republican value is political liberty, understood as non-domination or independence from arbitrary power."  (preamble)

> "According to the first, ‘negative’ conception of liberty, people are free simply to the extent that their choices are not interfered with."  (§1.1)

> "This idea of negative liberty Berlin associates especially with the classic English political philosophers Hobbes, Bentham, and J. S. Mill, and it is today probably the dominant conception of liberty, particularly among contemporary Anglo-American philosophers."  (§1.1)

> "Roughly speaking, a person or group is free in the positive sense to the extent that they exercise self-control or self-mastery."  (§1.1)

> "Berlin associates this second conception especially with such continental philosophers as Spinoza, Rousseau, and Hegel."  (§1.1)

> "we might equivalently say that freedom in the republican sense consists in the secure enjoyment of non-domination."  (§1.2)

> "Notice that the republican view of freedom is, at least in the broad sense, a negative conception of political liberty."  (§1.3)

> "Republican freedom merely requires the absence of something, namely, the absence of any structural dependence on arbitrary power or domination."  (§1.3)

> "Despite these similarities, however, republican freedom is not equivalent to the received view of negative liberty as non-interference."  (§1.3)

> "The most important of these is the tendency in the liberal tradition, especially beginning with Bentham, Paley, and Constant, to displace the robust conception of liberty as independence from arbitrary or uncontrolled power with a weaker conception of liberty as non-interference."  (§3.2)

Relied on for: a second encyclopedia's definitions of the negative, positive and republican conceptions; Lovett's classing of republican freedom as negative "in the broad sense" yet not equivalent to non-interference; the republican reading of Constant (with Bentham and Paley) as displacing liberty as independence from arbitrary power.
Context: Lovett writes as an exponent of the republican view (he is among the authors Carter's SEP entry lists for it). §1.1 also quotes Mill's "the only freedom which deserves the name" (On Liberty, 1859, p. 17) and attributes to Frankfurt (1982) one influential account of positive freedom as acting on second-order desires. The §3.2 sentence reports a republican interpretation of the liberal tradition, not Lovett's verdict in his own voice alone.

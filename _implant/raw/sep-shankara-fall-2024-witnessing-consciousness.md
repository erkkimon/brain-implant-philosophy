# SEP "Śaṅkara" (Dalal) — witnessing consciousness, self-illumination, and the denial that consciousness emerges from matter

Source: Neil Dalal, "Śaṅkara", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published 2021-10-04. §3 preamble, §3.1, §3.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/shankara/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read; section
  numbers are SEP's own)

> "Śaṅkara analyzes consciousness (cit or caitanyam) through both
>  perspectives of his two orders of reality. From the provisional
>  empirical order, consciousness is a witnessing presence (sākṣin) by
>  which all mental cognition is revealed as known. The absolute
>  perspective strips consciousness of all relational properties,
>  including intentionality and its status as a witness, leaving only the
>  intrinsic self-illuminating nature of consciousness remaining. He
>  identifies this pure non-intentional consciousness as numerically
>  identical with nondual existence, which is brahman."  (§3)

> "Śaṅkara distinguishes witnessing consciousness from the mind, mental
>  qualities, and cognitive modes. Consciousness is not composed of
>  physical materials, structures, or processes, whereas the mind is
>  composed of inert subtle matter. Consciousness is not an emergent
>  property of the brain and body, which are composed of inert coarse
>  matter, because that which is inert cannot give rise to sentiency.
>  (See Ram-Prasad 2001a on Advaita's consciousness and contemporary
>  physicalism)."  (§3.1)

> "The witness is not a separate substantial entity at all according to
>  Śaṅkara, nor does it possess agency in revealing cognition as a knower.
>  It is intransitive and receptive, simply the passive witnessing itself
>  (BṛUBh 1.4.10; TaiUBh 2.1.1; KeUBh 2.4). Only the mind possesses
>  object directed intentionality, not witnessing consciousness."  (§3.1)

> "If one infers that this non-finding of consciousness is evidence of
>  its non-existence, Śaṅkara counters that one cannot deny their own
>  consciousness (BrSūBh 2.3.7). Consciousness is self-evident. [...]
>  Śaṅkara defines the intrinsic nature of consciousness as uniquely
>  self-illuminating (svaprakāśa) because it does not require a second
>  awareness for it to be known."  (§3.2)

> "Śaṅkara repudiates rival philosophies such as the Nyāya school which
>  rejects witnessing consciousness and self-illumination in favor of
>  other-illumination (paraprakāśa)—a thesis more akin to contemporary
>  higher order theories."  (§3.2)

Relied on for: the claims that Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara) treats
consciousness as a non-intentional, self-illuminating witness distinct
from the mind, explicitly denies that consciousness emerges from inert
matter, and that its dispute with Nyāya parallels the self-representation
vs higher-order debate — all as reported by the SEP entry, which is the
citable source here; Śaṅkara's commentaries were not consulted.
Context: Dalal frames Śaṅkara's view through "two orders of reality"
(provisional/empirical and absolute; §2.3); §3.3 on reflection and
personal identity and §3.4 on superimposition (adhyāsa) are not excerpted.
The ellipsis omits the sentences on consciousness falling outside the
scope of negation.

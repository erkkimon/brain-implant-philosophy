> "In clinical practice, the doctrine of informed consent rose to dominance during the course of the 20th century."  (preamble)

> "How does informed consent in bioethics, the focus of the present entry, relate to consent in sexual ethics, business ethics, and political philosophy?"  (preamble)

> "Informed consent is shorthand for informed, voluntary, and decisionally-capacitated consent."  (§1)

> "Consent is considered fully informed when a capacitated (or “competent”) patient or research participant to whom full disclosures have been made and who understands fully all that has been disclosed consents voluntarily to treatment or participation on this basis."  (§1)

> "In its most important role in bioethics, informed consent is a legitimacy requirement for certain actions."  (§1)

> "bioethicists’ expositions of this instrumental rationale typically echo utilitarian philosopher John Stuart Mill, for whom “Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign” (Mill 1990, 135; see also ch. 3)."  (§2.1)

> "For instance, closing off patients’ options for voluntary euthanasia was said to increase their autonomy by freeing them, as well as similar patients, from family pressure to use that option (Velleman 1992); surely it may do so even when accomplished through forced injection of an antidote or other violations of informed consent."  (§2.2)

> "English admits of both psychological and behavioral senses of consent."  (§3)

> "Psychologically, consent can designate “a state of mind of acquiescence” (Westen 2003, 5), and “an act of will—a subjective mental state…” (Hurd 1996, 121)."  (§3)

> "But the medical requirement of informed consent seems to include at least some behavioral expression of consent (Kleinig 2010, 9–10), ranging from a patient’s signature, to her not protesting when a nurse approaches with a syringe (so-called implicit or tacit consent)."  (§3)

> "They say that presumed consent obtains, even if actual and express consent does not."  (§3)

> "Some authors oppose the terminology of presumed consent as a contrived “myth” or “fiction” of actual consent (Harmon 1990; Brownsword 2004, 232–3; Beauchamp and Childress 2008, 107; Dworkin 1988, 117)."  (§3)

> "Voluntary consent is usually thought incompatible with coercion, which philosophers define, roughly, as a threat to make someone seriously worse off than she is or should be, unless she consents (Wertheimer 1987)."  (§5.1)

> "Bioethicists and human rights lawyers tend to assume that informed consent is a natural right that generates a correlative objective moral duty and, downstream, legal and institutional duties."  (§8)

> "Many contemporary philosophers assume that consent has moral “magic” that turns what would have been a wrongful action into a right one, in an objective, moral sense (Hurd 1996)."  (§8)

> "But some of the aforementioned justifications for informed consent better support other assumptions."  (§8)

Relied on for: the elements of informed consent; its role as a legitimacy
  requirement; psychological vs behavioural senses (Westen, Hurd, Kleinig as

Context: the entry's bibliography lists Hurd, Heidi M., 1996, "The moral

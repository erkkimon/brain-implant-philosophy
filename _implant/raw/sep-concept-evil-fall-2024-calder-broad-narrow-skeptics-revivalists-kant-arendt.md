> "To avoid confusion, it is important to note that there are at least two concepts of evil: a broad concept and a narrow concept."  (preamble)

> "The broad concept picks out any bad state of affairs, wrongful action, or character flaw."  (preamble)

> "Natural evils are bad states of affairs which do not result from the intentions or negligence of moral agents."  (preamble)

> "By contrast, moral evils do result from the intentions or negligence of moral agents."  (preamble)

> "Evil in the broad sense, which includes all natural and moral evils, tends to be the sort of evil referenced in theological contexts, such as in discussions of the problem of evil."  (preamble)

> "In contrast to the broad concept of evil, the narrow concept of evil picks out only the most morally despicable sorts of actions, characters, events, etc."  (preamble)

> "Since the narrow concept of evil involves moral condemnation, it is appropriately ascribed only to moral agents and their actions."  (preamble)

> "Evil-skeptics believe we should abandon the concept of evil."  (§1)

> "By contrast, evil-revivalists believe that the concept of evil has a place in our moral and political thinking and discourse."  (§1)

> "Evil-skeptics believe the concept of evil is particularly problematic and should be abandoned while other moral concepts, such as right, wrong, good, and bad, are worth keeping."  (§1)

> "Some evil-skeptics believe that the concept of evil necessarily makes reference to supernatural spirits, dark forces, or creatures."  (§1.1; Calder cites Clendinnen 1999, 79–113; Cole 2006, 2019)

> "There is a secular moral concept of evil which is distinct from fictional or religious conceptions, and it is this secular conception of evil that is meant most often when the term ‘evil’ is used in moral and political contexts (see Garrard 2002, 325; Card 2010, 10–17)."  (§1.1)

> "To say that a person, or an action, is evil is just to say that that person, or action, defies explanation or is incomprehensible (see Clendinnen 1999, 81; Baron-Cohen 2011, 5-7; see also, Pocock 1985)."  (§1.2, reporting Clendinnen)

> "Another common response is to argue that evil is no less explanatorily useful than other moral concepts such as good, bad, right, and wrong (Garrard 2002, 322–326; Russell 2009, 268–269)."  (§1.2)

> "Claudia Card argues that “If the likelihood of the ideological abuse of a concept were sufficient reason to abandon the concept, we should probably abandon all normative concepts, certainly ‘right’ and ‘wrong.’” (Card 2010, 15)"  (§1.3)

> "Nietzsche believes that the concepts of good and evil contribute to an unhealthy view of life which judges relief from suffering as more valuable than creative self-expression and accomplishment."  (§1.3.1)

> "He contends that the powerless and weak created the concept of evil to take revenge against their oppressors."  (§1.3.1)

> "Instead, she argues that judgments of evil often indicate a healthy recognition that one has been treated unjustly."  (§1.3.1, on Card 2002, 27–49)

> "As Daniel Haybron puts it “Prefix your adjectives [such as ‘wrong’ or ‘bad’] with as many ‘very’s as you like; you still fall short. Only ‘evil’, it seems, will do” (Haybron 2002b, 260)."  (§1.4)

> "For instance, the evil of disease consists in a privation of health, and the evil of sin consist in a privation of virtue."  (§2.1, on the Neoplatonist theory)

> "Instead, Kant equates evil with having a will that is not fully good."  (§2.2)

> "There are three grades of evil which can be seen as increasingly more evil stages of corruption in the will."  (§2.2; frailty, impurity, perversity)

> "However, most theorists acknowledge that Kant’s theory is disappointing as a theory of evil in the narrow sense since it does not pick out only the morally worst sorts of actions and characters."  (§2.2)

> "Instead of prioritizing the moral law over all other incentives, she prioritizes self-love over the moral law."  (§2.2, on perversity)

> "For Arendt, radical evil involves making human beings as human beings superfluous."  (§2.3, on Arendt 1951)

> "In Eichmann in Jerusalem, she argues that “desk murderers” such as Eichmann were not motivated by demonic or monstrous motives."  (§2.3)

> "Arendt’s reflections on Eichmann and her concept of the banality of evil have been both influential and controversial"  (§2.3)

> "Others have contested Arendt’s suggestion that ordinary people can be regular sources of evil (see Card 2010; Calder 2003 and 2009)."  (§2.3)

> "One controversial answer to this question is that nothing more is required: an evil action is just a very wrongful action (Russell 2007 and 2014)."  (§3.1)

> "This position is resisted by most evil-revivalists who claim instead that evil is qualitatively, rather than merely quantitatively, distinct from mere wrongdoing (See, e.g., Steiner 2002; Garrard 1998 and 2002; Calder 2013 and 2019)."  (§3.1)

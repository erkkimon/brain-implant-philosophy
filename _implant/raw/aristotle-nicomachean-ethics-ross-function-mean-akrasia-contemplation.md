> "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete."  (I.7, 1098a, Perseus lookup at line 15; Perseus)

> "For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy."  (I.7, 1098a, following; Perseus)

> "Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit."  (II.1)

> "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."  (II.6, 1106b–1107a, Perseus lookups at 1106b35 and 1107a1; Perseus)

> "Practical wisdom, then, must be a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods."  (VI.5, 1140b, Perseus lookup at line 20; Perseus)

> "in thinking that all the virtues were forms of practical wisdom he was wrong, but in saying they implied practical wisdom he was right."  (VI.13, on Socrates)

> "We must, as in all other cases, set the observed facts before us and, after first discussing the difficulties, go on to prove, if possible, the truth of all the common opinions"  (VII.1, 1145b2–7; Kraut)

> "Socrates was entirely opposed to the view in question, holding that there is no such thing as incontinence; no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best-people act so only by reason of ignorance."  (VII.2, 1145b, Perseus lookup at line 25; Perseus)

> "Now this view plainly contradicts the observed facts"  (VII.2, 1145b, following; Perseus)

> "as a drunken man may utter the verses of Empedocles."  (VII.3, 1147b; Perseus, page 1147b line 10 region)

> "the position that Socrates sought to establish actually seems to result; for it is not in the presence of what is thought to be knowledge proper that the affection of incontinence arises (nor is it this that is 'dragged about' as a result of the state of passion), but in that of perceptual knowledge."  (VII.3, within 1147b13–17; Kraut)

> "If happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, it is reasonable that it should be in accordance with the highest virtue; and this will be that of the best thing in us. Whether it be reason or something else that is this element which is thought to be our natural ruler and guide and to take thought of things noble and divine, whether it be itself also divine or only the most divine element in us, the activity of this in accordance with its proper virtue will be perfect happiness."  (X.7, 1177a, Perseus lookups at lines 10 and 15; Perseus)

> "That this activity is contemplative we have already said."  (X.7, 1177a, following; Perseus)

Relied on for: the opening thesis on the good; the function argument and
its conclusion; "complete life"; virtue as habituated and as a mean fixed
by the person of practical wisdom; the definition of practical wisdom;
Aristotle on Socrates (virtue and practical wisdom; no akrasia); the
method of the appearances; the knowing-in-a-way answer on akrasia; X.7
contemplation as perfect happiness; the friends-and-truth remark on the
Platonists (aristotle.md).
Context: I.6 is the critique of the Form of the Good, written by a former
member of Plato's Academy (hence "friends"). The I.7 sentence on human good
closes the function argument and is followed by "But we must add 'in a
complete life.'" The VII.2 sentences report Socrates' view and Aristotle's
reaction; VII.3 gives his own analysis, comparing the incontinent to
sleepers, madmen and the drunk. X.7 opens the argument that the
contemplative life is happiest; X.8 calls the life of the other virtue
happy "in a secondary degree" (not quoted here).

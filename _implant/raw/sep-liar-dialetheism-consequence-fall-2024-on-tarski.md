# SEP "Liar Paradox", "Dialetheism", "Logical Consequence" — what three entries say about Tarski's hierarchy and his definition of consequence

## A. Liar Paradox

Source: Jc Beall, Michael Glanzberg & David Ripley, "Liar Paradox", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta &
  Uri Nodelman (eds.); first published 2011-01-20, substantive revision
  2016-12-12. §2.2, §3, §4.1.3, §4.3.1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/liar-paradox/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched with curl, converted to text)

> "The tradition, going back to Tarski (1935), is that the behavior of the truth predicate Tr is described by the following biconditional."  (§2.2; the biconditional is Tr(⌜A⌝) ↔ A)

> "In a skeptical vein, Tarski himself (1935, 1944) seems to have thought the Liar shows the ordinary notion of truth to be incoherent, and in need of replacement with a more scientifically respectable one."  (§3)

> "Working in classical logic, Tarski (1935) famously concluded from the Liar paradox that a language cannot define its own truth predicate. More generally, he took the lesson of the Liar to be that languages cannot express the full range of semantic concepts that describe their own workings. One of the main goals of the non-classical approaches to the Liar we have surveyed here is to avoid this conclusion, which many have seen as far too drastic. However, how successful these approaches have been in this regard remains a highly contentious issue."  (§4.1.3)

> "Instead, Tarski proposed that the truth predicate for a language is to be found only in an expanded metalanguage."  (§4.3.1)

> "With this restriction, it is easy enough to define a truth predicate which completely accurately states the truth values of every sentence in L0, obeys capture and release, and yields no paradox."  (§4.3.1; L0 is rendered in LaTeX as \mathcal{L}_0)

> "There is no Liar paradox because there is no Liar sentence."  (§4.3.1)

> "One is that in light of naturally occurring cases of self-reference, his ruling Liar sentences syntactically not well-formed seems overly drastic. Though Tarski himself was more concerned to resolve the Liar for formal languages, his solution seems implausible as applied to many naturally occurring uses of ‘true’."  (§4.3.1)

> "As Kripke notes, any syntactically fixed set of levels will make it extremely hard, if not impossible, to place various non-paradoxical claims within the hierarchy."  (§4.3.1)

> "In light of these sorts of problems, many have concluded that Tarski’s hierarchy of languages and metalanguages buys a solution to the Liar paradox at the cost of implausible restrictiveness."  (§4.3.1)

Relied on for: the T-schema traced to Tarski 1935; the entry's reading of
Tarski's scepticism about ordinary truth; the hierarchy and why it blocks
the liar; the criticisms the authors report (natural self-reference;
Kripke 1975 on fixed levels; "many have concluded").
Context: the SEP HTML writes the predicate as the LaTeX macro \(\Tr\); it is
rendered "Tr" here. The §4.3.1 opening sentence ("Traditionally, the main avenue ...")
is already held in sep-sorites-and-liar-fall-2024-paradox-formulations-and-responses.md.
The entry immediately goes on to Kripke's closed-off construction (§4.3.2).

## C. Logical Consequence

Source: Jc Beall, Greg Restall & Gil Sagi, "Logical Consequence", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta &
  Uri Nodelman (eds.); first published 2005-01-07, substantive revision
  2024-05-17. §2.2 (models), §3 (consequence operations).
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/logical-consequence/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched with curl, converted to text)

> "The contemporary model-theoretic definition of logical consequence traces back to Tarski (1936). It builds on the definition of truth in a model given by Tarski in (1935)."

> "John Etchemendy (1990) offers two perspectives for understanding models."

> "The interpretational approach, by looking only at the actual world fails to account for necessity, and the representational approach fails to account for formality (for details, see Etchemendy 1990, Sher 1996, and Shapiro 1998, and for refinements see Etchemendy 2008)."

> "A possible response to Etchemendy would be to blend the representational and the interpretational perspectives, viewing each model as representing a possible world under a re-interpretation of the nonlogical vocabulary (Shapiro 1998"

> "Tarski left the question of a principled distinction open in his 1936, and only gave the lines of a relativistic stance, by which different choices of the logical vocabulary may be admissible."

> "One way of answering this question goes back to Tarski , who introduced the notion of consequence operations."

Relied on for: the model-theoretic definition traced to Tarski 1936;
Etchemendy's challenge (as the entry reports it) and the Shapiro/Sher
response; the logical-vocabulary question left open; consequence
operations.
Context: the Etchemendy passage sets out two readings of models
(representational, interpretational) and the entry states each "is
flawed with respect to our analysis of logical consequence as necessary
and formal" before reporting the blended response.

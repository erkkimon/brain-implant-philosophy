# SEP Kant's Moral Philosophy (Fall 2024) — good will, the formulas, autonomy, teleological and metaethical readings

Source: Robert Johnson and Adam Cureton, "Kant's Moral Philosophy", *Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy* (Fall 2024 edition), ed. Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman; first published
  2004-02-23, substantive revision 2022-01-21 (copyright line: "Copyright © 2022 by Robert
  Johnson, Adam Cureton"), preamble and §§1, 2, 6–10, 14, 15.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/kant-moral/ (copyrighted; excerpts only)

  by script against the stripped text)

> "Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) argued that the supreme principle of morality is a principle of practical rationality that he dubbed the “Categorical Imperative” (CI)."  (preamble)

> "A second interpretation holds that the intelligible and sensible worlds are used as metaphors for two ways of conceiving of one and the same world (Korsgaard 1996; Allison 1990; Hill 1989a, 1989b)."  (§1)

> "Kant’s analysis of commonsense ideas begins with the thought that the only thing good without qualification is a “good will”."  (§2)

> "Kant’s views in this regard have understandably been the subject of much controversy."  (§2, on moral worth and the motive of duty)

> "Many object that we do not think better of actions done for the sake of duty than actions performed out of emotional concern or sympathy for others, especially those things we do for friends and family."  (§2)

> "Most philosophers who find Kant’s views attractive find them so because of the Humanity Formulation of the CI."  (§6)

> "First, the Humanity Formula does not rule out using people as means to our ends."  (§6)

> "The third formulation of the CI is “the Idea of the will of every rational being as a will that legislates universal law.” (G 4:432)."  (§7)

> "The formulation of the CI states that we must “act in accordance with the maxims of a member giving universal laws for a merely possible kingdom of ends” (G 4:439)."  (§8)

> "Kant claimed that all of these CI formulas were equivalent. Unfortunately, he does not say in what sense."  (§9)

> "At the heart of Kant’s moral theory is the idea of autonomy."  (§10)

> "As with Rousseau, whose views influenced Kant, freedom does not consist in being bound by no law, but by laws that are in some sense of one’s own making."  (§10)

> "Perhaps the first philosopher to suggest a teleological reading of Kant was John Stuart Mill."  (§14)

> "Thus, in his view, the CI is “simply utilitarianism put into other words” (1993, p. 103)."  (§14, of R. M. Hare)

> "And Wood argues that humanity itself is the grounding value for Kant."  (§14)

> "A second issue that has received considerable attention is whether Kant is a metaethical constructivist or realist."  (§15)

> "Constructivism in metaethics is the view that moral truths are, or are determined by, the outcomes of actual or hypothetical procedures of deliberation or choice."  (§15)

> "Many who interpret Kant as a constructivist claim that his analysis of “duty” and “good will” reveals that if there are moral requirements then the agents who are bound to them have autonomy of the will (Rawls 1980; Korsgaard 1996; O’Neil 1989; Reath 2006; Hill 1989a, 1989b, 2001; Cureton 2013, 2014; Engstrom 2009)."  (§15)

> "Kantians in this camp, however, disagree about how this rational procedure should be characterized."  (§15)

> "Other commentators interpret Kant as a robust moral realist (Ameriks 2003; Wood 1999; Langton 2007; Kain 2004)."  (§15)

> "According to these philosophers, Kant’s theory, properly presented, begins with the claim that rational nature is an objective, agent-neutral and intrinsic value."  (§15)

> "Some interpreters of Kant, most notably Korsgaard (1996), seem to affirm a kind of quietism about metaethics by rejecting many of the assumptions that contemporary metaethical debates rest on."  (§15)

> "Others have raised doubts, however, about whether Kantians can so easily avoid engaging in metaethical debates (Hussain & Shaw 2013)."  (§15)

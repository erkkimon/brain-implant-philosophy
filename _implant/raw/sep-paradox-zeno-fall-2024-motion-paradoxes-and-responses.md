## Who Zeno was, and what survives (preamble)

> "Almost everything that we know about Zeno of Elea is to be found in the opening pages of Plato’s Parmenides."  (preamble)

> "Since Socrates was born in 469 BC we can estimate a birth date for Zeno around 490 BC."  (preamble)

> "he wrote a book of paradoxes defending Parmenides’ philosophy. Sadly this book has not survived, and what we know of his arguments is second-hand, principally through Aristotle and his commentators"  (preamble)

> "(here we draw particularly on Simplicius, who, though writing a thousand years after Zeno, apparently possessed at least some of his book)."  (preamble)

> "Aristotle speaks of a further four arguments against motion (and by extension change generally), all of which he gives and attempts to refute."  (preamble)

> "almost none of these paradoxes are quoted in Zeno’s original words by their various commentators, but in paraphrase."  (preamble)

## Method and target (§1.1)

> "(This is what a ‘paradox’ is: a demonstration that a contradiction or absurd consequence follows from apparently reasonable assumptions.)"  (§1.1)

> "they are ‘reductio ad absurdum’ arguments (or ‘dialectic’ in the sense of the period)."  (§1.1)

> "some say that the target was a technical doctrine of the Pythagoreans, but most today see Zeno as opposing common-sense notions of plurality and motion."  (§1.1)

## The received view (§1.2)

> "Following a lead given by Russell (1929, 182–198), a number of philosophers—notably Grünbaum (1967), but also see Salmon (2001)—took up the task of showing how modern mathematics could solve all of Zeno’s paradoxes"  (§1.2)

> "This work remains the ‘received’ view, and thoroughly influences the following discussion, in approach and in specific analyses."  (§1.2)

> "This approach holds that—with certain qualifications—Zeno’s paradoxes reveal problems that cannot be resolved without the full resources of mathematics as worked out in the Nineteenth century (and perhaps beyond)."  (§1.2)

> "more recently a number of commentators have challenged the specific interpretations of the received view."  (§1.2)

## The Dichotomy (§3.1)

> "The resulting series contains no first distance to run, for any possible first distance could be divided in half, and hence would not be first after all."  (§3.1)

> "this description of her run has her travelling an infinite number of finite distances, which, Zeno would have us conclude, must take an infinite time, which is to say it is never completed."  (§3.1)

> "A couple of common responses are not adequate."  (§3.1)

> "as Simplicius (On Aristotle’s Physics, 1012.22) tells us Diogenes the Cynic did by silently standing and walking"  (§3.1)

> "But this would not impress Zeno, who, as a paid up Parmenidean, held that many things are not as they appear"  (§3.1)

> "Another response—given by Aristotle himself—is to point out that as we divide the distances run, we should also divide the total time taken"  (§3.1)

> "in an important monograph Sattler (2020) argues that understanding motion via such a mathematical correspondence between distance and duration was a crucial advance made by Aristotle, in response to his predecessors."  (§3.1)

> "However, he also realized (Physics, 263a15) that it could not be the end of the matter."  (§3.1)

> "(and again a complete solution would demand a rigorous account of infinite summation, like Cauchy’s). However, Aristotle did not make such a move."  (§3.1)

> "the potentially infinite series of halves in a continuous run is possible, while an actual infinity of discontinuous half runs is not"  (§3.1)

> "It is hard—from our modern perspective perhaps—to see how this answer could be completely satisfactory. In the first place it assumes that a clear distinction can be drawn between potential and actual infinities, something that was never fully achieved."  (§3.1)

> "note that there is a well-defined run in which the stages of Atalanta’s run are punctuated by finite rests, arguably showing the possibility of completing an infinite series of finite tasks in a finite time (Huggett 2010, 21–2)."  (§3.1)

> "the distinction between potential and actual infinities has played no role in mathematics since Cantor tamed the transfinite numbers"  (§3.1)

## Achilles and the Tortoise (§3.2)

> "Looked at this way the puzzle is identical to the Dichotomy"  (§3.2)

> "what the paradox in this form brings out most vividly is the problem of completing a series of actions that has no final member"  (§3.2)

> "Fortunately the theory of transfinites pioneered by Cantor assures us that such a series is perfectly respectable."  (§3.2)

> "Since the ordinals are standardly taken to be mathematically legitimate numbers, and since the series of points Achilles must pass has an ordinal number, we shall take it that the series is mathematically legitimate."  (§3.2)

## The Arrow (§3.3)

> "This argument against motion explicitly turns on a particular kind of assumption of plurality: that time is composed of moments (or ‘nows’) and nothing else."  (§3.3)

> "(According to Pemberton, 2022, Aristotle rejects the claim that time is composed of instants at all.)"  (§3.3)

> "whether it is in motion at an instant or not depends on whether it travels any distance in a finite interval that includes the instant in question."  (§3.3)

> "The answer is correct, but it carries the counter-intuitive implication that motion is not something that happens at any instant, but rather only over finite periods of time."  (§3.3)

> "In Bergson’s memorable words—which he thought expressed an absurdity—‘movement is composed of immobilities’ (1911, 308)"  (§3.3)

> "For further discussion of this ‘at-at’ conception of time see Arntzenius (2000) and Salmon (2001, 23-4)."  (§3.3)

## The Stadium (§3.4)

> "The general verdict is that Zeno was hopelessly confused about relative velocities in this paradox."  (§3.4)

> "(Sattler, 2015, argues against this and other common readings of the stadium.)"  (§3.4)

> "Perhaps (Davey, 2007) he had the following in mind instead"  (§3.4)

> "A final possible reconstruction of Zeno’s Stadium takes it as an argument against an atomic theory of space and time"  (§3.4)

## Influence (§5)

> "For the first half of the Twentieth century, the majority reading—following Tannery (1885)—of Zeno held that his arguments were directed against a technical doctrine of the Pythagoreans."  (§5)

> "However, in the middle of the century a series of commentators (Vlastos, 1967, summarizes the argument and contains references) forcefully argued that Zeno’s target was instead a common sense understanding of plurality and motion"  (§5)

> "That said, Tannery’s interpretation still has its defenders (see e.g., Matson 2001)."  (§5)

> "Such thinkers as Bergson (1911), James (1911, Ch 10–11) and Whitehead (1929) argued that Zeno’s paradoxes show that space and time are not structured as a mathematical continuum"  (§5)

> "they argued that the way to preserve the reality of motion was to deny that space and time are composed of points and instants."  (§5)

> "However, we have clearly seen that the tools of standard modern mathematics are up to the job of resolving the paradoxes, so no such conclusion seems warranted"  (§5)

> "central to this project was the recognition that a purely mathematical solution is not sufficient"  (§5)

> "So what they sought was an argument not only that Zeno posed no threat to the mathematics of infinity but also that that mathematics correctly describes objects, time and space."  (§5)

> "nothing guarantees a priori that space has the structure of the continuum, or even that parts of space add up according to Cauchy’s definition."  (§5)

> "it is also the case that quantum theories of gravity likely imply that they do not."  (§5)

> "A further strand of thought concerns what Black (1950–51) dubbed ‘infinity machines’."  (§5)

> "Black and his followers wished to show that although Zeno’s paradoxes offered no problem to mathematics, they showed that after all mathematics was not applicable to space, time and motion."  (§5)

> "But is it really possible to complete any infinite series of actions: to complete what is known as a ‘supertask’?"  (§5)

> "McLaughlin and Miller (1992) and McLaughlin (1994) show how Zeno’s paradoxes can be resolved in non-standard analysis"  (§5)

> "It should be emphasized however that—contrary to McLaughlin and Miller’s suggestions—there is no need for non-standard analysis to solve the paradoxes: either system is equally successful."  (§5)

> "(Reeder 2015 argues that non-standard analysis is unsatisfactory regarding the arrow, and offers an alternative account using a different conception of infinitesimals.)"  (§5)

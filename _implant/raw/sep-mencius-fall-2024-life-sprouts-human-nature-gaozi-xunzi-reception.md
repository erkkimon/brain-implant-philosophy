> "Mencius (fourth century BCE) was a Confucian philosopher. Often referred to as the “Second Sage” of Confucianism (meaning second in importance only to Confucius himself), Mencius is best known for his claim that “human nature is good.”"  (preamble)

> "“Mencius” is a Latinization (coined by Jesuit missionaries in the 17th century) of the Chinese “Mengzi,” meaning Master Meng. His full name was “Meng Ke.”"  (§1)

> "This work was probably compiled by his disciples or disciples of his disciples. It was subsequently edited and shortened by Zhao Qi in the second century C.E., who also wrote a commentary on the text."  (§1)

> "The received text of the Mengzi is divided into seven “books,” each of which is subdivided into two parts (labeled “A” and “B” in English), and then further subdivided into “chapters.”"  (§1)

> "Mencius was born about a century after the death of Confucius."  (§1)

> "Details of Mencius’s early life are sketchy and uncertain, but he is said to have been born in the state of Zou, in what is now Shandong Province."  (§1)

> "Mencius is said to have studied under Confucius’s grandson Zisi"  (§1)

> "Mencius held office in the state of Qi (2B6–7)."  (§1)

> "Mencius eventually resigned his position, when it became clear to him that the ruler was unwilling to enact any of the reforms that he proposed (2B10–13)."  (§1)

> "Traditionally, Mencius is said to have lived from 372–289 BCE (Legge 1970, 16). However, many recent scholars put his lifetime about twenty years earlier."  (note 6)

> "Mencius is treated as a respected older man in the Mengzi, so it is likely that he was born around 390 BCE."  (note 6)

> "One of Mencius’s most influential views was his list of four innate ethical dispositions, which he treats as what Western ethicists would call cardinal virtues: benevolence (rén), righteousness (yì), wisdom (zhì), and propriety (lĭ)."  (§2)

> "Mencius holds that all humans have innate but incipient tendencies toward benevolence, righteousness, wisdom, and propriety. Employing an agricultural metaphor, he refers to these tendencies as “sprouts” (2A6)."  (§2)

> "In order to become genuinely virtuous, a person must “extend” or “fill out” his or her nascent virtuous inclinations."  (§2)

> "The discussion of this issue has a long history, but was revived in Western scholarship by Nivison (1980)."  (§2)

> "However, according to Confucians, one should always have greater concern for, and has greater obligations toward, relatives, teachers, and friends than one does for strangers and animals (7A45)."  (§2)

> "For example, he notes that rescuing one’s sister–in–law if she were drowning would justify violation of the ritual prohibition on physical contact between unmarried men and women (4A17; cf. 5A2, 6B2). Knowing when to violate ritual is a matter of wisdom."  (§2)

> "As A.C. Graham (1967) demonstrated in a classic essay, Mencius and his contemporaries regarded the nature of X as the characteristics that X will develop if given a healthy environment for the kind of thing X is."  (§3)

> "This thesis runs the danger of becoming viciously circular: we might characterize goodness as the result of growing up in a healthy environment, and a healthy environment as one that results in humans being good."  (§3)

> "Graham (1967, 14–15) takes this to be a decisive objection to Mencius’s view."  (note 11)

> "Mencius’s views might be mistaken, but they cannot be casually dismissed as naively circular; rather, they must be evaluated as a complex and subtle account of ethics and philosophical psychology."  (§3)

> "The statement that “[human] nature is good [xìng shàn]” is often misquoted as “[human] nature is fundamentally good [xìng bĕn shàn].” The latter phrase actually derives from a gloss on Mencius’s view popularized by the seminal commentator Zhu Xi (1130–1200)."  (§3)

> "While ingenious, this interpretation of Mencius is unmotivated without the assumption that the Analects and Mengzi must be expressing the same view."  (§3)

> "the specific phrase “[human] nature is good” only occurs in two of these (3A1, 6A6)"  (§3)

> "Mencius performs a swift reductio ad absurdum, pointing out that—since dogs, oxen, and humans are the same in being alive—the proposed definition entails that dogs, oxen, and humans have the same nature (6A3)."  (§4)

> "It is open to the rival philosopher to bite the bullet and accept this implication. However, Mencius has at the least raised a strong, prima facie objection."  (note 17)

> "Mencius viewed himself as a defender of the Confucian tradition, and saw the two major contemporary threats to that tradition as the doctrines of Mozi and Yang Zhu"  (§4)

> "Mencius’s doctrine that human nature is good was part of his strategy to present a Confucian response to both the Mohists and Yang Zhu."  (§4)

> "However, he argued that Yang Zhu had an implausibly impoverished conception of what human nature is like."  (§4)

> "In summary, the impartial caring advocated by the Mohists is both perverse and impractical."  (§4)

> "In his own era, Mencius’s views were criticized by the Daoist Zhuangzi and by his fellow Confucian Xunzi."  (§5)

> "From where I see it, the sprouts of benevolence and righteousness and the pathways of right and wrong are all snarled and jumbled"  (§5, quoting Zhuangzi 2)

> "Mengzi says: When people engage in learning, this manifests the goodness of their nature. I say: This is not so."  (§5, quoting Xunzi)

> "Those things in people that cannot be learned and cannot be worked at are called their “nature.” Those things in people that they become capable of through learning and that they achieve through working at them are called their “deliberate efforts.”"  (§5, quoting Xunzi)

> "It is common to observe that Mencius and Xunzi are speaking at cross–purposes, because they assume different conceptions of the key term “nature” (Graham 1967)."  (§5)

> "However, it is possible that Mencius and Xunzi still have a substantive disagreement, even if Xunzi’s way of framing that disagreement is misleading."  (§5)

> "crooked wood must await steaming and straightening on the shaping frame, and only then does it become straight. Blunt metal must await honing and grinding, and only then does it become sharp"  (§5, quoting Xunzi)

> "In addition to denouncing Buddhist practices as unnatural and antithetical to the well being of the state, Han Yu singles out Mencius as the “most pure” of the followers of Confucius."  (§5)

> "The most influential Neo–Confucian philosopher was Zhu Xi, who grouped the Mengzi along with three other Confucian classics (the Great Learning, Analects, and Mean) as the Four Books, and wrote clear, detailed commentaries on each."  (§5)

> "This criticism was made within the Chinese tradition by later Confucians such as Dai Zhen (1724–1777), who argued that, due to Buddhist influence, the Neo–Confucians understood terms like “Pattern” in anachronistic ways, and were led to an ethics that unnaturally devalued physical human desires (see Dai 1777)."  (§5)

> "many New Confucians are influenced by the views of Mou Zongsan (1909–1995), who argued that Mencius and the Neo–Confucian Wang Yangming accurately explicated what is implicit in the views of Confucius."  (§5)

> "Some argue that this illustrates that Mencius (and Confucianism in general) is committed to a form of nepotism that encourages corruption (see, e.g., Liu 2007). Others have argued for alternative interpretations of the significance of these passages (e.g., Guo 2007)."  (§5, on 5A3, 7A35)

> "Mencius has been invoked as providing a potential foundation for democracy (Bai 2008), but others have cautioned about overestimating the extent to which he champions the will of the people as the source of political authority (Tiwald 2008)."  (§5)

> "Another recent trend in the study of Mencius and other Confucian philosophers has been the application of the framework of virtue ethics, beginning with Yearley (1990)."  (§5)

> "New Confucian and virtue ethics interpreters of the Mengzi have both been criticized for projecting an alien vocabulary onto the text."  (§5)

> "Wang Yangming (1472–1529) famously argued for the “unity of knowing and acting,” as the most plausible interpretation of Mencius’s view"  (§2)

> "His most famous argument for this conclusion is the thought experiment of the child at the well:"  (§4)

> "Suppose someone suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well: anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion—not because one sought to get in good with the child’s parents, not because one wanted fame among one’s neighbors and friends, and not because one would dislike the sound of the child’s cries."  (§4, quoting 2A6 in Van Norden 2008, 46)

> "As for what they are inherently [qíng], they can become good."  (§3, quoting 6A6 in Van Norden 2008, 149)

> "In a much–discussed example (1A7), Mencius draws a ruler’s attention to the fact that he had shown compassion for an ox being led to slaughter by sparing it."  (§2)

> "Mencius was saying that human beings have a constitution comprising certain emotional predispositions that already point in the direction of goodness and by virtue of which people are capable of becoming good"  (§3, quoting Shun 1997, 219–20)

> "Ever since man came into this world, there has never been one greater than Confucius"  (§1, quoting 2A2 in Lau 2003, 67)

> "Benevolence is manifested in the affection one has for his or her own kin, as well as compassion for the suffering of other humans, and even concern for non–human animals."  (§2)

> "Mencian wisdom (somewhat like Aristotelian phronēsis) seems to be only partially constrained by rules."  (§2)

> "Mencius thinks that matters of ritual place legitimate ethical demands on us, but he stresses that they are not categorical, and can be overridden by more exigent obligations."  (§2)

> "Mozi (fifth century BCE) is the first systematic philosophical critic of Confucianism."  (§4)

> "There is controversy about what the views of Yang Zhu (fifth to fourth century BCE) were."  (§4)

> "Mou saw Kant as providing a way of understanding what is distinctive about the Mencian conception of morality, but also thought that Confucian ethics transcends certain limitations of Kant’s thought."  (§5)

> "Mencius does seem similar to some major virtue ethicists such as Aristotle in emphasizing wisdom as a flexible responsiveness to complex situations, the cultivation of virtue over action–guiding rules, and nuanced discussions of the differences between genuine virtues and semblances or counterfeits of them (e.g., 7B37)."  (§5)

> "However, as advocates of virtue ethics interpretations acknowledge, there are also substantial differences between Mencius and any Western virtue ethics."  (§5)

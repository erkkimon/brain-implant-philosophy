# Marcus 1980 — "Moral Dilemmas and Consistency": thesis, the definition of consistency for rules, footnote on Williams

Source: Ruth Barcan Marcus, "Moral Dilemmas and Consistency", The Journal
  of Philosophy 77(3) (March 1980): 121–136, pp. 121, 127–128, 131.
Original: https://doi.org/10.2307/2025665 (JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/2025665)

  quotation in SEP "Moral Dilemmas", Fall 2024, §4; DOI metadata verified

> "I WANT to argue that the existence of moral dilemmas, even where the dilemmas arise from a categorical principle or principles, need not and usually does not signify that there is some inconsistency (in a sense to be explained) in the set of principles, duties, and other moral directives under which we define our obligations either individually or socially."  (p. 121)

> "I want also to argue that, on the given interpretation, consistency of moral principles or rules does not entail that moral dilemmas are resolvable in the sense that acting with good reasons in accordance with one horn of the dilemma erases the original obligation with respect to the other."  (p. 121)

> "we ought to conduct our lives and arrange our institutions so as to minimize predicaments of moral conflict."  (p. 121)

> "Analogously we can define a set of rules as consistent if there is some possible world in which they are all obeyable in all circumstances in that world."  (p. 128)

> "On the proposed definition, rules are consistent if there are possible circumstances in which no conflict will emerge."  (p. 128)

> "For dilemmas, when they occur, are data of a kind. They are to be taken into account in the future conduct of our lives."  (p. 131)

> "Bernard Williams. in Problems of the Self (New York: Cambridge. 1977). chs. II and 12. also recognizes that the source of some apparent inconsistencies in imperatives and rules is to be located in the contingency of their simultaneous inapplicability on a given occasion."  (p. 127, fn. 8; "II" is the scan's rendering of "11")

Relied on for: thinkers/marcus.md — Marcus's two theses (dilemmas need not
signal inconsistency; acting on one horn does not erase the other
obligation), the second-order regulative principle, the definition of
consistency for rules, dilemmas as "data", her footnote on Williams.
Context: hyphenation at line ends removed in transcription. Between p. 121
and p. 128 Marcus reviews the view that dilemmas show a code inconsistent
(Lemmon, Ross, Hare) and a card-game analogy for rules that may produce
dilemmas in some deals but not in all. p. 129 continues the definition:
"By extension, a set of rules is inconsistent if there are no
circumstances, no possible world, in which all the rules are satisfiable."

## Consistency arguments: 'ought' implies 'can' and agglomeration (added 2026-09-27 for arguments/consistency-arguments-against-moral-dilemmas.md)

Source: as above; pp. 121, 134–135.
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (scan at
  https://pdf.stafforini.com/marcus-1980-moral-dilemmas-consistency.pdf,
  text layer read with pdftotext; page numbers from the printed running
  heads; line-end hyphenation joined; DOI 10.2307/2025665 verified by
  content negotiation: title, J Phil 77, p. 121, 1980-03)

> "I WANT to argue that the existence of moral dilemmas, even where the dilemmas arise from a categorical principle or principles, need not and usually does not signify that there is some inconsistency (in a sense to be explained) in the set of principles, duties, and other moral directives under which we define our obligations either individually or socially."  (p. 121)

> "Analogously we can define a set of rules as consistent if there is some possible world in which they are all obeyable in all circumstances in that world."  (p. 128)

> "If we interpret the 'can' of the precept as "having the ability in this world to bring about," then, as indicated above, in a moral dilemma, 'ought' does imply 'can' for each of the conflicting obligations, before either one is met."  (p. 134)

> "Just as 'possible P and possible Q' does not imply 'possible both P and Q', so 'A can do x and A can do y' does not imply 'A can do both x and y'."  (p. 134)

> "From 'A ought to do x' and 'A ought to do y' it does not follow that 'A ought to do x and y'. Such a claim is of course a departure from familiar systems of deontic logic."  (p. 134)

> "For example, John Lemmon, in "Moral Dilemmas," Philosophical Review, LXXI, 2 (April 1962): 139-158, p. 150, rejects the principle that 'ought' implies 'can'. Van Fraassen, op. cit., pp. 12/3, accepts it, as does Bernard Williams seemingly in Problems of the Self, op. cit., pp. 179--184. Van Fraassen and Williams see that such acceptance requires modification of the principle of factoring for the deontic "ought.""  (p. 134, n. 13)

> "One ought to act in such a way that, if one ought to do x and one ought to do y, then one can do both x and y. But the second-order principle is regulative."  (p. 135)

Relied on for: Marcus's acceptance of 'ought' implies 'can' read as
"ability in this world", her consequent rejection of agglomeration
("factoring"), her report of Lemmon, van Fraassen and Williams (n. 13), the
regulative second-order principle.
Context: p. 134 opens by noting that "some versions" of 'ought' implies
'can' appear to be denied by dilemmas and that philosophers who recognise
dilemmas divide on the precept; n. 13 goes on to say other received
deontic principles "will have to be rejected" in "a subsequent paper".

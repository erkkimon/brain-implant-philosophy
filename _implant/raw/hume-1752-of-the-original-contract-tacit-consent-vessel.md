# Hume 1752 — "Of the Original Contract": the two parties; the peasant and the vessel against tacit consent

Source: David Hume, "Of the Original Contract" (dated "published in 1752" by
  Dagger & Lefkowitz, SEP "Political Obligation" §1.3), in *Hume's Political Discourses*, ed. with an
  introduction by William Bell Robertson, The Scott Library, London and
  Felling-on-Tyne: Walter Scott Publishing Co.; Project Gutenberg eBook #59792.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/59792/pg59792.txt
  (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-10-15 (plain text fetched with curl; quotations checked by
  script against the text)

> "The other party, by founding government altogether on the consent of the people, suppose that there is a kind of original contract by which the subjects have reserved the power of resisting their sovereign whenever they find themselves aggrieved by that authority with which they have, for certain purposes, voluntarily entrusted him."  (opening paragraphs)

> "Can we seriously say that a poor peasant or artisan has a free choice to leave his own country, when he knows no foreign language or manners, and lives from day to day by the same small wages which he acquires?"  (middle of the essay)

> "We may as well assert that a man, by remaining in a vessel, freely consents to the dominion of the master, though he was carried on board while asleep, and must leap into the ocean and perish the moment he leaves her."  (same paragraph)

> "If the reason is asked of that obedience which we are bound to pay to government, I readily answer, because society could not otherwise subsist."  (later in the essay)

Relied on for: Hume's statement of the consent party's view; his objection to
  tacit consent by residence; his alternative ground of allegiance.
Context: the first sentence follows Hume's description of the other party,
  which traces government to "the Deity"; he then says both systems "are just,
  though not in the sense intended by the parties". The vessel paragraph
  follows his remark that implied consent "can only take place where a man
  imagines that the matter depends on his choice". Later he offers "a more
  philosophical, refutation of this principle of an original contract or
  popular consent", and says "The general interests or necessities of
  society are sufficient to establish both" (promise-keeping and allegiance).

> "My aim in this book is to examine the arguments for and against the existence of God carefully and in some detail"  (Preface)

> "While my own view is atheist, I have tried to give a full and fair hearing to the opposing case."  (Preface)

> "Derek Parfit"  (Preface, in the list of those thanked "for a number of corrections and suggestions")

> "for a number of corrections and suggestions"  (Preface)

> "Chapter 9 incorporates and develops the arguments of‘Evil and Omnipotence’, published in Mind 64 (1955) and of replies to several critics of that article."  (Preface)

> "But my title also echoes Hume’s ironic remark that the Christian religion cannot be believed without a miracle by any reasonable person."  (Introduction, p. 12)

> "Since this defence is formally possible, and its principle involves no real abandonment of our ordinary view of the opposition between good and evil, we can concede that the problem of evil does not, after all, show that the central doctrines of theism are logically inconsistent with one another. But whether this offers a real solution of the problem is another question."  (ch. 9(a), p. 154; "this defence" is a theodicy of first- and second-order goods and evils)

> "The second alternative poses the paradox of omnipotence: can an omnipotent being make things which he cannot control?"  (ch. 9(c), p. 160)

> "When I originally raised this question, I thought that it was a genuine paradox, that the question could not be answered satisfactorily either in the affirmative or in the negative."  (ch. 9(c), p. 160)

> "However, this was a mistake. Clearly, a god might have both first- and second-order omnipotence, so long as he did not exercise his second-order power in such a way as to limit his first-order power."  (ch. 9(c), p. 160)

> "Hence the negative answer to the paradox question is satisfactory after all."  (ch. 9(c), p. 160, reporting an argument others gave)

> "But Alvin Plantinga argues that it can cover them, since they can be ascribed to the malevolent actions of fallen angels."  (ch. 9(d), p. 162, on natural evils)

> "Plantinga has not rescued the free will defence but made its weakness all too clear."  (ch. 9(f), p. 174)

> "it is obvious that my original criticism of the free will defence holds good: had there been such a god, it would have been open to him to create beings such that they would always freely choose the good."  (ch. 9(f), p. 174)

> "In short, all forms of the free will defence fail, and since this defence alone had any chance of success there is no plausible theodicy on offer."  (ch. 9(f), p. 176)

> "We cannot, indeed, take the problem of evil as a conclusive disproof of traditional theism"  (ch. 9(f), p. 176)

> "there is a strong presumption that theism cannot be made coherent without a serious change in at least one of its central doctrines."  (ch. 9(f), p. 176)

> "The balance of probabilities, therefore, comes out strongly against the existence of a god."  (ch. 14, p. 253)

Relied on for: the book's aim, stance and title; Mackie's 1982 revision
of his 1955 orders-of-omnipotence answer; his concession on logical
inconsistency (p. 154, the passage Beebe quotes in IEP) and his reply to
Plantinga's free will defence; his overall conclusion.
Context: ch. 9(a) surveys the problem and lists theodicies; the p. 154
concession concerns a theodicy of "absorbed" evils, after which Mackie
asks whether it is "a real solution". In (c) the "negative answer" is an
argument he reports ("it has been argued"), and he goes on to ask whether
the affirmative answer is equally defensible. (f) discusses Plantinga's
individual essences and transworld depravity before the p. 174 verdict.
The Preface also thanks Norbert Hoerster, Derek Parfit and others.

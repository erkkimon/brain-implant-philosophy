> "(The following articles are the first two lectures of a course of eight lectures delivered in London in the first months of 1918, and are very largely concerned with explaining certain ideas which I learnt from my friend and former pupil Ludwig Wittgenstein."  (Monist 28, 1918, p. 495)

> "I pass now to the contradiction about classes that are not members of themselves."  (lecture VII, p. 353)

> "Accordingly, that being so, you could go on to make up the class of all those classes that are not members of themselves, and you can ask yourself, when you have done that, is that class a member of itself or is it not?"  (p. 354)

> "Let us first suppose that it is a member of itself. In that case it is one of those classes that are not members of themselves, i. e., it is not a member of itself."  (p. 354)

> "Hence either hypothesis, that it is or that it is not a member of itself, leads to its contradiction. If it is a member of itself, it is not, and if it is not, it is."  (p. 354)

> "That contradiction is extremely interesting. You can modify its form ; some forms of modification are valid and some are not. I once had a form suggested to me which was not valid, namely the question whether the barber shaves himself or not."  (pp. 354–355)

> "You can define the barber as "one who shaves all those, and those only, who do not shave themselves." The question is, does the barber shave himself? In this form the contradiction is not very difficult to solve."  (p. 355)

> "But in our previous form I think it is clear that you can only get around it by observing that the whole question whether a class is or is not a member of itself is nonsense, i. e., that no class either is or is not a member of itself, and that it is not even true to say that, because the whole form of words is just a noise without meaning."  (p. 355)

> "That has to do with the fact that classes, as I shall be coming on to show, are incomplete symbols in the same sense in which the descriptions are that I was talking of last time ; you are talking nonsense when you ask yourself whether a class is or is not a member of itself"  (p. 355)

Relied on for: Russell's own wording on the barber (a form "suggested to
me" that "was not valid"; "not very difficult to solve"); the contrast he
draws with the class form, which he treats as nonsense because classes are
incomplete symbols; the dating of the lectures (London, early 1918;
lecture VII printed 1919).
Context: lecture VII opens with remarks on existence prompted by a letter
from a member of the class, then counts classes against things (2^n > n),
then states the class contradiction with the teaspoon examples and the
two-hypothesis derivation, then the barber passage, then the Epimenides
liar; the theory of types follows in the same lecture. Russell does not say
in this passage who suggested the barber form, nor how the barber form is
"solved".

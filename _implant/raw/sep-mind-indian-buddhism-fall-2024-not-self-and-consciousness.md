# SEP "Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy" (Coseru) — not-self, momentariness, and consciousness as dependently arising

Source: Christian Coseru, "Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N.
  Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first published 2009-12-03, substantive
  revision 2012-10-12. Preamble, §1.1, §2.1, §3.4, §5.6.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read; section
  numbers are SEP's own)

> "While Buddhists share with other Indian philosophers the view that the
>  domain of the mental encompasses a set of interrelated faculties and
>  processes, they do not associate mental phenomena with the activity of
>  a substantial, independent, and enduring self or agent. Rather,
>  Buddhist theories of mind center on the doctrine of not-self (Pāli
>  anatta, Skt. anātma), which postulates that human beings are reducible
>  to the physical and psychological constituents and processes which
>  comprise them."  (preamble)

> "In one of his earlier discourses, the Buddha declares that we ought to
>  regard any form of sensation and consciousness, whether 'past, future,
>  or present; internal or external; manifest or subtle...as it actually
>  is...: "This is not mine. This is not my self. This is not what I am"'
>  (Majjhima Nikāya I, 130)."  (§1.1)

> "Impermanence (anitya). As the second mark of existence, impermanence
>  pervades all compounded phenomena. It forms an integral part of the
>  theory of momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda), which asserts that phenomena do
>  not endure for more than a moment."  (§2.1)

> "Instead of reifying each moment of existence, and operating with the
>  assumption that continuity is the hallmark of our lives, the Buddhist
>  view presents a fluid account of experience as an ever-changing stream
>  of psycho-physical events. [...] There is no self or substantive mind
>  that either supervenes on or exists apart from these aggregates."
>  (§2.1)

> "The canonical literature presents us with a standard formula for the
>  dependently arising phenomenon of consciousness: Dependent on the eye
>  and forms, visual-consciousness arises. The meeting of the three is
>  contact. With contact as condition there is feeling. [...] (Majjhima
>  Nikaya, I, 111–112). As this passage illustrates, a specific type of
>  consciousness accompanies each of the sensory modalities"  (§3.4)

> "One group of Buddhist philosophers, the Pudgalavādins (lit.
>  personalists), usually identified with the Vātsīputrīyas, seem to have
>  advocated the view that persons can be said to conventionally exist,
>  insofar as they are conceived in dependence upon the five aggregates
>  and the six types of cognitive awareness (cf. Priestley 1999)."  (§5.6)

Relied on for: the claims that Indian Buddhist philosophy of mind treats
consciousness as a stream of momentary, dependently arising events
without an enduring self (a possible non-Western counterpart to bundle
and process views of the mind), that consciousness is modality-specific
in the canonical formula, and that the tradition was not monolithic (the
Pudgalavāda personalists) — all as reported by the SEP entry, which is
the citable source here; the Pāli texts themselves were not consulted.
Context: the entry covers roughly fifteen centuries of Indian Buddhist
thought and notes parallels with Brahmanical traditions; §5–6 discuss
Abhidharma, Yogācāra "repository consciousness" and idealism, not
excerpted here. Ellipses omit the list of aggregates and the remainder of
the canonical formula on perception and proliferation.

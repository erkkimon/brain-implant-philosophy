# Mill 1863, Sidgwick 1907, Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2022 — utilitarian and virtue-ethical treatments of exceptions to truthfulness

## Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), ch. II

Source: John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism* (1863), ch. II "What Utilitarianism Is"; Project
  Gutenberg eBook #11224 (text of the 1879 edition).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11224 (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain text downloaded and read)

> "any, even unintentional, deviation from truth, does that much towards weakening the trustworthiness of human assertion"  (ch. II)

> "we feel that the violation, for a present advantage, of a rule of such transcendent expediency, is not expedient"  (ch. II)

> "Yet that even this rule, sacred as it is, admits of possible exceptions, is acknowledged by all moralists; the chief of which is when the withholding of some fact (as of information from a male-factor, or of bad news from a person dangerously ill) would preserve some one (especially a person other than oneself) from great and unmerited evil, and when the withholding can only be effected by denial."  (ch. II)

> "and if the principle of utility is good for anything, it must be good for weighing these conflicting utilities against one another, and marking out the region within which one or the other preponderates."  (ch. II)

Context: the passage answers the charge that utility is mere "Expediency"; Mill first argues
that a lie weakens "the trustworthiness of human assertion" and that its violator "acts the part
of one of their worst enemies", then states the exception. Mahon (SEP §2.3) lists Mill 1863
among those holding lying to a would-be murderer permissible.

## Sidgwick, The Methods of Ethics (7th ed. 1907), Book III, ch. VII

Source: Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, 7th edition (1907), Book III, ch. VII "The
  Classification of Duties—Veracity", §2 and the note to §3; Project Gutenberg eBook #46743.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46743 (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain text downloaded and read; its front matter lists "First Edition 1874" and "Seventh Edition 1907")

> "Still, the latter does not seem to decide clearly whether truth-speaking is absolutely a duty, needing no further justification: or whether it is merely a general right of each man to have truth spoken to him by his fellows, which right however may be forfeited or suspended under certain circumstances."  (III.vii.2)

> "so if we may even kill in defence of ourselves and others, it seems strange if we may not lie, if lying will defend us better against a palpable invasion of our rights: and Common Sense does not seem to prohibit this decisively."  (III.vii.2)

> "for the deceived assailant trusts his veracity, otherwise he would not be deceived"  (III.vii, note to §3, replying to Leslie Stephen)

Context: "the latter" is "the morality of Common Sense"; Sidgwick is examining whether
Common-Sense veracity can serve as a self-evident axiom, and the same paragraph opens with
Kant's view that lying is "an abandonment or, as it were, annihilation of the dignity of man".
Mahon (SEP §2.3) lists Sidgwick (7th ed., Hackett 1981) among those holding the lie permissible.

## Hursthouse & Pettigrove, SEP "Virtue Ethics" (Fall 2024)

Source: Rosalind Hursthouse and Glen Pettigrove, "Virtue Ethics", *Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy* (Fall 2024 edition); first published 2003-07-18, substantive revision 2022-10-11
  (copyright line: "Copyright © 2022 by Rosalind Hursthouse, Glen Pettigrove"), §§1.1, 3.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/ethics-virtue/ (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched and read)

> "The honest person recognises “That would be a lie” as a strong (though perhaps not overriding) reason for not making certain statements in certain circumstances, and gives due, but not overriding, weight to “That would be the truth” as a reason for making them."  (§1.1)

> "Honesty points to telling the hurtful truth, kindness and compassion to remaining silent or even lying."  (§3)

> "Both aim to resolve a number of dilemmas by arguing that the conflict is merely apparent; a discriminating understanding of the virtues or rules in question, possessed only by those with practical wisdom, will perceive that, in this particular case, the virtues do not make opposing demands or that one rule outranks another, or has a certain exception clause built into it."  (§3)

Context: §3 is objection (d), "the conflict problem"; "Both" refers to deontology and virtue
ethics. The entry does not discuss Kant's murderer case; it is used only for the general
virtue-ethical treatment of honesty and conflicting virtues.

Relied on for (all three): the consequentialist replies (Mill, Sidgwick) and the virtue-ethical
framing, each attributed; none of the three texts mentions Kant's 1797 essay.

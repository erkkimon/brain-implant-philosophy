# SEP "Normative Economics and Economic Justice" (Fleurbaey) — §7.1 Freedom and Rights: Sen 1970, Gibbard 1974, game forms

Source: Marc Fleurbaey, "Normative Economics and Economic Justice", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta
  & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first published 2004-05-28, substantive revision
  2021-09-18. §7.1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/economic-justice/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (read in a text extraction of the archived page;
  every quotation below checked against it)

> "Sen (1970b) and Gibbard (1974) propose, within the framework of social choice, paradoxes showing that it may not be easy to rank alternatives when some individuals have a special right to rank some alternatives that differ only in matters belonging to their private sphere, and when their preferences are sensitive to what happens in other individuals’ private spheres."  (§7.1)

> "For instance, as an illustration of Gibbard’s paradox, individuals have the right to choose the color of their shirt, but, in terms of social ranking, should A and B wear the same color or different colors, when A wants to imitate B and B wants to have a different color?"  (§7.1)

> "There is a huge literature on this topic, and after Gaertner, Pattanaik and Suzumura (1992), who argue that no matter what choice A and B make, their rights to choose their own shirt is respected, a good part of it examines how to describe rights properly."  (§7.1)

> "The framework of game forms is an interesting alternative to the social choice model."  (§7.1)

Relied on for: Gibbard 1974's paradox as Fleurbaey illustrates it; the
  Gaertner–Pattanaik–Suzumura claim; Fleurbaey's assessment of game forms.
Context: In this entry's bibliography "Sen 1970b" is "The Impossibility of
  a Paretian Liberal", J. Pol. Econ. 78: 152–157; Gibbard 1974 is "A
  Pareto-consistent libertarian claim", J. Econ. Theory 7: 388–410. The
  section goes on to the literature on measuring freedom of choice and to
  the relation of economic theory to libertarian philosophy.

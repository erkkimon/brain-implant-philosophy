## Joyce, "Moral Anti-Realism"

> "Defenders of moral error theory include Mackie 1977, Hinckfuss 1987, Joyce 2001, and Olson 2014."  (§3.2)

> "John Mackie (who coined the term “error theory” in 1977) argues that when we participate in moral discourse we commit ourselves to the existence of objective values and objective prescriptions, but there are no such things."  (§3.2)

> "Moral facts place demands upon us, but (Mackie asks) how could such demands exist objectively? This would seem to require rules of conduct somehow written into the fabric of the universe, and nothing in our understanding of the objective naturalistic world (Mackie goes on) suggests that anything of the kind exists."  (§3.2)

> "Mackie famously calls such properties “queer” (in what seems now an increasingly anachronistic use of the term)."  (§3.2)

> "Mackie occasionally cashes out his “argument from queerness” in terms of practical reasons."  (§3.2; followed by a quotation of Mackie 1982: 115)

> "If we consider Mackie’s argument, for example, then one kind of opponent will agree that objective values and prescriptions would be too weird to countenance, but maintain that it doesn’t matter because moral discourse is not committed to the existence of such strange creatures. Another kind of opponent will agree with Mackie that morality is committed to the existence of objective values and prescriptions, but maintain that there is nothing particularly strange about them."  (§3.2)

> "Such an error theorist is forced to fall back on vague metaphors in presenting their case: Moral properties have a “to-be-pursuedness” to them (Mackie 1977: 40)"  (§3.2)

> "“Strictly speaking, Mackie is right: genuine values would have to meet an impossible condition, so it is an error to think there are any. Loosely speaking, the name may go to a claimant that deserves it imperfectly … What to make of the situation is mainly a matter of temperament” (Lewis [1989] 2000: 93)."  (§3.2)

## Sayre-McCord, "Moral Realism"

> "moral realists need also to respond to the error theorist's contention that the arguments and disagreements all rest on some false supposition to the effect that there are actually facts of the sort there would have to be for some of the claims to be true."  (§1)

> "Some error theorists do argue that combining cognitivism with motivational internalism results in an untenable position (Mackie 1977)."  (§3)

> "Yet if the motivational internalism one embraces for moral claims has a parallel for pain claims, then this argument must be wrong (assuming it is true of some experiences that they are painful)."  (§3)

> "While there may well be reason to think there are no moral facts, this argument does not provide it."  (§3)

## Sinnott-Armstrong, "Moral Skepticism"

> "Skepticism about moral reality is the claim that no moral facts or properties exist."  (§1)

> "moral falsehood, as developed by Mackie (1977), or skepticism about moral truth-value, as developed by Joyce (2001)."  (§1)

> "Since smart and knowledgable people do take moral nihilism seriously and even argue for it (Mackie 1977, Joyce 2001), moral nihilism cannot be dismissed as readily as Descartes’s deceiving demon."  (§3.4)

Relied on for: the encyclopedists' summaries of Mackie's error theory and
the argument from queerness; the two kinds of opponent (Joyce); Lewis's
"strictly / loosely speaking" reply; Sayre-McCord's realist reply on
motivational internalism.
Context: Joyce's §3.2 first sketches an error-theoretic argument from
practical reasons, then turns to Mackie; after Lewis he concludes that the
dispute between error theorists and "success theorists" may be "a
fundamentally undecidable matter" (Joyce's assessment). Sayre-McCord's §3
reply follows his argument that realists can grant a necessary connection
between moral claims and action; his entry also says that "None of this is
to defend, as realists must, the idea that some of the claims are actually
true."

> "But if this account is true, which the Stoics were in no manner able to see, that argument also of Epicurus is done away."  (ch. 13)

> "God, he says, either wishes to take away evils, and is unable; or He is able, and is unwilling; or He is neither willing nor able, or He is both willing and able. If He is willing and is unable, He is feeble, which is not in accordance with the character of God; if He is able and unwilling, He is envious, which is equally at variance with God; if He is neither willing nor able, He is both envious and feeble, and therefore not God; if He is both willing and able, which alone is suitable to God, from what source then are evils? Or why does He not remove them?"  (ch. 13)

> "I know that many of the philosophers, who defend providence, are accustomed to be disturbed by this argument, and are almost driven against their will to admit that God takes no interest in anything, which Epicurus especially aims at"  (ch. 13)

> "He is able, therefore, to take away evils; but He does not wish to do so, and yet He is not on that account envious. For on this account He does not take them away, because He at the same time gives wisdom, as I have shown; and there is more of goodness and pleasure in wisdom than of annoyance in evils."  (ch. 13)

> "Therefore, unless we first know evil, we shall be unable to know good."  (ch. 13)

> "But the Academics, arguing against the Stoics, are accustomed to ask why, if God made all things for the sake of men, many things are found even opposed, and hostile, and injurious to us, as well in the sea as on the land."  (ch. 13, earlier in the same chapter)

Relied on for: the four-case form of the argument and its attribution to
Epicurus by Lactantius; Lactantius' own reply (evils are the condition of
wisdom); the neighbouring mention of the Academics' objection against the
Stoics.
Context: chapter 13 argues that the world was made for man. Lactantius
first reports the Academics' objection to the Stoics about harmful
creatures, then gives the answer he thinks the Stoics should have given
(God gave man wisdom, which requires knowledge of evils), and presents
the Epicurus argument as also answered by it. The book as a whole argues,
against Epicurus and the Stoics, that God is capable of anger (ch. 4 is
headed "Of God and His Affections, and the Censure of Epicurus").

## Preamble

> "Between antiquity and modernity stands Thomas Aquinas (ca. 1225–1274). The greatest figure of thirteenth-century Europe in the two preeminent sciences of the era, philosophy and theology, he epitomizes the scholastic method of the newly founded universities."  (preamble)

> "Viewed through a theological lens, Aquinas has often been seen as the summit of the Christian tradition that runs back to Augustine and the early Church."  (preamble)

## §1.1 Life

> "Like Dante or Michelangelo, Aquinas takes inspiration from antiquity, especially Aristotle, and builds something entirely new."  (preamble)

> "It was Albert’s firm conviction, which became Aquinas’s own, that the Christian faith could only benefit from a profound engagement with philosophy and science."  (§1.1)

> "Thomas Aquinas was born near Aquino, halfway between Rome and Naples, around the year 1225."  (§1.1)

> "Joining the order at the age of nineteen, he was assigned to Paris for further study"  (§1.1, of the Dominicans)

> "Thomas spent three years in Paris, studying philosophy, and then was sent to Cologne, in 1248, under the supervision of Albert the Great."  (§1.1)

> "From 1256 until 1259, he held the Dominican chair of theology in Paris, preaching, lecturing on the Bible, and presiding over various philosophical and theological disputations."  (§1.1)

> "In 1268, Aquinas was asked to return to Paris for an unusual second term as master of theology."  (§1.1)

> "A few months after that he died, in the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, on March 7, 1274."  (§1.1)

> "This chronology of Aquinas’s life is fairly well attested. There are also many colorful stories about his life, many of which stem from testimony given during his canonization inquiry in 1319. The authenticity of these stories is unknowable."  (§1.1)

## §1.2 Works

> "Over a mere two decades of literary activity, Aquinas left behind more than eight million words (eight times more than has survived, for instance, from Aristotle)."  (§1.2)

> "The Summa theologiae (ST) generally represents Aquinas’s most considered thought on a given topic"  (§1.2)

## §8.2 Natural Law

> "natural law: the distinctive way rational beings participate in the eternal law (q. 94);"  (§8.2, list of the four kinds of law in ST 1a2ae 90–108)

> "In fact, however, Aquinas’s theory offers something more complex and nuanced, because he thinks the moral law arises not out of brutely innate inclination but out of rational reflection on the good (Finnis 1998 ch. 3; Rhonheimer 1987 [2000])."  (§8.2)

> "Among its champions, this approach has been celebrated for the way it grounds traditional values in facts about human nature. To its critics, the view looks intellectually dubious twice over: on scientific grounds inasmuch as it makes false claims about the universality of various inclinations (Massey 1999); and philosophically inasmuch as it grounds normativity in metaethics on descriptive facts about human nature."  (§8.2)

> "Such criticisms, however, miss the philosophical sophistication of the overarching framework."  (§8.2, Pasnau's own assessment)

> "(The literature on the natural law in Aquinas is large and contentious. For a sample see Grisez 1965; Lisska 1996; Murphy 2001; Jensen 2015; Porter 2018."  (§8.2)

## §9 Influence

> "This process culminated in his canonization on July 18, 1323."  (§9)

> "Among the 219 articles condemned in Paris in 1277 are a significant number that seem to implicate Aquinas’s teachings (Hissette 1977; Wippel 1995b)."  (§9)

> "Later in March 1277, in Oxford, Archbishop Robert Kilwardby condemned a series of theses that explicitly includes those two distinctively Aquinian doctrines."  (§9)

> "For the quarter century after Aquinas’s death, scholasticism took shape around his Dominican supporters and his Franciscan critics."  (§9)

> "In 1567, Pope Pius V numbered Aquinas among the doctors of the Church, a title previously reserved for the ancient Church fathers."  (§9)

> "“soon no Thomist, Albertist, Scotist, or Ockhamist will be left in the world, but all will be simple children of God and true Christians” (Oberman 1966, 18–19)."  (§9, quoting Luther)

> "By the seventeenth century, Thomas Hobbes and his contemporaries flaunted their scorn for the scholastics, who “converse in questions of matters incomprehensible” (Leviathan viii.27)."  (§9)

> "In 1879, Pope Leo XIII called for a revival in the study and teaching of Aquinas."  (§9)

> "the twentieth century witnessed a flourishing of Thomisms of all kinds, in scholars like Jacques Maritain, Etienne Gilson, Elizabeth Anscombe, and Alasdair MacIntyre."  (§9)

> "Aquinas today, viewed as a philosopher, looks not so much like the culmination of an era, but rather like the brilliant beginning of Europe’s philosophical renaissance."  (§9, Pasnau's assessment)

# Grotius 1625 — The Rights of War and Peace II.1: the justifiable causes of war

Source: Hugo Grotius, De jure belli ac pacis (1625), Book II, ch. 1
  ("Defence of person and property"), §§I–II; English: The Rights of War
  and Peace, tr. A. C. Campbell, introduction by David Jayne Hill
  (New York: M. Walter Dunne, copyright 1901); Project Gutenberg eBook #46564.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46564 (plain text:
  https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46564/pg46564.txt)
  (public domain translation)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (Gutenberg plain text read)

> "Injury, or the prevention of injury forms the only justifiable cause of war."  (II.1.I)

> "II. The grounds of war are as numerous as those of judicial actions. For where the power of law ceases, there war begins."  (II.1.II)

> "The justifiable causes generally assigned for war are three, defence, indemnity, and punishment"  (II.1.II)

The chapter summary (Campbell's edition) adds:

> "War for defence of life, justifiable, and lawful--This kind of war lawful against an aggressor only--The danger must be present and real, not an imaginary danger"  (II.1, chapter summary)

Relied on for: Grotius's three just causes (defence, indemnity/recovery,
  punishment), war as the continuation of judicial redress, and the
  present-danger condition on defensive war.
Context: §I distinguishes "justifiable causes" from "pretexts" and "the
  beginning of war", with classical quotations (Polybius, Livy, Seneca);
  Grotius cites Augustine on unjustly acquired dominions as "the spoils of
  robbery". The Gutenberg transcriber notes that several chapters were
  omitted from Campbell's English translation.

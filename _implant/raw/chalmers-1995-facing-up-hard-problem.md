# Chalmers 1995 — "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness": easy problems, the hard problem, the explanatory gap; The Conscious Mind (1996) bibliographic

Source: David J. Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness",
  Journal of Consciousness Studies 2 (3), 1995, pp. 200–219; §1
  "Introduction" and §2 "The easy problems and the hard problem".
Original: https://consc.net/papers/facing.html  (author's own online copy;
  the journal article itself has no Crossref DOI — Crossref lists only the
  reprints, e.g. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195311105.003.0001
  in The Character of Consciousness, OUP 2010, pp. 3–34). Copyrighted;
  excerpts only.
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (read in the HTML at consc.net; the page's own
  header reads "[Published in the Journal of Consciousness Studies
  2(3):200-19, 1995 ...]". The online text carries no page numbers;
  section numbers are used as locators.)

> "The easy problems of consciousness are those that seem directly
>  susceptible to the standard methods of cognitive science, whereby a
>  phenomenon is explained in terms of computational or neural mechanisms.
>  The hard problems are those that seem to resist those methods. The easy
>  problems of consciousness include those of explaining the following
>  phenomena: the ability to discriminate, categorize, and react to
>  environmental stimuli; the integration of information by a cognitive
>  system; the reportability of mental states; the ability of a system to
>  access its own internal states; the focus of attention; the deliberate
>  control of behavior; the difference between wakefulness and sleep."  (§2)

> "The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience.
>  When we think and perceive, there is a whir of information-processing,
>  but there is also a subjective aspect. As Nagel (1974) has put it,
>  there is something it is like to be a conscious organism. This
>  subjective aspect is experience."  (§2)

> "Why doesn't all this information-processing go on 'in the dark', free
>  of any inner feel? Why is it that when electromagnetic waveforms
>  impinge on a retina and are discriminated and categorized by a visual
>  system, this discrimination and categorization is experienced as a
>  sensation of vivid red? We know that conscious experience does arise
>  when these functions are performed, but the very fact that it arises is
>  the central mystery. There is an explanatory gap (a term due to Levine
>  1983) between the functions and experience, and we need an explanatory
>  bridge to cross it."  (§2)

> "If someone says 'I can see that you have explained how DNA stores and
>  transmits hereditary information from one generation to the next, but
>  you have not explained how it is a gene', then they are making a
>  conceptual mistake. [...] But if someone says 'I can see that you have
>  explained how information is discriminated, integrated, and reported,
>  but you have not explained how it is experienced', they are not making
>  a conceptual mistake. This is a nontrivial further question."  (§2)

The Conscious Mind (bibliographic only; not read): David J. Chalmers, The
  Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory (New York: Oxford
  University Press, 1996), ISBN 9780195105537 (hardback) / 9780195117899
  (paperback), per Open Library. As reported by SEP, "Zombies" (Fall 2024
  edition, Robert Kirk), §1: "The most systematic use of the zombie idea
  against physicalism is by David Chalmers 1996"; and §3 gives the
  conceivability argument's simplest form — "Zombies are conceivable.
  Whatever is conceivable is possible. Therefore zombies are possible." —
  with references to "Chalmers 1996, 93–171"
  (https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/zombies/, §§1, 3).

Relied on for: the claims that Chalmers introduced the easy/hard problem
distinction with a specific list of easy problems, that the hard problem
is "the problem of experience" defined via Nagel's "something it is like",
that he explicitly credits Levine 1983 for "explanatory gap", and that
the 1996 book develops the zombie conceivability argument (the last
supported bibliographically and by SEP's report only).
Context: the paper goes on to reject reductive explanation for experience
and to sketch a "nonreductive" theory with principles of structural
coherence, organisational invariance, and a double-aspect view of
information; the consc.net header notes 26 commentary articles and
Chalmers's reply "Moving Forward on the Problem of Consciousness". The
ellipsis omits the sentence "All it means to be a gene is to be an entity
that performs the relevant storage and transmission function."

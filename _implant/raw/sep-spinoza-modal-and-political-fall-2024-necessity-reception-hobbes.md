(a) Newlands:

> "Spinoza’s views on necessity and possibility, which he claimed were the “principal foundation” of his Ethics (Ep75), have been less than well received by his readers, to put it mildly. From Spinoza’s contemporaries to our own, readers of the Ethics have denounced Spinoza’s views on modality as metaphysically confused at best, ethically nihilistic at worst."  (preamble)
> "considerable disagreement has emerged about Spinoza’s modal commitments."  (preamble)
> "Curley suggests that Spinoza’s finite modes are only partially determined by other finite modes (Curley 1969)."  (§1.2.4)
> "This is a notoriously difficult question facing Spinoza’s metaphysics, one that was raised by Leibniz just a year after Spinoza’s death (Leibniz 1969, 203)."  (§1.2.4)

(b) Steinberg:

> "At least in anglophone countries, Spinoza’s reputation as a political thinker is eclipsed by his reputation as a rationalist metaphysician."  (preamble)
> "Hobbes’ influence on Spinoza is unmistakable. We know that Spinoza read De Cive carefully and that it was among his possessions when he died in 1677."  (§1.2)
> "when he died on February 21, 1677."  (§4.3.3)
> "The publication of the unfinished TP in Spinoza’s posthumous Opera was met with relative indifference, upstaged as it was by the simultaneous appearance of Ethics (Laerke 2010, 122)."  (§6)

Relied on for: how readers received the necessity doctrine (Newlands's
report); Curley's partial-determination reading; Leibniz raising the
infinite-finite gap in 1678; the exact death date; Hobbes's De Cive among
Spinoza's possessions; the Ethics appearing in the posthumous Opera.
Context: Newlands's preamble goes on to say the picture of consensus would
be "quite misleading" and that whether Spinoza was a necessitarian is the
"predominant topic of discussion in the recent secondary literature". Steinberg §4.3.3 is on democracy in
the unfinished Political Treatise.

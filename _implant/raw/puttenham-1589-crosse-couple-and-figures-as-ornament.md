# Puttenham 1589 — the "Crosse-couple" (synoeciosis) and figures as ornament and "trespasses in speach"

Source: [George Puttenham], The Arte of English Poesie (London: Richard
  Field, 1589), Book III "Of Ornament": ch. 1 "Of Ornament Poeticall";
  ch. 7 "Of Figures and figuratiue speaches"; ch. 19 "Of Figures
  sententious, otherwise called Rhetoricall", the figure "Synteiosis, or
  the Crosse copling" (table of Book III: "Sineciosis, or the
  crossecoople", p. 172; "Antitheton, or the quareller", p. 175).

> "[Sidenote: _Synteiosis_, or the Crosse copling.]
> Ye haue another figure which me thinkes may well be called (not much
> sweruing from his originall in sence) the _Crosse-couple_, because it
> takes me two contrary words, and tieth them as it were in a paire of
> couples, and so makes them agree like good fellowes, as I saw once in
> Fraunce a wolfe coupled with a mastiffe, and a foxe with a hounde."  (III.19; table p. 172)

> "_Thus for your sake I daily dye,
>   And do but seeme to liue in deede:
>   Thus is my blisse but miserie,
>   My lucre losse without your meede._"  (III.19, Puttenham's own verses "for a forlorne louer")

Table of the third booke, verbatim: "Sineciosis, _or the crossecoople._ 172";
"Antitheton, _or the quareller, otherwise called the ouerthwart or rencounter._ 175";
"Paradoxon, _or the wonderer._ 189".

> "[Sidenote: _Antitheton_, or the renconter]
> Ye haue another figure very pleasnt and fit for amplification, which to
> answer the Greeke terme, we may call the encounter, but following the
> Latine name by reason of his contentious nature, we may call him the
> Quarreller"  (III.19; table p. 175)

> "As figures be the instruments of ornament in euery language, so be they
> also in a sorte abuses or rather trespasses in speach, because they passe
> the ordinary limits of common vtterance, and be occupied of purpose to
> deceiue the eare and also the minde, drawing it from plainnesse and
> simplicitie to a certaine doublenesse, whereby our talke is the more
> guilefull & abusing"  (III.7)

> "to such purpose as it may delight and allure as well
> the mynde as the eare of the hearers with a certaine noueltie and strange
> maner of conueyance, disguising it no litle from the ordinary and
> accustomed"  (III.1 "Of Ornament Poeticall")

Relied on for: Puttenham's English name for the figure of coupled
contraries ("Crosse-couple", "crossecoople") and its Greek label
("Synteiosis" in the sidenote, "Sineciosis" in the table); his account of
what it does; his distinction from antitheton ("the Quarreller"); his
double description of figures as ornament and as "trespasses in speach".
Context: Puttenham does not use the word "oxymoron"; the identification of
his Crosse-couple with oxymoron is Silva Rhetoricae's (entry
"synoeciosis", which quotes the same verses). The "trespasses" passage
opens his general chapter on figures and goes on to list metaphor,
allegory, irony and others; it is about all figures, not this one only.

## (a) Hind Swaraj / Indian Home Rule

> "It was written in 1908 during my return voyage from London to South Africa in answer to the Indian school of violence, and its prototype in South Africa."  (Reply to Critics, 1921)

> "It teaches the gospel of love in the place of that of hate. It replaces violence with self-sacrifice. It pits soul force against brute force."  (Reply to Critics)

> "I am individually working for the self-rule pictured therein. But to-day my corporate activity is undoubtedly devoted to the attainment of Parliamentary Swaraj in accordance with the wishes of the people of India."  (Reply to Critics)

> "The booklet is a severe condemnation of 'modern civilization.' It was written in 1908. My conviction is deeper to-day than ever."  (Reply to Critics)

> "Satyagrah--the law of love is the Law of life. Departure from it leads to disintegration. A firm adherence to it leads to regeneration."  (Foreword, 1919)

> "Civilization is that mode of conduct which points out to man the path of duty. Performance of duty and observance of morality are convertible terms. To observe morality is to attain mastery over our mind and our passions. So doing, we know ourselves."  (ch. XIII, "What is True Civilization?"; the Editor speaking)

> "It is Swaraj when we learn to rule ourselves. It is therefore in the palm of our hands."  (ch. XIV, "How can India become Free?")

> "The means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree; and there is just the same inviolable connection between the means and the end as there is between the seed and the tree."  (ch. XVI, "Brute Force")

> "Passive resistance is a method of securing rights by personal suffering; it is the reverse of resistance by arms. When I refuse to do a thing that is repugnant to my conscience, I use soul-force. For instance, the government of the day has passed a law which is applicable to me: I do not like it, if, by using violence, I force the government to repeal the law, I am employing what may be termed body-force. If I do not obey the law and accept the penalty for its breach, I use soul-force. It involves sacrifice of self."  (ch. XVII, "Passive Resistance")

> "Real home-rule is possible only where passive resistance is the guiding force of the people. Any other rule is foreign rule."  (ch. XVII)

Appendix "Some Authorities" lists, among others: "The Kingdom of God is
Within You", "What is Art?", "Letter to a Hindoo" (Tolstoy); "On the Duty
of Civil Disobedience", "Life Without Principle" (Thoreau); "Unto This
Last", "A Joy for Ever" (Ruskin); "Duties of Man" (Mazzini); "Defence and
Death of Socrates"--From _Plato_.

## (b) An Autobiography

> "Of these parents I was born at Porbandar, otherwise known as Sudamapuri, on the 2nd October, 1869."  (I.1 "Birth and Parentage")

> "But the New Testament produced a different impression, especially the Sermon on the Mount which went straight to my heart. I compared it with the Gita."  (I.20 "Acquaintance with Religions")

> "young mind tried to unify the teaching of the Gita, the Light of Asia and the Sermon on the Mount. That renunciation was the highest form of religion appealed to me greatly."  (I.20; preceded by "My")

> "Three moderns have left a deep impress on my life and captivated me: Raychandbhai by his living contact; Tolstoy by his book, The Kingdom of God Is Within You; and Ruskin by his Unto this Last."  (II.1 "Raychandbhai")

> "Tolstoy's The Kingdom of God Is Within You overwhelmed me. It left an abiding impression on me."  (II.15 "Religious Ferment")

> "the one that brought about an instantaneous and practical transformation in my life was Unto This Last. I translated it later into Gujarati, entitling it Sarvodaya (the welfare of all)."  (IV.18 "The Magic Spell of a Book")

> "When in a meeting of Europeans I found that the term 'passive resistance' was too narrowly construed, that it was supposed to be a weapon of the weak, that it could be characterized by hatred, and that it could finally manifest itself as violence, I had to demur to all these statements and explain the real nature of the Indian movement."  (IV.26 "The Birth of Satyagraha")

> "As a result Maganlal Gandhi coined the word ‘Sadagraha’ (Sat=truth, Agraha=firmness) and won the prize. But in order to make it clearer I changed the word to ‘Satyagraha’ which has since become current in Gujarati as a designation for the struggle."  (IV.26)

> "Ahimsa is a comprehensive principle. We are helpless mortals caught in the conflagration of himsa."  (IV.39 "A Spiritual Dilemma"; Fiala's SEP quotation of the same passage, Beacon ed. p. 439, is in raw/sep-pacifism-fall-2024-fiala-gandhi-ahimsa-satyagraha-critics.md)

> "What I want to achieve – what I have been striving and pining to achieve these thirty years - is self-realization, to see God face to face, to attain Moksha"  (Introduction)

> "All that I do by way of speaking and writing, and all my ventures in the political field, are directed to this same end."  (Introduction)

> "But I worship God as Truth only. I have not yet found Him, but I am seeking after Him."  (Introduction)

> "My uniform experience has convinced me that there is no other God than Truth."  (V, "Farewell")

> "a perfect vision of Truth can only follow a complete realization of Ahimsa. To see the universal and all-pervading Spirit of Truth face to face one must be able to love the meanest of creation as oneself."  (V, "Farewell")

> "That is why my devotion to Truth has drawn me into the field of politics; and I can say without the slightest hesitation, and yet in all humility, that those who say that religion has nothing to do with politics do not know what religion means."  (V, "Farewell")

> "The first edition of Gandhiji’s Autobiography was published in two volumes. Vol. I in 1927 and Vol. II in 1929."  (Desai, Translator's Preface, 1940)

Relied on for: the thinker page's dates of birth and of the two books,
satyagraha (name and definition), ahimsa, swaraj, means and ends, truth
and God (as Gandhi's own claims), the Sermon on the Mount, Tolstoy,
Ruskin and Thoreau as his reported reading.
Context: Hind Swaraj is a dialogue of "READER" and "EDITOR"; the quoted
chapter passages are the Editor's. In the 1921 "Reply to Critics" Gandhi
also warns "against thinking that I am to-day aiming at the Swaraj
described therein" and says his "corporate activity is undoubtedly
devoted to the attainment of Parliamentary Swaraj". In IV.26 the prize
for a new name was offered "through Indian Opinion".

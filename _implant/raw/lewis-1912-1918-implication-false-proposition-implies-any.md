# C. I. Lewis 1912 and 1918 — the "startling theorems" of material implication and the system of Strict Implication

> "THE development of the algebra of logic brings to light two somewhat startling theorems: (1) a false proposition implies any proposition, and (2) a true proposition is implied by any proposition."  (A, p. 522)

> "In themselves, they are neither mysterious sayings, nor great discoveries, nor gross absurdities."  (A, p. 522)

> "They exhibit only, in sharp outline, the meaning of “implies” which has been incorporated into the algebra."  (A, p. 522)

> "This is the famous — or notorious — theorem: "A false proposition implies any proposition"."  (B, p. 229, after theorem 9·32)

> "We have already called attention to the fact that this is not the usual meaning of "implies"."  (B, ch. V, p. 291)

> "We shall call it the system of Strict Implication."  (B, ch. V, p. 291)

> "If p is impossible (not self-consistent, absurd), then p strictly implies any proposition, q."  (B, ch. V, theorem 3·52, p. 303)

> "It does not contain the useless and doubtful theorems such as "A false proposition implies any proposition", and "A true proposition is implied by any proposition"."  (B, p. 319, of the "Calculus of Ordinary Inference")

Relied on for: Lewis naming the two theorems in 1912 and saying what they
"exhibit"; his 1918 system of Strict Implication introduced against the
divergence from "the usual meaning"; the strict analogue 3·52 that an
impossible proposition strictly implies any proposition.
Context: (A) continues with examples ("Caesar did not die" implies "the
moon is made of green cheese", p. 527) and argues the algebra's
"implies" differs from that of "ordinary valid inference". (B) p. 291
opens chapter V by contrasting material implication with "the 'implies'
of ordinary inference"; 3·52 sits in a table pairing material theorems
(3·42 "If p is false, then p materially implies any proposition, q")
with strict ones. Quotation (A, p. 522) uses double curly quotes in the
OCR as ‘‘implies’’; normalised here.

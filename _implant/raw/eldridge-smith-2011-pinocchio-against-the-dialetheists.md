# Eldridge-Smith 2011 — Pinocchio against the dialetheists (origin, empirical predicate, metaphysical impossibility)

Source: Peter Eldridge-Smith, "Pinocchio against the dialetheists",
  Analysis 71(2), April 2011, pp. 306–308.
Original: doi:10.1093/analys/anr007 (verified by DOI content negotiation:
  Analysis 71(2) 306–308, published-print 2011-04-01); open copy in the ANU
  Open Research repository, http://hdl.handle.net/1885/54731
  (copyrighted, "All rights reserved"; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-02 (PDF from the ANU repository, text via pdftotext;
  quotations copied from the extracted text, line breaks and hyphenation
  at line ends removed)

> "Pinocchio, who came to life like a little boy – but with such a nose for truth that it would grow if, and only if, he was telling a lie."  (p. 306)

> "Should Pinocchio say that his nose is growing, a contradiction would obtain (Eldridge-Smith and Eldridge-Smith 2010)."  (p. 306)

> "Astute dialetheists do not believe in metaphysical contradictions, just semantic ones."  (p. 307)

> "It has been substantially demonstrated by Priest (2006) that this can be made systematic, and that such a system of paraconsistent logic is a solution to the Liar paradox."  (p. 307)

> "However, the Pinocchio paradox, devised by my daughter, Veronique, is a version of the Liar paradox that resists such a solution."  (p. 307)

> "The Pinocchio paradox turns on the truth of Pinocchio – whose nose grows if, and only if, what he is saying is not true – saying ‘My nose is growing.’ ‘Is growing’ is an empirical predicate, not a semantic one."  (p. 307)

> "If it is a true contradiction that Pinocchio’s nose grows and does not grow, then such a world is metaphysically impossible, not merely semantically impossible."  (p. 307)

> "The Pinocchio paradox was devised by Veronique Eldridge-Smith in February 2001."  (p. 307, n. 1)

> "I thank the referee and the editor for pointing out that the Pinocchio paradox has intellectual ancestors also involving empirical predicates. These are scenarios about promises paradoxically thwarted by logic."  (p. 307, n. 1)

> "For example, in Buridan’s 17th sophism in Chapter 8 of his Sophismata, Plato promises to either let Socrates across a bridge or throw him into the water depending on whether his next statement is respectively true or false."  (pp. 307–308, n. 1)

Relied on for: the principle and the statement; the origin (Veronique
Eldridge-Smith, February 2001); the empirical-not-semantic claim; the
argument against semantic (not metaphysical) dialetheism and Priest 2006;
Buridan's bridge as an ancestor with empirical predicates.
Context: most of the note is a fable (Pinocchio in politics, Giuseppe as
counsellor, a "dialetheist lord" named Protagoras-Euathlas); the argument
proper is the paragraph beginning "Dialetheists astutely dodge Explosion",
which says dialetheists treat being in and not in a room as "a semantic
contradiction in the extension of the predicate ‘am in the room’". The
reference list cites only Eldridge-Smith & Eldridge-Smith 2010 and Priest
2006 (In Contradiction, 2nd edn).

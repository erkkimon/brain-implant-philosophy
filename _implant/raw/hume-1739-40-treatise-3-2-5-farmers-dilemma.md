# Hume 1739–40 — the two farmers (Treatise 3.2.5), read by Skyrms and Vanderschraaf as an asynchronous prisoner's dilemma

Source: David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III "Of Morals",
  Part II "Of Justice and Injustice", Sect. V "Of the Obligation of
  Promises" (T 3.2.5; the paragraph on the limits of the transference
  of property and the exchange of services).
Original: Project Gutenberg eBook #4705 (release 2003-12-01, updated
  2025-06-10), https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4705 — text of the
  original edition (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain-text file fetched from gutenberg.org,
  passage located by grep and read in context.)

> "Your corn is ripe to-day; mine will be so tomorrow. It is profitable for us both, that I should labour with you to-day, and that you should aid me to-morrow. I have no kindness for you, and know you have as little for me. I will not, therefore, take any pains upon your account; and should I labour with you upon my own account, in expectation of a return, I know I should be disappointed, and that I should in vain depend upon your gratitude. Here then I leave you to labour alone: You treat me in the same manner. The seasons change; and both of us lose our harvests for want of mutual confidence and security."  (T 3.2.5, Gutenberg text)

Relied on for: the farmers passage that Kuhn (SEP "Prisoner's Dilemma",
§9) reports Skyrms (1998) and Vanderschraaf identify as an asynchronous
PD, "the farmer's dilemma".
Context: Hume has just said the transference of property cannot cover
"services and actions, which we may exchange to our mutual interest and
advantage"; the next paragraph turns to how promises, as a human
convention, remedy this. The SEP entry quotes the passage in a
different spelling ("’Tis profitable", "shou'd"), from another edition;
the Gutenberg wording is given here. Date 1739–1740 as in the SEP
"David Hume" entry (Morris & Brown, Fall 2024 archive), checked
2026-09-27.

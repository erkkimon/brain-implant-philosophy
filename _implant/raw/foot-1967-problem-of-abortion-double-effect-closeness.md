# Foot 1967 — "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect": definition, Hart's query, the closeness question

Source: Philippa Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the
  Double Effect", Oxford Review no. 5 (1967), pp. 5–15; repr. in Foot,
  Virtues and Vices (Oxford: Blackwell / Berkeley: University of California
  Press, 1978), pp. 19–32 (OUP online DOI 10.1093/0199252866.003.0002,
  Crossref pp. 19-32).

> "As used in the abortion argument this doctrine has often seemed to non-Catholics to be a piece of complete sophistry"  (opening; continued "In the last number of the Oxford Review it was given short shrift by Professor Hart")

> "By ‘the doctrine of the double effect’ I mean the thesis that it is sometimes permissible to bring about by oblique intention what one may not directly intend."  (definition)

> "I believe that those who use the doctrine of the double effect would rightly reject such a suggestion, though they will, of course, have considerable difficulty in explaining where the line is to be drawn."  (fat-man-in-the-cave case)

> "What is to be the criterion of ‘closeness’ if we say that anything very close to what we are literally aiming at counts as if part of our aim?"  (after the cave case)

> "This last application of the doctrine has been queried by Professor Hart on the ground that the child’s death is not strictly a means to saving the mother’s life and should logically be treated as an unwanted but foreseen consequence by those who make use of the distinction between direct and oblique intention."  (craniotomy)

> "I have only tried to show that even if we reject the doctrine of the double effect we are not forced to the conclusion that the size of the evil must always be our guide."  (before returning to abortion)

Foot's footnotes as printed: "H. L. A. Hart, ‘Intention and Punishment’, Oxford Review, Number 4, Hilary 1967."; "See, e.g., J. Bennett, ‘Whatever the Consequences’, Analysis, January 1966, and G. E. M. Anscombe’s reply in Analysis, June 1966."

Relied on for: the closeness problem as Foot states it (cave, craniotomy),
  Hart's query as Foot reports it, and Foot's own conclusion that rejecting
  the doctrine does not force a size-of-evil rule.
Context: Foot sets out the doctrine to test it against the abortion
  debate; she goes on to propose the distinction between negative and
  positive duties (the tram driver and judge cases) as doing the work she
  finds the doctrine unable to do. Bennett 1966 (DOI
  10.1093/analys/26.3.83) is cited in her note on the mad-murderer case.

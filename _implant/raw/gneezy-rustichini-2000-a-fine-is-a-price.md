# Gneezy & Rustichini 2000 — "A Fine Is a Price": the Haifa day-care field study

> "The deterrence hypothesis predicts that the introduction of a penalty that leaves everything else unchanged will reduce the occurrence of the behavior subject to the fine. We present the result of a field study in a group of day-care centers that contradicts this prediction."  (abstract, p. 1)

> "We introduced a monetary fine for late-coming parents. As a result, the number of late-coming parents increased significantly. After the fine was removed no reduction occurred. We argue that penalties are usually introduced into an incomplete contract, social or private."  (abstract, p. 1)

> "If this is true, the deterrence hypothesis loses its predictive strength, since the clause ‘‘everything else is left unchanged’’ might be hard to satisfy."  (abstract, p. 1)

> "Our data include observations of 10 day-care centers over a period of 20 weeks. In the first 4 weeks we simply observed the number of parents who arrived late. At the beginning of the fifth week we introduced a fine in six of the 10 day-care centers."  (§I, p. 3)

> "No fine was introduced in the four other day-care centers, which served as a control group."  (§I, p. 3)

> "The fine was removed at the beginning of the seventeenth week. In the following 4 weeks the number of parents coming late remained at the same high level of the previous period, higher than in the initial 4 weeks."  (§I, p. 3)

> "We do not claim that the effect is similar every time a fine is introduced. It is easy to speculate that no parent would come late if a very large fine were involved."  (§I, p. 3)

> "The introduction of the fine into this incomplete contract, however, reshapes the parents’ perception of this environment."  (§I, p. 3)

> "The announcement specified that the fine would be NIS"  …  "10 for a delay of 10 minutes or more."  (§II.B; the sentence runs across a page break in the PDF)

> "The fine was per child; thus, if parents had two children in the center and they came late, they had to pay NIS 20."  (§II.B)

> "which had been selected randomly."  (§II.B; of the six centres given the fine)

> "The rate finally settled, at a level that was higher, and almost twice as large as the initial one."  (§III "Results", p. 7)

> "So an additional norm seems to be needed: ‘‘A fine is a price.’’"  (§IV "Interpretation of the Results", p. 14)

> "Perhaps a third social norm is needed: ‘‘Once a commodity, always a commodity.’’"  (§IV "Interpretation of the Results", p. 14)

> "Overall, three norms seem necessary to explain three facts. This may not look optimal from a methodological point of view, but the real test of this explanation is in an independent verification of the fact that these norms are indeed active."  (§IV "Interpretation of the Results", p. 14)

> "Fines, as well as rewards, are decided in a larger context, which for convenience we may define as a game."  (§V, p. 15)

Relied on for: the design and finding of the day-care study (as the authors report it); the authors' incomplete-contract reading; the "A fine is a price" and "Once a commodity, always a commodity" norms; their own disclaimer about very large fines.
Context: the paper also gives a formal model of differential information and multiple equilibria (§IV.A–C). The authors frame their result against the deterrence hypothesis in law and the punishment literature in psychology; the words "crowding out" and "intrinsic motivation" do not occur in the extracted text (grep, 2026-10-09) — that reading of their study is Frey & Jegen's (see frey-jegen-2001-motivation-crowding-theory-survey.md).

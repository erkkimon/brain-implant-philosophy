# Kripke 1980 — Naming and Necessity, Lecture III: the modal argument against identity theory, as reported by SEP

Source: Saul A. Kripke, Naming and Necessity (Cambridge, MA: Harvard
  University Press, 1980; the lectures were first published in 1972 in
  D. Davidson & G. Harman (eds.), Semantics of Natural Language), Lecture

SEP, "The Mind/Brain Identity Theory" (Fall 2024 edition; J. J. C. Smart),
§8 "Later Objections to the Identity Theory"
(https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/mind-identity/):

> "Kripke (1980) calls an expression a rigid designator if it refers to
>  the same object in every possible world. [...] Kripke considers the
>  identity 'heat = molecular motion', and holds that this is true in
>  every possible world and so is a necessary truth. [...] Kripke says
>  (1980, p. 151) that when we think that molecular motion might exist in
>  the absence of heat we are confusing this with thinking that the
>  molecular motion might have existed without being felt as heat. He asks
>  whether it is analogously possible that if pain is a certain sort of
>  brain process that it has existed without being felt as pain. He
>  suggests that the answer is 'No'. [...] Kripke concludes (p. 152) that
>  the apparent contingency of the connection between the mental state and
>  the corresponding brain state thus cannot be explained by some sort of
>  qualitative analogue as in the case of heat."  (§8)

SEP, "Zombies" (Fall 2024 edition; Robert Kirk), §2 and §3
(https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/zombies/):

> "A metaphor of Saul Kripke's helps to show how the zombie idea threatens
>  physicalism (Kripke 1972/80, 153f.). Imagine God creating the world and
>  deciding to bring into existence the whole of the purely physical
>  universe. Having created this physical universe, did he have to do any
>  more work to provide for consciousness? Answering yes to this question
>  implies there is more to consciousness than the physical facts alone
>  can supply."  (§2)

> "The simplest version of this argument goes: Zombies are conceivable.
>  Whatever is conceivable is possible. Therefore zombies are possible.
>  (Kripke used a similar argument in his 1972/80. [...])"  (§3)

Relied on for: the claims that Kripke's rigid-designator apparatus turns
true identities into necessary ones, that he argued the pain/C-fibre
identity cannot be defended by the "felt as heat" move that rescues
heat/molecular motion, and that his God-creating-the-world image is the
standard ancestor of the zombie conceivability argument — all as reported
by SEP, not from Kripke's text.
Context: the SEP identity-theory entry is written by Smart, an identity
theorist, who in the same section offers his own replies (counterpart
contextualism; "having a pain" rather than "pain"); the passage is
therefore a party's report of an opponent. Levine 1983 (see
levine-1983-materialism-and-qualia-explanatory-gap.md) explicitly takes
Kripke's argument as its starting point. Ellipses omit Smart's remarks on
counterpart theory, twin earth and radiant heat, and SEP's list of later
versions of the conceivability argument.

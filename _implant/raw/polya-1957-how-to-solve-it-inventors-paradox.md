> "Inventor’s paradox. The more ambitious plan may have more chances of success."  (p. 121, entry head)

> "This sounds paradoxical. Yet, when passing from one problem to another, we may often observe that the new, more ambitious problem is easier to handle than the original problem. More questions may be easier to answer than just one question. The more comprehensive theorem may be easier to prove, the more general problem may be easier to solve."  (p. 121)

> "The paradox disappears if we look closer at a few examples (GENERALIZATION, 2; INDUCTION AND MATHEMATICAL INDUCTION, 7). The more ambitious plan may have more chances of success provided it is not based on mere pretension but on some vision of the things beyond those immediately present."  (pp. 121–122)

Auxiliary problem, §8 "Unilateral reduction":

> "If we could solve A we could hence derive the full solution of B. But not conversely"  (p. 55)

> "Let us call A the more ambitious, and B the less ambitious of the two problems."  (p. 55)

> "These examples show that, with some luck, we may be able to use a less ambitious auxiliary problem as a stepping stone"  (p. 56)

> "Unilateral reduction to a more ambitious problem may also be successful."  (p. 56)

> "In fact, the more ambitious problem may be more accessible; this is the INVENTOR’S PARADOX."  (p. 56)

Generalization, §2 (the octahedron):

> "A straight line and a regular octahedron are given in position. Find a plane that passes through the given line and bisects the volume of the given octahedron."  (p. 108)

> "A straight line and a solid with a center of symmetry are given in position. Find a plane that passes through the given line and bisects the volume of the given solid."  (p. 109)

> "The plane required passes, of course, through the center of symmetry of the solid, and is determined by this point and the given line."  (p. 109)

> "The reader will not fail to observe that the second problem is more general than the first, and, nevertheless, much easier than the first. In fact, our main achievement in solving the first problem was to invent the second problem."  (p. 109)

> "The more general problem may be easier to solve. This sounds paradoxical but, after the foregoing example, it should not be paradoxical to us."  (p. 109)

> "The main achievement in solving the special problem was to invent the general problem."  (p. 109)

Induction and mathematical induction (sum of cubes):

> "The sum of the first n cubes is a square."  (p. 115, the conjecture "strongly suggested by induction")

The more precise form reached in §2 (p. 117) is, in the OCR's garbled typesetting, 1³ + 2³ + 3³ + ··· + n³ = (1 + 2 + 3 + ··· + n)² for n = 1, 2, 3, … (formula reconstructed from the table on p. 115: 1 = 1², 1 + 8 = 9 = 3², …, 1 + 8 + 27 + 64 + 125 = 225 = 15², and the bases 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 = 1, 1+2, 1+2+3, …).

> "Thus, the stronger theorem is easier to master than the weaker one; this is the INVENTOR’S PARADOX."  (p. 121)

> "The second assertion is stronger; it implies immediately the first, whereas the somewhat “hazy” first assertion can hardly imply the more “clear-cut’’ second one."  (p. 121)

> "Dealing with the first assertion, and ignoring the precision added to it by the second one, we should scarcely have been able to find such a proof."  (p. 121)

Problem 6 (Part IV, solution):

> "The required line passes, of course, through the center of symmetry. See INVENTOR’S PARADOX."  (p. 243; the problem: a point and a plane figure with a centre of symmetry; find a line through the point bisecting the area)

Relied on for: Pólya's name and statement of the inventor's paradox; his own "This sounds paradoxical" and "The paradox disappears"; his proviso; the octahedron, sum-of-cubes and plane-figure examples; the definition of more/less ambitious problems by unilateral reduction; the remark that a less ambitious auxiliary problem may also work.
Context: the dictionary entry is short (pp. 121–122) and consists of cross-references; the examples sit in the entries it names. The induction entry also says the names "induction" and "mathematical induction" are connected by "a random circumstance" (p. 121).

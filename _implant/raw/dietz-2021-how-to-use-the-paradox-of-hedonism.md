# Dietz 2021 — "How to Use the Paradox of Hedonism": the paradox as an objection to hedonism about well-being

> "The paradox of hedonism: If one intrinsically desires nothing other than one’s own pleasure, then one will be prevented from obtaining pleasure."  (p. 388)

> "The paradox of hedonism is a familiar idea in moral philosophy; the classic statement of the paradox, by Joseph Butler, dates back to 1726."  (p. 388)

> "In fact, in recent general discussions of hedonism about well-being, the paradox of hedonism is often not even mentioned."  (p. 388)

> "Note that the phenomenon is meant to be “paradoxical” not in the strong sense of presenting us with an apparently sound argument leading to an absurd conclusion, but rather in the milder sense of being a surprising observation."  (p. 388, n. 3)

> "For example, the idea is not mentioned in Feldman (2004), a prominent book-length defense of hedonism about well-being; nor is it mentioned in Gregory (2016)."  (p. 388, n. 4)

> "C. D. Broad finds Butler’s observations “profoundly true and important”: the “most wretched lives are led by men who have nothing to do but think of their own happiness and scheme for it.”"  (p. 389, citing Broad 1930: 74)

> "In his autobiography, John Stuart Mill reports having experienced the paradox firsthand in the form of a depression."  (p. 389)

> "First, it may just not be obvious how, exactly, the paradox of hedonism is supposed to provide us with an objection to hedonism about welfare."  (p. 390)

> "It is commonly assumed that this phenomenon, assuming that it is real, would have to be explained by contingent empirical facts, such as the difficulty of predicting what will make us happy."  (p. 390)

> "In other words, Butler’s account relies on an early version of a desire-based theory of pleasure, which remains a leading view in the contemporary literature, though it does face significant rivals."  (pp. 390–391)

> "Butler’s rough idea is that pleasure has to do with satisfying our desires, and so in order to obtain pleasure, we had better have independent desires to satisfy."  (p. 391)

> "Axiological hedonism: What is intrinsically good for subjects, and (therefore) what is intrinsically desirable simpliciter, is (just) pleasure."  (p. 393)

> "P1. Rational Desire: If you are rational, then if you believe that X is not (intrinsically) desirable, you will not (intrinsically) desire X."  (p. 394)

> "P3. The paradox of hedonism: If we are rational, and we intrinsically desire nothing other than pleasure, then we will be significantly prevented from obtaining pleasure."  (p. 394)

> "P4. The acceptability requirement: Other things equal, we should reject an axiological theory if rational agents who believe in its claims about which objects are intrinsically desirable will be significantly prevented from obtaining those objects."  (p. 394)

> "C3. Therefore, other things equal, we should reject axiological hedonism."  (p. 394)

> "Since the argument is clearly valid, it shows that we face an inconsistent set: Rational Desire, the acceptability requirement, the paradox of hedonism, and axiological hedonism"  (p. 394)

> "It has been observed that there is a parallel “paradox of consequentialism,” that is, that thinking in consequentialist terms can prevent agents from producing the best consequences."  (p. 394, n. 16)

> "One response to this worry has been to argue that even if it would be a bad idea for agents to believe in or try to follow a consequentialist theory, this does not mean that the theory is incorrect."  (p. 394, n. 16)

> "The parallel response in this context would be to deny our acceptability requirement."  (p. 394, n. 16)

> "The parallel response in this context would be to deny Rational Desire, and thus to argue that we could accept hedonism without falling into the paradox of hedonism."  (p. 394, n. 16)

> "That is, these theories could claim that pleasure, desire satisfaction, or a hybrid of objective and subjective conditions are not themselves intrinsically good for us, but rather that they operate in the background, making other states intrinsically good for us."  (pp. 408–409)

> "I have also argued that the paradox of hedonism, if it is a problem at all, is in fact a symptom of a deeper problem, the paradox of desire satisfaction, which challenges all theories which claim that satisfying our desires is an essential part of well-being."  (p. 409)

Context: P2 and C1–C2 of the argument (p. 394) read: "P2. If axiological
  hedonism is true, then pleasure is the only thing that is intrinsically
  desirable. Thus, if you believe in axiological hedonism, you will believe
  that nothing other than pleasure is intrinsically desirable." "C1.
  Therefore, rational agents who believe in axiological hedonism will
  intrinsically desire nothing other than pleasure." "C2. Therefore,
  rational agents who believe in axiological hedonism will be significantly
  prevented from obtaining pleasure." Dietz's target is hedonism together
  with welfarism; he names Eggleston (2013a) "Paradox of Happiness" and
  his own Dietz (2019), https://doi.org/10.1080/00048402.2018.1483409, on
  explaining the paradox. Sources n. 16 lists for the first response: Hare

  Railton's (1984) "sophisticated consequentialism".

# Diogenes Laertius, Lives II.65–97 — Aristippus, his succession and the Cyrenaic doctrines (Hicks tr.)

Source: Diogenes Laërtius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers, tr. R. D. Hicks
  (Loeb Classical Library, 1925), Book II, Life of Aristippus (§§65–85)
  and the Cyrenaic schools (§§85–97; Theodorus continues to §104). Section
  numbers are the anchors printed in the Wikisource transcription.
Original: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_II
  (Wikisource, public domain in the United States; translator Robert Drew
  Hicks named in the page header). Excerpts only.

  from it, wiki markup and footnotes removed, checked by script).

> "Aristippus was by birth a citizen of Cyrene and, as Aeschines informs us, was drawn to Athens by the fame of Socrates."  (II.65)

> "Having come forward as a lecturer or sophist, as Phanias of Eresus, the Peripatetic, informs us, he was the first of the followers of Socrates to charge fees and to send money to his master."  (II.65)

> "Xenophon was no friend to Aristippus; and for this reason he has made Socrates direct against Aristippus the discourse in which he denounces pleasure."  (II.65)

> "Not but what Theodorus in his work On Sects abuses him, and so does Plato in the dialogue On the Soul, as has been shown elsewhere."  (II.65; Hicks's footnote refers this to the Phaedo, 59c)

> "He derived pleasure from what was present, and did not toil to procure the enjoyment of something not present"  (II.66; the Wikisource text has no full stop here)

> "To those who censured him his defence was, "I have Lais, not she me; and it is not abstinence from pleasures that is best, but mastery over them without ever being worsted.""  (II.75)

> "There have been four men called Aristippus, (1) our present subject, (2) the author of a book about Arcadia, (3) the grandchild by a daughter of the first Aristippus, who was known as his mother's pupil, (4) a philosopher of the New Academy."  (II.83)

> "Some also maintain that he wrote six Books of Essays; others, and among them Sosicrates of Rhodes, that he wrote none at all."  (II.84)

> "He laid down as the end the smooth motion resulting in sensation."  (II.85)

> "The disciples of Aristippus were his daughter Arete, Aethiops of Ptolemais, and Antipater of Cyrene. The pupil of Arete was Aristippus, who went by the name of mother-taught, and his pupil was Theodorus, known as the atheist, subsequently as "god.""  (II.86)

> "Antipater's pupil was Epitimides of Cyrene, his was Paraebates, and he had as pupils Hegesias, the advocate of suicide, and Anniceris, who ransomed Plato."  (II.86)

> "They laid down that there are two states, pleasure and pain, the former a smooth, the latter a rough motion, and that pleasure does not differ from pleasure nor is one pleasure more pleasant than another."  (II.86)

> "However, the bodily pleasure which is the end is, according to Panaetius in his work On the Sects, not the settled pleasure following the removal of pains, or the sort of freedom from discomfort which Epicurus accepts and maintains to be the end."  (II.87)

> "Our end is particular pleasure, whereas happiness is the sum total of all particular pleasures, in which are included both past and future pleasures."  (II.87)

> "Particular pleasure is desirable for its own sake, whereas happiness is desirable not for its own sake but for the sake of particular pleasures."  (II.88)

> "That pleasure is the end is proved by the fact that from our youth up we are instinctively attracted to it, and, when we obtain it, seek for nothing more, and shun nothing so much as its opposite, pain."  (II.88)

> "The removal of pain, however, which is put forward in Epicurus, seems to them not to be pleasure at all, any more than the absence of pleasure is pain."  (II.89)

> "Nor again do they admit that pleasure is derived from the memory or expectation of good, which was a doctrine of Epicurus."  (II.89)

> "However, they insist that bodily pleasures are far better than mental pleasures, and bodily pains far worse than mental pains, and that this is the reason why offenders are punished with the former."  (II.90)

> "It is sufficient even if we enjoy but each single pleasure as it comes."  (II.91)

> "They affirm that mental affections can be known, but not the objects from which they come; and they abandoned the study of nature because of its apparent uncertainty, but fastened on logical inquiries because of their utility."  (II.92)

> "They also held that nothing is just or honourable or base by nature, but only by convention and custom."  (II.93)

> "They denied the possibility of happiness, for the body is infected with much suffering, while the soul shares in the sufferings of the body and is a prey to disturbance, and fortune often disappoints."  (II.94, the school of Hegesias)

> "The school of Anniceris in other respects agreed with them, but admitted that friendship and gratitude and respect for parents do exist in real life, and that a good man will sometimes act out of patriotic motives."  (II.96)

> "Theodorus was a man who utterly rejected the current belief in the gods."  (II.97)

Relied on for: the life, the four men named Aristippus, the succession,
  the doctrines listed as Cyrenaic, and the explicit contrasts with
  Epicurus (thinkers/aristippus.md).
Context: §§66–83 are anecdotes and repartees; §85 attributes the end
  "smooth motion resulting in sensation" to Aristippus himself, while
  §§86–93 report the opinions of "Those then who adhered to the teaching of
  Aristippus and were known as Cyrenaics"; §§93–97 give the Hegesian and
  Annicerian variants, §§97–104 Theodorus.

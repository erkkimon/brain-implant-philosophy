> "The most well-know epistemic paradox is the paradox of the knower (Kaplan & Montague, 1960; Montague, 1963)."  (§1.3; "well-know" sic)

> "This paradox has many equivalent formulations, one of them based on the sentence “This sentence is not known by anyone.”"  (§1.3)

> "KS\) is obviously quite similar to the liar sentence, except the central concept involved is knowledge rather than truth."  (§1.3; the sentence is "\(KS\) is obviously quite similar ...")

> "Assume to obtain a contradiction that \(KS\) is not true. Then what \(KS\) expresses cannot be the case, that is, \(KS\) must be known by someone. Since everything known is true (this is part of the definition of the concept of knowledge), \(KS\) is true, contradicting our assumption."  (§1.3)

> "The piece of reasoning just carried out to prove the truth of \(KS\) should be available to any agent (person) with sufficient reasoning capabilities."  (§1.3)

> "However, if \(KS\) is known by someone, then what it expresses is not the case, and thus it cannot be true. This is a contradiction, and thus we have a paradox."  (§1.3)

> "The epistemic paradoxes constitute a threat to the construction of formal theories of knowledge, as the paradoxes become formalisable in many such theories."  (§2.3)

> "First of all, all knowable sentences must be true."  (§2.3; A1: K⟨φ⟩ → φ)

> "Of course this principle must itself be knowable"  (§2.3; A2)

> "In addition, all theorems of first-order arithmetic ought to be knowable"  (§2.3; A3)

> "Furthermore, knowability must be closed under logical consequences"  (§2.3; A4)

> "Any formal theory extending first-order arithmetic and containing axiom schemas A1–A4 is inconsistent."  (§2.3, "Montague’s theorem")

> "Montague’s theorem shows that in the setting of first-order arithmetic we cannot have a theory of knowledge or knowability satisfying even the basic principles A1–A4."  (§2.3)

> "Montague’s theorem is a generalisation of Tarski’s theorem."  (§2.3)

> "One possible reply might be that even axiom schemas A1–A4 are too strong, and should be weakened further."  (§2.3)

> "It is however not at all clear that we can sensibly point out exactly which sentences are normal and which are pathological."  (§2.3)

> "Revières and Levesque (1988) showed that the principles stay consistent when only instantiated over the so-called regular sentences, and this result was later generalised to the more inclusive class of so-called RPQ sentences (Morreau and Kraus, 1998)."  (§2.3)

> "In the semantic treatment of knowledge, one generally avoids problems of self-reference, and thus inconsistency, but it is at the expense of the expressive power of the formalism"  (§2.3)

> "and a knowledge predicate to have (Montague’s theorem)."  (§2.4)

> "In the case of the epistemic paradoxes, a similar stratification could be obtained by making an explicit distinction between first-order knowledge (knowledge about the external world), second-order knowledge (knowledge about first-order knowledge), third-order knowledge (knowledge about second-order knowledge), and so on."  (§3.1)

> "This stratification actually comes for free in the semantic treatment of knowledge, where knowledge is formalised as a modal operator."  (§3.1)

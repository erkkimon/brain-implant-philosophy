# Rothbard 1973 — the nonaggression axiom, its three foundations, self-ownership alternatives, air pollution as aggression (For a New Liberty)

Source: Murray N. Rothbard, For a New Liberty: The Libertarian Manifesto,
  2nd ed. (Ludwig von Mises Institute, 2006; first ed. 1973, rev. 1978),
  ch. 2 "Property and Exchange" pp. 27–28, 30, 34; ch. 13 pp. 318–319.
Original: ISBN 978-0-945466-47-5; PDF published by the Mises Institute at

> "The libertarian creed rests upon one central axiom: that no man or group of men may aggress against the person or property of anyone else."  (p. 27)

> "This may be called the “nonaggression axiom.” “Aggression” is defined as the initiation of the use or threat of physical violence against the person or property of anyone else."  (p. 27)

> "Aggression is therefore synonymous with invasion."  (p. 27)

> "If no man may aggress against another; if, in short, everyone has the absolute right to be “free” from aggression, then this at once implies that the libertarian stands foursquare for what are generally known as “civil liberties”"  (p. 27)

> "there has been one central, dominant, and overriding aggressor upon all of these rights: the State."  (p. 28)

> "The libertarian, in short, insists on applying the general moral law to everyone, and makes no special exemptions for any person or group."  (p. 28)

> "Roughly, there are three broad types of foundation for the libertarian axiom, corresponding to three kinds of ethical philosophy: the emotivist, the utilitarian, and the natural rights viewpoint."  (p. 30)

> "There are then only two alternatives: either (1) a certain class of people, A, have the right to own another class, B; or (2) everyone has the right to own his own equal quotal share of everyone else."  (p. 34, on denying self-ownership)

> "All such emanations which injure person or property constitute aggression against the private property of the victims."  (p. 319)

> "Air pollution that injures others is aggression pure and simple."  (p. 319)

Relied on for: Rothbard's statement and definition of the axiom; his
  claim to apply one moral law to State and individual alike; his three
  candidate foundations; his self-ownership dilemma; his classification of
  injurious air pollution as aggression.
Context: p. 27 goes on to derive civil liberties, opposition to
  conscription and war, and "laissez-faire capitalism"; p. 28 names the
  State as "one central, dominant, and overriding aggressor"; pp. 30–31
  reject the emotivist and utilitarian foundations in favour of natural
  rights; p. 34 rejects the second alternative as the human race would
  "quickly perish"; p. 319 adds that government "has failed grievously to
  exercise its defense function against air pollution".

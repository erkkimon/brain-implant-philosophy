# Horace — Odes 3.11.35 "splendide mendax", Epistles 1.11.28 "strenua ... inertia", Epistles 1.12.19 "concordia discors"

Source: Q. Horatius Flaccus, Carmina III.11 (line 35); Epistulae I.11
  (line 28) and I.12 (line 19). Latin text with the edition's line
  numbers (every fifth line numbered) at The Latin Library; English in the
  literal prose translation of C. Smart, revised by T. A. Buckley.

Retrieved: 2026-09-28 (all three pages fetched; lines located by grep and

> "Una de multis face nuptiali
> digna periurum fuit in parentem
> splendide mendax et in omne virgo               35
>      nobilis aevom,"  (Odes 3.11.33–36)

> "caelum, non animum mutant, qui trans mare currunt.
> Strenua non exercet inertia; nauibus atque
> quadrigis petimus bene uiuere."  (Epistles 1.11.27–29; the Latin
> Library text reads "non", other editions "nos")

> "quid uelit et possit rerum concordia discors,
> Empedocles an Stertinium deliret acumen?               20"  (Epistles 1.12.19–20)

Smart/Buckley translation (Gutenberg #14020), verbatim:

> "One out of the many, worthy of the nuptial torch, was nobly false to her
> perjured parent"  (Odes 3.11)

> "they change their climate, not their disposition, who run beyond the
> sea: a busy idleness harrasses us"  (Epistles 1.11)

> "what is the intention and power of the jarring harmony of things,
> whether Empedocles or the clever Stertinius be in the wrong."  (Epistles 1.12)

Relied on for: the three Horace example rows and their translations.
Context: Odes 3.11 — Hypermestra, the one Danaid who spared her husband
against her father's order. Epistles 1.11 — to Bullatius, on travel as a
cure for restlessness. Epistles 1.12 — to Iccius, on natural philosophy
("Empedocles"). That these phrases are oxymora is said by Jebb (1900, of
"splendide mendax") and Lewis & Short (1879, of "strenua ... inertia"):

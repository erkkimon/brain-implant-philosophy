§1 — the question and Hart's three questions:

> "How can a practice that not only burdens those subjected to it but aims to burden them, and which conveys society’s condemnation, be justified?"  (§1)

> "As Hart famously pointed out (Hart 1968: 1–27), we must distinguish at least three justificatory issues."  (§1)

> "there is a significant strand of ‘abolitionist’ penal theorising"  (§1)

§2 — abolitionist critique of the concept of crime:

> "Some abolitionists, however, argue that we should seek to eliminate the concept of crime from our social vocabulary"  (§2)

> "the law ‘steals’ the conflicts which crime involves from those to whom they properly belong (Christie 1977)"  (§2)

§3 — consequentialist accounts and the objection from punishing the innocent:

> "We need not be Benthamite utilitarians to be moved by Bentham’s famous remark"  (§3)

> "It is commonly suggested that punishment can help to reduce crime by deterring, incapacitating, or reforming potential offenders"  (§3)

> "It is a contingent question whether punishment can satisfy these conditions, and some objections to punishment rest on the empirical claim that it cannot"  (§3; citing Wootton 1963; Menninger 1968; Golash 2005; Boonin 2008)

> "The most familiar line of objection to consequentialist penal theories contends that consequentialists would be committed to regarding manifestly unjust punishments (the punishment of those known to be innocent, for instance, or excessively harsh punishment of the guilty) to be in principle justified if they would efficiently serve the aim of crime reduction: but such punishments would be wrong, because they would be unjust"  (§3; the entry cites "McCloskey 1957: 468–69; Hart 1968, chs. 1–2; Ten 1987; Primoratz 1999, chs. 2–3; Boonin 2008: ch. 2")

> "One is to argue that such ‘unjust’punishments would be justified if they would really produce the best consequences"  (§3; citing "Smart 1973: 69–72; Bagaric and Amarasekara 2000")

> "Another is to argue that in the real world it is extremely unlikely that such punishments would ever be for the best, and even less likely that the agents involved could be trusted reliably to pick out those rare cases in which they would be"  (§3)

> "thus we, and especially our penal officials, will do best if we think and act as if such punishments are intrinsically wrong and unjustifiable"  (§3; citing "Rawls 1955; Hare 1981, chs. 3, 9.7")

> "to which the critic will respond that this still makes the wrongness of punishing a known innocent contingent on its effects, and fails to recognise the intrinsic wrong that such punishment does"  (§3; citing "Duff 1986: 151–64; Primoratz 1999, chs. 3.3, 6.5")

> "Another response is to argue that a richer or subtler account of the ends that the criminal law should serve will generate suitable protection against unjust punishments"  (§3; citing "Braithwaite and Pettit 1990, especially 71–76")

> "but the objection remains that any purely consequentialist account will make the protection of the innocent against injustice contingent on its instrumental contribution to the system’s aims"  (§3)

> "In Kantian terms, such punishment treats those punished as mere means to achieving some social good, rather than respecting them as ends in themselves (Kant 1797: 473; Murphy 1973)."  (§3)

> "Indeed, Kant himself suggested that as long as we reserve punishment only for those found guilty of crimes, then it is permissible to punish with an eye toward potential benefits (Kant 1797: 473)."  (§3)

> "consequentialist systems of punishment are inappropriately exclusionary"  (§3; "A more recent objection (Duff 2011: 75–79)"; reply cited to Hoskins 2011a: 379–81 and Chiao 2018: 36)

§4 — retributivist accounts:

> "retributivist accounts contend that punishment is justified as an intrinsically appropriate, because deserved, response to wrongdoing"  (§4)

> "Positive retributivism holds that an offender’s desert provides a reason in favour of punishment"  (§4)

> "Negative retributivism, by contrast, provides not a positive reason to punish, but rather a constraint on punishment"  (§4)

> "punishment removes that unfair advantage"  (§4; fair-play theory, citing H. Morris 1968; Murphy 1973 and others)

> "how are we to determine how great was the unfair advantage gained by a crime"  (§4; the entry's objection)

§5 — punishment as communication:

> "Perhaps the most influential version of retributivism in recent decades seeks the meaning and justification of punishment as a deserved response to crime in its expressive or communicative character."  (§5; citing "Feinberg 1970; Primoratz 1989")

> "His punishment then constitutes a kind of secular penance that he is required to undergo for his crime"  (§5; "see Duff 2001, 2011b")

> "in particular that it cannot show penal hard treatment to be a necessary aspect of a communicative enterprise which is still to respect offenders as responsible and rational agents who must be left free to remain unpersuaded"  (§5; objections cited to Bickenbach 1988; Ten 1990; von Hirsch 1999; Bagaric and Amarasekara 2000; and others)

> "Free will scepticism holds that people’s behaviour is the product of determinism, luck, or chance, and thus that we are not morally responsible for our behaviour in the respects that would justify the ideas that those who commit crimes are blameworthy and deserve punishment (see Pereboom 2013; Caruso 2018)."  (§5)

> "In response, retributivists may point out that only if punishment is grounded in desert can we provide more than contingent assurances against punishment of the innocent or disproportionate punishment of the guilty"  (§5)

> "the notion of deserved censure can have no appropriate place in a suitably reformed penal system"  (§5; the retributivist reply denies this: "this is not to say that ...")

§6 — mixed accounts:

> "On Hart’s account, the compelling rationale for punishment (what he termed its ‘general justifying aim’) lies in its beneficial effects, but our pursuit of that aim must be constrained by nonconsequentialist principles"  (§6)

> "Rawls’s account (1955) has also been characterised as a hybrid view of this sort, but in fact it is a version of rule utilitarianism"  (§6)

> "Critics have charged that hybrid accounts are ad hoc or internally inconsistent (see Kaufman 2008: 45–49)."  (§6)

> "In addition, retributivists argue that hybrid views that integrate consequentialist rationales with retributivist side-constraints thereby relegate retributivism to a merely subsidiary role"  (§6; citing Wood 2002: 303)

> "instead of addressing them as responsible moral agents, in terms of the moral reasons which justify the law’s demands on them, it addresses them as merely self-interested beings, in the coercive language of threat"  (§6)

> "deterrence treats ‘a man like a dog instead of with the freedom and respect due to him as a man’ (Hegel 1821: 246."  (§6)

> "Whether any of these is more plausible than pure consequentialist or pure retributivist alternatives is, not surprisingly, a matter of ongoing philosophical debate."  (§6)

§7 — abolition and alternatives:

> "The more powerful abolitionist challenge is that punishment cannot be justified even in principle."  (§7)

> "Abolitionists find that the various attempted justifications of this intentionally burdensome condemnatory treatment fail, and thus that the practice is morally wrong — not merely in practice but in principle."  (§7; classic texts listed as "Christie 1977, 1981; Hulsman 1986, 1991; de Haan 1990; Bianchi 1994"; against punishment in any form "Golash 2005; Boonin 2008; Zimmerman 2011")

> "Most prominently, many abolitionists look to ‘restorative justice’ as an alternative to punishment."  (§7)

> "Advocates of restorative justice often contrast it with ‘retributive’ justice; they argue that we should look for restoration rather than retribution or punishment, and seek to repair harms caused rather than to inflict punitive suffering for wrongs done. But one might regard this as a false dichotomy (see Allais 2011; Duff 2011a)."  (§7)

> "‘restoration’ is better understood, in this context, as the proper aim of punishment, not as an alternative to it"  (§7; "see further Duff 2001, ch. 3.4–6, but also Zedner 1994")

> "some free will sceptics (see Pereboom 2013; Caruso 2021) instead endorse incapacitating dangerous offenders on a model similar to that of public health quarantines"  (§7)

> "they cannot in principle rule out disproportionate punishment, or that they are inconsistent with respect for persons and with human dignity"  (§7; "see, e.g., Smilansky 2011")

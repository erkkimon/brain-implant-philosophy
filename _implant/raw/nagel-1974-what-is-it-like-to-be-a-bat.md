> "Consciousness is what makes the mind-body problem really intractable.
>  Perhaps that is why current discussions of the problem give it little
>  attention or get it obviously wrong."  (p. 435)

> "But no matter how the form may vary, the fact that an organism has
>  conscious experience at all means, basically, that there is something
>  it is like to be that organism. [...] But fundamentally an organism has
>  conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is
>  like to be that organism—something it is like for the organism. We may
>  call this the subjective character of experience. It is not captured by
>  any of the familiar, recently devised reductive analyses of the mental,
>  for all of them are logically compatible with its absence."  (p. 436)

> "If physicalism is to be defended, the phenomenological features must
>  themselves be given a physical account. But when we examine their
>  subjective character it seems that such a result is impossible. The
>  reason is that every subjective phenomenon is essentially connected
>  with a single point of view, and it seems inevitable that an objective,
>  physical theory will abandon that point of view."  (p. 437)

> "But bat sonar, though clearly a form of perception, is not similar in
>  its operation to any sense that we possess, and there is no reason to
>  suppose that it is subjectively like anything we can experience or
>  imagine. This appears to create difficulties for the notion of what it
>  is like to be a bat."  (p. 438)

Relied on for: the claims that Nagel defines conscious experience by the
"something it is like" criterion, calls this the subjective character of
experience, holds that functional and intentional analyses are
"logically compatible with its absence", and uses bat echolocation to
argue that an objective physical theory abandons the point of view on
which subjective phenomena depend.
Context: the paper does not conclude that physicalism is false; Nagel
writes later that "it would be a mistake to conclude that physicalism must
be false" and that we do not currently understand how it could be true.
Between the second and third quotations he allows that mental states may
cause behaviour and admit functional characterisations, denying only that
"this kind of thing exhausts their analysis". The ellipsis omits two
sentences on further implications about form and behaviour.

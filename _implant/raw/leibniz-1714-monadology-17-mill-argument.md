# Leibniz 1714 — Monadology §17: the mill argument

Source: G. W. Leibniz, The Monadology (written 1714), §17. Translated by
  Robert Latta, in Leibniz, The Monadology and Other Philosophical Writings
  (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1898); public-domain translation.
Original: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Monadology_(Leibniz,_tr._Latta)
  (public domain). Canonical locator: Monadology §17 (Gerhardt vi. 609).
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (read in the Wikisource raw wikitext of the Latta
  translation)

> "Moreover, it must be confessed that perception and that which depends
>  upon it are inexplicable on mechanical grounds, that is to say, by means
>  of figures and motions. And supposing there were a machine, so
>  constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, it might be conceived
>  as increased in size, while keeping the same proportions, so that one
>  might go into it as into a mill. That being so, we should, on examining
>  its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never
>  anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in a simple
>  substance, and not in a compound or in a machine, that perception must be
>  sought for."  (Monadology §17; Latta tr.)

Relied on for: the claim that Leibniz argued perception cannot be explained
by mechanical arrangement of parts, using the image of walking through an
enlarged thinking machine "as into a mill" — an early statement of what
later writers file under the explanatory gap.
Context: §§14–16 introduce perception as the internal state of a monad and
distinguish it from apperception; §17 ends by adding that "nothing but
this (namely, perceptions and their changes) can be found in a simple
substance", with Latta's cross-reference to the preface of the Theodicy.

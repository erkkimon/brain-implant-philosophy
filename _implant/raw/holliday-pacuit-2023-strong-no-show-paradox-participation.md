# Holliday & Pacuit 2023 — "no show paradox" versus "strong no show paradox"; a critique of participation

Source: Wesley H. Holliday & Eric Pacuit, "Split Cycle: a new
  Condorcet-consistent voting method independent of clones and immune to
  spoilers", Public Choice 197 (2023) 1–62, §1.2 "The Strong No Show
  Paradox" and Appendix B "Participation" (arXiv 2004.02350v10, read here).
Original: doi:10.1007/s11127-023-01042-3 (verified by content
  negotiation; licence CC BY 4.0); https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.02350
  (CC BY 4.0)
Retrieved: 2026-10-09 (arXiv PDF via pdftotext, passages grepped)

> "The term “no show paradox” was coined by Fishburn and Brams (1983) for violations of what is now called the negative involvement criterion (see Pérez 2001)."  (§1.2)

> "In an example of Fishburn and Brams, two voters are unable to make it to an Instant Runoff election, due to their car breaking down. They later realize that had they voted in the election, their least favorite candidate would have won."  (§1.2)

> "The failure of positive or negative involvement is now sometimes called the “strong no show paradox.”"  (§1.2)

> "The reason seems to be that Moulin (1988) changed the meaning of “no show paradox” to stand not for a violation of negative (or positive) involvement but rather for a violation of the participation criterion"  (§1.2)

> "In our view, this participation criterion is problematic."  (§1.2)

> "Of course, a method not satisfying participation will incentivize some strategic non-voting, as the voters in question will have an incentive not to vote (sincerely)."  (§1.2)

> "Thus, we are not so troubled by results showing that all Condorcet consistent voting methods fail versions of participation11 and therefore incentivize some strategic behavior. By contrast, we are troubled by failures of positive or negative involvement, as this shows that the method responds in the wrong way to unequivocal support for (resp. rejection of) a candidate."  (§1.2; "11" is a footnote marker in the extracted text)

> "Note that Moulin (1988) only proves participation failure for Condorcet consistent voting methods that are resolute, i.e., always pick a unique winner, which requires imposing an arbitrary tiebreaking rule that violates anonymity or neutrality (see Section 5.1.1)."  (§1.2, footnote 11)

> "Perez (2001) calls violations of positive involvement the “positive strong no show paradox” and violations of negative involvement the “negative strong no show paradox.”"  (§1.2, footnote 10)

> "A certain kind of neutrality then requires that the winner changes from x to y."  (§1.2; x and y are italic variables in the original)

> "Hence it is not only immune to spoilers but also immune to the strong no show paradox."  (§1.2, of Split Cycle)

> "In our view, the examples in the proof of Proposition B.6 show that participation is too strong to require."  (Appendix B)

Relied on for: the history of the term (Fishburn & Brams's sense versus
  Moulin's); the car-breakdown example as these authors report it; the
  distinction between participation and positive/negative involvement;
  the authors' assessment of participation and of Moulin's resoluteness
  assumption; Split Cycle's claimed immunity.
Context: The passage argues that when new voters rank x above y without x
  at the top or y at the bottom, majority cycles can make y's new position
  symmetric to x's old one, so that "a certain kind of neutrality then
  requires" the winner to change. Eric Pacuit is also the author of the
  SEP entry "Voting Methods".

## Sidgwick, Oct. 1894, p. 582

> "the second premiss “If A then B” is stated as a fact, quite independently of the truth of C; while “If A, then not B” is not stated as a fact, but as being possibly false."

> "Moreover, the two propositions can only be compatible on the assumption that A is false; and this assumption is not only not warranted by anything in the premisses, but would render each of the ‘compatible’ propositions meaningless as hypotheticals; they would become mere denials of the existence of certain classes."

> "It is of the essence of a hypothetical that both its clauses should (in the absence of further information) be regarded as possibly false."

## Johnson, Oct. 1894, p. 583

> "I presume Mr Lewis Carroll’s position to be that the problem raised by him in the last number of Mind presents a conflict between common sense and the rules of logic. It appears to me certain that the rules of logic when properly applied agree with the results of common sense."

> "But in reality the two sub-hypotheticals which form his principal consequents are not incompatible. For in saying that two propositions are incompatible we mean that their combination involves a logical impossibility. Now the combination of these sub-hypotheticals does not involve any impossibility, but involves merely the denial that Allen can be out."

> "Since, then, the two sub-hypotheticals taken separately would prove “Allen is in,” the two principal hypotheticals of which these are the consequents prove “If Carr is out Allen is in.”"

> "As regards the hypothetical of the general form “If A then B” we have interpreted this as the mere denial of the conjunction “A true and B false.” The consistent application of this interpretation yields the above solution to the whole problem and reduce to equivalence the four propositions numerated in Mr Lewis Carroll’s note."

## Sidgwick and Johnson, Jan. 1895, pp. 143–144

> "My contention is, not that the sentence ‘If A is true, B is not true’ must on all occasions mean something more than ‘the conjunction of A with B is false, but that in the context in which it is here placed, it requires to be taken in a less negative sense than this,—at least in the absence of a special warning that its meaning is to be thus limited."  (Sidgwick)

> "It is evident that Lewis Carroll has tacitly assumed, what Mr Johnson’s treatment of hypotheticals assumes openly, that these propositions are equivalent."  (Sidgwick, of "If C is true, then if A is true, B is not true" and "A, B, C, cannot be all true at once")

> "When Mr Sidgwick states that the two premisses (i.) ‘If A then B’ and (ii.) “ From the truth of C would follow ‘If A then not B’” constitute together a modus tollens, he is simply begging the question at issue."  (Johnson)

> "For the import or force of a given form of words is altogether unaffected by its possible falsity."  (Johnson)

## Jones, Jan. 1905, pp. 146–148

> "The view in question is a modification of that propounded by J. S. Mill, according to whom If A then C means The proposition C is a legitimate inference from the proposition A."  (p. 146)

> "(a) to calling Carr is out the “ Principal Antecedent,” and think that the whole root of the matter is that Carr is out is not the Antecedent of (2)."  (p. 147, after "I demur, however,")

> "They do this indeed if A gives the whole ground for the contradictory conclusions"  (p. 147, of "If A, then C" and "If A, then not C" proving "the falsity of the common antecedent")

> "Therefore if C is out, A is in."  (p. 148, last line of her derivation)

## "W." (Cook Wilson), Apr. 1905, pp. 292–293

> "It is difficult to see how this argument, which is no paradox but a paralogism, should have been taken seriously, and that the fallacy in it should not have had its true character made clear nor have received the simple treatment of which it is capable."  (p. 292)

> "Thus the conclusion is that the conjunction of Carr’s absence and Allen’s absence is impossible, not that Carr’s absence is impossible."  (p. 293)

> "The false reductio ad absurdum which constitutes the supposed paradox, concludes that Carr’s absence is impossible. It would necessitate, of course, if consistent, that Allen’s absence was also impossible."  (p. 293)

> "which is interpreted to mean that the proposition ‘If Allen is out Brown is in’ is a consequent of the assumption ‘Carr is out’. This is a mere verbal fallacy."  (p. 293, of proposition (v.) "If Carr is out, then, if Allen is out Brown is in")

> "But the proposition ‘If Allen is out Brown is in’ is a universal proposition which if valid at any time is valid at all times; it represents a rule which is always valid."  (p. 293)

> "The attempt to solve the ‘puzzle’ in the January number of Mind fails in all points."  (p. 293, of Jones 1905)

Relied on for: the positions of Sidgwick, Johnson, Jones and Cook Wilson on
the page; the 1894–1895 Sidgwick–Johnson exchange.
Context: Sidgwick and Johnson answer in the issue after Carroll's; Jones
reopens the "now ancient puzzle" in 1905, quoting Johnson; "W." answers
Jones in the next issue and gives a "direct argument" from "Either Allen is
in, or Brown is in, or Carr is in" and "If Allen is out Brown is out" to
"Either Allen is in, or Carr is in".

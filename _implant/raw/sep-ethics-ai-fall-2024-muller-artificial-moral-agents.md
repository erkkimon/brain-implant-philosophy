> "If one takes machine ethics to concern moral agents, in some substantial sense, then these agents can be called “artificial moral agents”, having rights and responsibilities."  (§2.9)

> "Several authors use “artificial moral agent” in a less demanding sense, borrowing from the use of “agent” in software engineering in which case matters of responsibility and rights will not arise (Allen, Varner, and Zinser 2000)."  (§2.9)

> "James Moor (2006) distinguishes four types of machine agents: ethical impact agents (e.g., robot jockeys), implicit ethical agents (e.g., safe autopilot), explicit ethical agents (e.g., using formal methods to estimate utility), and full ethical agents (who “can make explicit ethical judgments and generally is competent to reasonably justify them. An average adult human is a full ethical agent”.)"  (§2.9)

> "Programmed agents are sometimes not considered “full” agents because they are “competent without comprehension”, just like the neurons in a brain (Dennett 2017; Hakli and Mäkelä 2019)."  (§2.9)

> "In some discussions, the notion of “moral patient” plays a role: Ethical agents have responsibilities while ethical patients have rights because harm to them matters."  (§2.9)

> "It seems clear that some entities are patients without being agents, e.g., simple animals that can feel pain but cannot make justified choices."  (§2.9; Müller's assessment)

> "Usually, being a person is supposed to be what makes an entity a responsible agent, someone who can have duties and be the object of ethical concerns."  (§2.9)

> "With distributed agency comes distributed responsibility. (Taddeo and Floridi 2018: 751)."  (§2.9.1, end of a block quotation)

> "Fortunately, there is such representation available: the famous Prisoner’s Dilemma game, invented about 1950 by two Rand Corporation scientists."  (p. 2)

> "If both players defect:           Both players get $1."  (p. 2, payoff list; spacing as extracted)

> "If both players cooperate:        Both players get $3."  (p. 2)

> "The defector gets $5 and the cooperator gets zero."  (p. 2; right-hand column of the two-column payoff list, its two lines joined)

> "One can see that no matter what the other player does, defection yields a higher payoff than cooperation."  (p. 2)

> "Therefore the temptation is to defect. But, the dilemma is that if both defect, both do worse than if both had cooperated."  (p. 2)

> "Amazingly enough, the winner was the simplest of all candidates submitted. This was a strategy of simple reciprocity which cooperates on the first move and then does whatever the other player did on the previous move."  (p. 2; "sub-mitted" hyphenated across a line in the PDF)

> "The analysis of the data from these tournaments reveals four properties which tend to make a strategy successful: avoidance of unnecessary conflict by cooperating as long as the other player does, provocability in the face of an uncalled-for defection by the other, forgiveness after responding to a provocation, and clarity of behavior so that the other player can recognize and adapt to your pattern of action."  (pp. 2–3; "con-flict" hyphenated, and the sentence interrupted by the page header "The Evolution of Cooperation / 3", in the PDF)

> "For instance, the individuals involved do not have to be rational: The evolutionary process allows successful strategies to thrive, even if the players do not know why or how."  (p. 4)

> "If the game is played a known, finite number of times, the players likewise have no incentive to cooperate on the last move, nor on the next-to-last move since both can anticipate a defection by the other player."  (p. 4)

> "For cooperation to prove stable, the future must have a sufficiently large shadow."  (p. 4)

> "The foundation of cooperation is not really trust, but the durability of the relationship."  (p. 6)

Relied on for: Axelrod's own statement of the payoffs (5/3/1/0), the
dominance argument in his words, the tournament winner (reciprocity,
named Tit for Tat), his four success properties, the finite-iteration
unravelling and the "shadow of the future" condition, the WWI
"live and let live" example (pp. 3–4, not quoted), and his claim that
players "do not have to be rational".

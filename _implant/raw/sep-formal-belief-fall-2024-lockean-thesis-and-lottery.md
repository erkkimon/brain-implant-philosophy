## Deductive cogency (§4.2)

> "Deductive Cogency. The belief set Bel is consistent and B∈Bel if and only if ∩Bel ⊆ B."  (§4.2; LaTeX in the original)

> "In other words, deductive cogency means that there is a single, non-empty proposition, ∩Bel, which is the logically strongest proposition that the agent believes, entailing all her other beliefs."  (§4.2)

## The Lockean thesis (§4.2.2)

> "Perhaps full belief corresponds to partial belief above some threshold falling short of certainty. Foley (1993) dubbed this view the Lockean thesis, after some apparently similar remarks in Book IV of Locke’s (1690/1975) Essay Concerning Human Understanding."  (§4.2.2)

> "(SLT) There is a threshold s∈(1/2, 1) such that all rational ⟨Bel, Pr⟩ satisfy Bel(A) iff Pr(A) ≥ s."  (§4.2.2; LaTeX in the original)

> "(WLT) For every rational ⟨Bel, Pr⟩ there is a threshold s∈(1/2, 1) such that Bel(A) iff Pr(A) ≥ s."  (§4.2.2)

> "Most discussions of the Lockean thesis have in mind the strong thesis. More recent work, especially Leitgeb (2017), adopts the weaker thesis."  (§4.2.2)

## The lottery (§4.2.2)

> "The strong Lockean thesis gives rise to the well-known Lottery paradox, due originally to Kyburg (1961, 1997). The lesson of the Lottery is that the strong thesis is in tension with deductive cogency."  (§4.2.2)

> "Now think of a fair lottery with N tickets, where N is chosen large enough that 1−(1/N) ≥ s."  (§4.2.2)

> "Since cogency requires belief to be closed under conjunction, she ought to believe that all the tickets are losers. But now she violates cogency by believing both that every ticket is a loser and that some ticket is a winner. Since s was arbitrary, we have shown that no matter how high we set the threshold, there is some Lottery for which an agent must either violate the Lockean thesis or violate deductive cogency."  (§4.2.2)

> "According to Kyburg, what the paradox teaches is that we should give up on deductive cogency: full belief should not necessarily be closed under conjunction. Many others take the lesson of the Lottery to be that the strong Lockean thesis is untenable."  (§4.2.2)

## Defeater proposals and Korb's objection (§4.2.2)

> "Several authors (Pollock (1995), Ryan (1996), Douven (2002)) attempt to revise the strong Lockean thesis by placing restrictions on when a high degree of belief warrants full belief."  (§4.2.2)

> "For example, Douven (2002) says that it is sufficient except when the proposition is a member of a probabilistically self-undermining set."  (§4.2.2)

> "All proposals of this kind are vitiated by the following sort of example due to Korb (1992)."  (§4.2.2)

> "Therefore, these proposals prohibit full belief in any proposition with degree of belief short of certainty. Douven and Williamson (2006) generalize this sort of example to trivialize an entire class of similar formal proposals."  (§4.2.2)

## Statistical evidence (§4.2.2)

> "Buchak (2014) argues that what partial beliefs count as full beliefs cannot merely be a matter of the degree of partial belief, but must also depend on the type of evidence it is based on."  (§4.2.2)

> "You have only statistical evidence in the first scenario, whereas in the second, a causal chain of events connects your belief to the accident (see also Thomson (1986), Nelkin (2000) and Schauer (2003))."  (§4.2.2; the scenarios are two blue-bus cases, 90% base rate vs 90%-reliable eyewitness)

## The stability (Humean) thesis (§4.2.3)

> "For many, sacrificing deductive cogency is simply too high a price to pay for a bridge principle, even one so simple and intuitive as the strong Lockean thesis."  (§4.2.3)

> "One proposal, due to Leitgeb (2013, 2014, 2017) and Arló-Costa (2012), holds that rational full belief corresponds to a stably high degree of belief, i.e. a degree of belief that remains high even after conditioning on new information."  (§4.2.3)

> "No matter how many tickets are in the lottery, a Humean agent cannot believe any ticket will lose."  (§4.2.3)

> "In this Lottery situation the agent cannot fully believe any non-trivial proposition. This example also shows how sensitive the Humean proposal is to the fine-graining of possibilities."  (§4.2.3)

> "If Buchak (2014) is right, no agent should have beliefs in lottery propositions–these beliefs would necessarily be formed on the basis of purely statistical evidence."  (§4.2.3)

## Epistemic decision theory (§4.2.5)

> "Many proposals, such as Easwaran (2015), Dorst (2017), are equivalent to a version of the Lockean thesis, where the threshold is determined by the utility the agent assigns to true and false beliefs. Since these are essentially Lockean proposals, they are subject to Lottery-style paradoxes."  (§4.2.5)

> "However, Levi takes pains to make sure that the result of this operation is deductively cogent and therefore avoids Lottery-type paradoxes."  (§4.2.5)

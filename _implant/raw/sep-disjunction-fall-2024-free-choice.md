# SEP "Disjunction" (Aloni) — the paradox of free choice permission and its proposed solutions

Source: Maria Aloni, "Disjunction", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published 2016-03-23. §2 (end, on addition and Ross's paradox), §6
  "Modal accounts of disjunction and free choice", and the bibliography.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/disjunction/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read with curl;
  SEP's LaTeX rendered as Unicode only where a formula is described outside
  the quotations; the quotations below contain no formulas)

> "Sentences of the form “You may A or B” are normally understood as implying “You may A and you may B”."  (§6)

> "The following, however, is not a valid principle in standard deontic logic, e.g., von Wright (1968)."  (§6; the principle that follows, labelled (36) "Free Choice Principle", is P(α ∨ β) → Pα)

> "As Kamp (1973) pointed out, plainly making the Free Choice principle valid, for example by adding it as an axiom, would not do because it would allow us to derive P q from P p as shown in (37), which is clearly unacceptable:"  (§6; (37) is the derivation 1. Pp [assumption], 2. P(p ∨ q) [from 1, by principle (38)], 3. Pq [from 2, by free choice principle])

> "The step leading to 2 in (37) uses the following principle which holds in standard deontic logic:"  (§6; (38) is Pα → P(α ∨ β))

> "Intuitively, however, (38) seems invalid (You may go to the beach doesn’t seem to imply You may go to the beach or the cinema), while (36) seems to hold, in direct opposition to the principles of deontic logic."  (§6)

> "Von Wright (1968) labeled this the paradox of free choice permissions."  (§6)

> "Similar paradoxes arise also for imperatives (see Ross’ paradox, introduced in section 2), epistemic modals (Zimmermann 2000), and other modal constructions."  (§6)

> "Many have argued that what we called the Free Choice Principle is merely a pragmatic inference and therefore the step leading to 3 in (37) is unjustified."  (§6)

> "One argument in favor of such a pragmatic account comes from the observation that free choice effects disappear in negative contexts."  (§6)

> "For example, No one is allowed to eat the cake or the ice-cream cannot merely mean that no one is allowed to eat the cake and the ice-cream, as would be expected if free choice effects were semantic entailments rather than pragmatic implicatures (Alonso-Ovalle 2006)."  (§6)

> "Various ways of deriving free choice inferences as implicatures have been proposed (e.g., Gazdar 1979; Kratzer and Shimoyama 2002; Schulz 2005; Fox 2007 and Franke 2011; see however Fusco 2014 for a critical discussion of pragmatic accounts to free choice)."  (§6)

> "Others have proposed modal systems where the step leading to 3 in (37) is justified while the step leading to 2 is no longer valid, e.g., Aloni 2007, which proposes a uniform account of free choice effects of disjunctions and indefinites under both modals and imperatives."  (§6)

> "Simons (2005) and Barker (2010) also proposed semantic accounts of free choice inferences, the latter crucially employing an analysis of or in terms of linear logic additive disjunction combined with a representation of strong permission using the deontic reduction strategy (as in Lokhorst 2006)."  (§6)

> "Zimmermann (2000), by contrast, proposes a modal analysis of linguistic disjunction which identifies the semantic contribution of or with precisely these epistemic effects (see also Geurts 2005 for a further development of this idea)."  (§6, opening)

> "On Zimmermann’s account linguistic disjunctions should be analyzed as conjunctive lists of epistemic possibilities:"  (§6; (34) is "S1 or…or Sn ↦ ◇S1 ∧ … ∧ ◇Sn", ◇ an epistemic possibility operator)

> "Grice, as we just saw, argued against a semantic account of such effects, which he labeled as the non truth-functional ground of disjunction (Grice 1989)."  (§6, opening)

> "Finally Zimmermann (2000) distinguishes between (36), which, according to him, is an unjustified logical principle, from the following intuitively valid principle:"  (§6; (39) is "X may A or may B ⊨ X may A and X may B")

> "Zimmermann, however, actually derives only the weaker principle in (41) (under certain assumptions including his Authority principle)."  (§6; (40) is ◇PA ∧ ◇PB ⊨ PA ∧ PB, (41) is ◇PA ∧ ◇PB ⊨ □PA ∧ □PB, with □ read "it is certain that")

> "Although Kratzer and Shimoyama, Alonso-Ovalle, Aloni, Simons and Zimmermann differ in their solution of the free choice paradox, they all agree in endorsing an “alternative-based” analysis of or according to which a disjunctive sentence “A or B” contributes the set of propositional alternatives {A, B}."  (§6)

> "We can think of disjunction as a means of entertaining different alternatives."  (§6, opening sentence)

> "Alonso-Ovalle (2006), for example, uses this alternative set in his pragmatic derivation of the free choice implicatures."  (§6)

> "Thus, You may go to the beach or to the cinema is true only if You may go to the beach and You may go to the cinema are both true."  (§6, on Aloni 2007)

> "The most natural interpretation of disjunctive imperatives is as one presenting a choice between different actions:"  (§2, on Ross's paradox, imperatives (17) "Post this letter!" and (18) "Post this letter or burn it!")

> "Imperative (17) then cannot imply (18) otherwise when told the former one would be justified in burning the letter rather than posting it (e.g., Mastop 2005; Aloni 2007; Aloni and Ciardelli 2013)."  (§2)

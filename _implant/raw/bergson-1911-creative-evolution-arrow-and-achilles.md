# Bergson 1911 — Creative Evolution ch. IV: the arrow and Achilles as indivisible movement

Source: Henri Bergson, Creative Evolution, authorized translation by Arthur
  Mitchell, New York: Henry Holt and Company, 1911, ch. IV ("The
  Cinematographical Mechanism of Thought and the Mechanistic Illusion"),
  pp. 308–313 per the book's index ("Arrow of Zeno, 308-13"). Project
  Gutenberg eBook #26163 (page numbers not marked in the e-text).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/26163 (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (read in the Gutenberg plain text)

> "attempt to reconstitute change out of states implies the absurd proposition, that movement is made of immobilities."  (ch. IV, p. 308 per index range)

> "The arguments of Zeno of Elea, although formulated with a very different intention, have no other meaning."  (ch. IV)

> "But the arrow never is in any point of its course. The most we can say is that it might be there, in this sense, that it passes there and might stop there."  (ch. IV; "is" italicised in the original)

> "The truth is that if the arrow leaves the point A to fall down at the point B, its movement AB is as simple, as indecomposable, in so far as it is movement, as the tension of the bow that shoots it."  (ch. IV)

> "When Achilles pursues the tortoise, each of his steps must be treated as indivisible, and so must each step of the tortoise."  (ch. IV)

> "That is, we do not consider the sophism of Zeno refuted by the fact that the geometrical progression a(1 + 1/n + 1/n2 + 1/n3 +,... etc.)--in which a designates the initial distance between Achilles and the tortoise, and n the relation of their respective velocities--has a finite sum if n is greater than 1."  (ch. IV, footnote 99; exponents flattened in the e-text)

> "The movement considered by Zeno would only be the equivalent of the movement of Achilles if we could treat the movement as we treat the interval passed through, decomposable and recomposable at will."  (ch. IV)

Relied on for: Bergson's own response (including his footnote that the
finite sum of the geometric series does not answer Zeno): motion is an indivisible act that
is not composed of positions — the "temporal becoming" response the SEP
attributes to Bergson (1911).
Context: The SEP (Huggett §3.3) quotes Bergson as "‘movement is composed
of immobilities’ (1911, 308)"; the Mitchell translation read here has
"movement is made of immobilities". Bergson says of the other arguments "We have examined them elsewhere"
(the place is not named in the lines read). Footnote 99 cites F. Evellin,
Infini et quantité (1880), pp. 63–97, as arguments he regards "as
conclusive".

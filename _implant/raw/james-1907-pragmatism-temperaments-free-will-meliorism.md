# James 1907 — Pragmatism: tender- and tough-minded temperaments; free-will "pragmatically" and meliorism

Source: William James, Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking
  (New York: Longmans, Green & Co., 1907), Lecture I "The Present Dilemma
  in Philosophy" and Lecture III "Some Metaphysical Problems Pragmatically
  Considered". The Gutenberg text has no page markers; locators are lectures.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5116 (Project Gutenberg eBook
  #5116; public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (read in https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt;
  quotations cut by script, whitespace normalised; capitals as in the source)

> "THE TENDER-MINDED Rationalistic (going by 'principles'), Intellectualistic, Idealistic, Optimistic, Religious, Free-willist, Monistic, Dogmatical. THE TOUGH-MINDED Empiricist (going by 'facts'), Sensationalistic, Materialistic, Pessimistic, Irreligious, Fatalistic, Pluralistic, Sceptical."  (Lecture I; two columns in the source)

> "Tender-minded and tough-minded, what a barbaric disjunction!"  (Lecture I)

> "Free-will pragmatically means NOVELTIES IN THE WORLD, the right to expect that in its deepest elements as well as in its surface phenomena, the future may not identically repeat and imitate the past."  (Lecture III)

> "may naturally welcome free-will as a MELIORISTIC doctrine."  (Lecture III)

Relied on for: the two temperaments (the list Jung discusses in Psychological
Types, ch. VIII); James's pragmatic reading of free will as novelty and as a
"melioristic" doctrine.
Context: The "barbaric disjunction" sentence is James voicing the objection
professional philosophers in his audience might make to his own two-column
scheme, before he proceeds with it. In Lecture III the free-will passage
follows his claim that the "real ground for supposing free-will is indeed
pragmatic" and not the "right to punish"; the "melioristic" clause is said
of persons whose knowledge of the past "has bred pessimism".

> "There are no objective values. This is a bald statement of the thesis of this chapter"  (§1, p. 15, opening sentences)

> "'error theory', a theory that although most people in making moral judgements implicitly claim, among other things, to be pointing to something objectively prescriptive, these claims are all false."  (§7, p. 35)

> "It is this that makes the name 'moral scepticism' appropriate."  (§7, p. 35)

> "Traditionally it has been supported by arguments of two main kinds, which I shall call the argument from relativity and the argument from queerness"  (§7, p. 35)

> "The argument from relativity has as its prerniss the well-known variation in moral codes from one society to another and from one period to another"  (§8, p. 36)

> "the argument from relativity has some force simply because the actual variations in the moral codes are more readily explained by the hypothesis that they reflect ways of life than by the hypothesis that they express perceptions, most of them seriously inadequate and badly distorted, of objective values."  (§8, p. 37)

> "This has two parts, one metaphysical, the other epistemological. If there were objective values, then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe."  (§9, p. 38)

> "such principles as provide the foundations of what Sidgwick has called"  (§8, p. 37; the scan continues "Merent methods of ethics", OCR for "different")

> "what Hare calls 'ideals' or, less kindly. 'fanaticism'."  (§8, p. 37; punctuation as extracted from the scan)

> "Correspondingly, if we were aware of them, it would have to be by some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else."  (§9, p. 38)

> "These points were recognized by Moore when he spoke of non-natural qualities"  (§9, p. 38)

> "Indeed, the best move for the moral objectivist is not to evade this issue, but to look for companions in guilt."  (§9, p. 39; Mackie then takes Richard Price as the example)

> "The Form of the Good is such that knowledge of it provides the knower with both a direction and an overriding motive"  (§9, p. 40, on Plato)

> "just because the end has to-be-pursuedness somehow built into it."  (§9, p. 40)

Relied on for: the thesis and its name (error theory, moral scepticism);
the two arguments and their premisses in Mackie's words; Mackie's naming
of Moore, Plato, Price ("companions in guilt") in §9.
Context: §1 opens the book's first chapter; §§2–6 distinguish first- and
second-order questions; §7 argues that ordinary moral concepts claim
objectivity, so that the denial "will have to be put forward not as the
result of an analytic approach" but as an error theory; §8 grants a
"well-known counter" (general principles rather than specific codes)
and answers it; §9 adds that the argument from queerness is "Even more
important, however, and certainly more generally applicable"; §§10–12
(not excerpted) give further arguments (patterns of objectification).

## Rawson, "Meno"

> "Socrates replies by reformulating that objection as a paradoxical dilemma, then arguing that the dilemma is based on a false dichotomy."  (§1c)

> "Socrates tries to expose the false dichotomy by identifying states of cognition between complete knowledge and pure ignorance."  (§1c)

> "(Forgotten-but-capable-of-being-remembered is a state of cognition between complete knowledge and pure ignorance.)"  (§1c)

> "(Implicit true belief is another state of cognition between complete knowledge and pure ignorance.)"  (§1c)

> "Is Meno here honestly identifying a practical difficulty with this particular kind of inquiry, where the participants now seem not to know even what they are looking for? Or is he just throwing up an abstract, defensive obstacle, so that he does not have to keep trying? Socrates interprets Meno’s objection in the obstructionist way, and reformulates it as a paradoxical theoretical dilemma"  (§2b)

> "This reformulation of Meno’s objection has come to be known as “Meno’s Paradox.”"  (§2b)

> "Here, Socrates clearly asks “leading questions,” and eventually even shows the slave the answer in the form of a question (84e)."  (§2b)

> "That is enough to refute Meno’s Paradox, which inferred the impossibility of learning from a false dichotomy between complete knowledge and pure ignorance."  (§2b)

> "But the geometry lesson with the slave clearly does not demonstrate the reminding of something that was learned in a previous life."  (§2b)

> "This time he concludes not that the slave has remembered some geometrical knowledge from what his mind had learned from experiences in previous lives, but instead that the slave has discovered the relevant true beliefs in his mind, which is somehow “always in a state of having learned” (86a)."  (§2b)

> "This paradoxical phrasing turns the initial statement of the theory of recollection, which stretched a common-sense notion of learning from experience over a number of successive lifetimes, into the beginnings of a theory of innate ideas, because the geometrical beliefs or concepts somehow belong to the mind at all times."  (§2b, on 86a)

> "Later in the conversation, Socrates even seems to identify “recollection” with this latter part of the process (98a)."  (§2b)

> "Accordingly, many scholars believe that the Meno was written between those groups of dialogues, and probably about 385 B.C.E."  (§3)

Context: §2b calls the theory of recollection "an early example of what
would later be called a theory of innate ideas", notes that the priests
and poets cited at 81a are "just the kind of people Socrates later
criticizes" (99c–d), and says Socrates infers both innate knowledge (85d)
and innate beliefs (85c, 86a) while the dialogue ends with the agreement
that "men have neither of these by nature" (98c–d). The entry lists Scott
2006, Plato's Meno (Cambridge) and Nehamas 1992, “Meno’s Paradox and
Socrates as a Teacher” among its references.

## O'Keefe, "Epicurus"

> "Epicurus thinks that, in order to make judgments about the world, or even to start any inquiry whatsoever, we must already be in possession of certain basic concepts, which stand in need of no further proof or definition, on pain of entering into an infinite regress. This concern is similar to the Paradox of Inquiry explored by Plato in the Meno, that one must already know about something in order to be able to inquire about it. However, instead of postulating that our immaterial souls had acquaintance with transcendent Forms in a pre-natal existence, as Plato does, Epicurus thinks that we have certain ‘preconceptions’–concepts such as ‘body,’ ‘person,’ ‘usefulness,’ and ‘truth’–which are formed in our (material) minds as the result of repeated sense-experiences of similar objects."  (§4a)

Context: O'Keefe lists "three criteria of truth: sensations,
‘preconceptions,’ and feelings", and notes that Epicurus' Canon "is lost,
so many of the details of his views are unavailable to us". The Greek
term (prolēpsis) is not printed in this paragraph; the SEP "Epicurus"
entry's bibliography lists Long 1971 "Aesthesis, Prolepsis and Linguistic
Theory in Epicurus" and Tsouna 2016 "Epicurean Preconceptions" (titles
read, works not fetched).

Relied on for: Rawson's reading (false dichotomy; intermediate cognitive
states; leading questions at 84e; recollection restated at 86a and 98a);
O'Keefe's report of Epicurean preconceptions as a response comparable to
the paradox of inquiry.

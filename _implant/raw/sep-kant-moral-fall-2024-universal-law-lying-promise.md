# SEP Kant's Moral Philosophy (Fall 2024) — universal law formula and the lying promise

Source: Robert Johnson and Adam Cureton, "Kant's Moral Philosophy", *Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy* (Fall 2024 edition), ed. Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman; first published
  2004-02-23, substantive revision 2022-01-21 (copyright line: "Copyright © 2022 by Robert
  Johnson, Adam Cureton"), §§3, 5.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/kant-moral/ (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched and read)

> "“I ought never to act except in such a way that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law” (G 4:402)."  (§3)

> "Kant’s first formulation of the CI states that you are to “act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law” (G 4:421)."  (§5)

> "O’Neill (1975, 1989) and Rawls (1980, 1989), among others, take this formulation in effect to summarize a decision procedure for moral reasoning"  (§5)

> "If your maxim fails the third step, you have a “perfect” duty admitting “of no exception in favor of inclination” to refrain from acting on that maxim (G 4:421)."  (§5)

> "Again, Kant’s interpreters differ over exactly how to reconstruct the derivation of these duties."  (§5)

> "The maxim of lying whenever it gets you what you want generates a contradiction once you try to combine it with the universalized version that all rational agents must, by a law of nature, lie when doing so gets them what they want."  (§5)

> "Hence, it is inconceivable that I could sincerely act on my maxim in a world in which my maxim is a universal law of nature."  (§5)

Relied on for: the Akademie locators of the universal-law formulas; the four-step decision
procedure reading and its owners; the lying-promise reconstruction as one reconstruction among
others (the authors' own caveat).
Context: the entry does not discuss the 1797 essay or the murderer case (a search of the

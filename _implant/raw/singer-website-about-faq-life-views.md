# Singer (author website) — About Me and FAQ: life dates, positions, the 1979 "defective infant" sentence, the move from preference to hedonistic utilitarianism

> "Journalists have bestowed on me the tag of “world’s most influential living philosopher.”"  (About Me)

> "I am also known for my controversial critique of the sanctity of life ethics in bioethics."  (About Me)

> "My wife, Renata, and I stopped eating meat in 1971 because we recognized that there is no ethical justification for treating animals as if their interests didn’t count."  (About Me)

> "That realization led me to write Animal Liberation, first published in 1975."  (About Me)

> "I am the founder, and currently Chair of the Board, of The Life You Can Save, an organization based on my book of the same name."  (About Me)

> "My writings about giving include the 1972 essay “ Famine, Affluence, and Morality ”, in which I argue for donating to help the global poor, and two books that make the case for effective giving, The Life You Can Save (2009, 2nd edition 2019) and The Most Good You Can Do (2015)."  (About Me)

> "I was born in Melbourne, Australia, in 1946 and educated at the University of Melbourne and the University of Oxford."  (About Me)

> "For 25 years, I was the Ira W. DeCamp Professor of Bioethics at the University Center for Human Values at Princeton University."  (About Me)

> "I retired from Princeton in 2024 and returned to live in Melbourne,"  (About Me)

> "In 1970, when I was a graduate student at Oxford, a chance lunch with Richard Keshen, a Canadian graduate student and a vegetarian, introduced me to concerns about factory farming."  (The ethics of our treatment of animals)

> "Eventually I wrote Animal Liberation, which has been described as the “bible” of the animal rights movement. It was published in 1975 and has never been out of print."  (The ethics of our treatment of animals)

> "I argued in the opening chapter of Animal Liberation that humans and animals are equal in the sense that the fact that a being is human does not mean that we should give the interests of that being preference over the similar interests of other beings."  (FAQ)

> "That would be speciesism, and wrong for the same reasons that racism and sexism are wrong."  (FAQ)

> "I did write that, in the 1979 edition of Practical Ethics. Today the term “defective infant” is considered offensive, and I no longer use it, but it was standard usage then."  (FAQ)

> "I use the term "person" to refer to a being who is capable of anticipating the future, of having wants and desires for the future."  (FAQ)

> "So killing a newborn baby is never equivalent to killing a person, that is, a being who wants to go on living."  (FAQ)

> "I believe it should be possible to carry out that decision, not only by withholding or withdrawing life-support — which can lead to the baby dying slowly from dehydration or from an infection — but also by taking active steps to end the baby’s life swiftly and humanely."  (FAQ)

> "Hence even if the parents do not want their own child, it would be wrong to kill it."  (FAQ)

> "I support law reform to allow people to decide to end their lives, if they are terminally or incurably ill."  (FAQ)

> "the most elaborated philosophical elaboration of my views is Practical Ethics, now in its 3rd edition (2011)."  (FAQ)

> "My views are not fixed. Many good philosophers disagree with me, and all philosophers should be open to changing their views on the basis of new arguments and objections."  (FAQ)

> "I am currently reconsidering some of the answers given above, especially those in which I regard the wrongness of killing as significantly affected by the capacity of those killed to see themselves as existing over time. This is a view that derived from my earlier acceptance of preference utilitarianism, but it does not fit well with hedonistic utilitarianism, which I am now more inclined to favour."  (FAQ)

> "I have written, co-authored, edited or co-edited more than 50 books, including Practical Ethics, The Expanding Circle, Rethinking Life and Death, One World, The Ethics of What We Eat (with Jim Mason), and The Point of View of the Universe (with Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek)."  (About Me)

## Book II — Eubulides

> "To the school of Euclides belongs Eubulides of Miletus, the author of many dialectical arguments in an interrogatory form, namely, The Liar, The Disguised, Electra, The Veiled Figure, The Sorites, The Horned One, and The Bald Head."  (II.108)

> "Eubulides the Eristic, who propounded his quibbles about horns and confounded the orators with falsely pretentious arguments, is gone with all the braggadocio of a Demosthenes."  (II.108, verse "said by one of the Comic poets"; Hicks's note: Meineke, C.G.F. iv. 618)

> "Demosthenes was probably his pupil and thereby improved his faulty pronunciation of the letter R."  (II.108)

> "Eubulides kept up a controversy with Aristotle and said much to discredit him."  (II.109)

> "Among other members the school of Eubulides included Alexinus of Elis, a man very fond of controversy, for which reason he was called Elenxinus."  (II.109)

> "To the school of Eubulides also belonged Euphantus of Olynthus, who wrote a history of his own times."  (II.110)

> "There are also other pupils of Eubulides, amongst them Apollonius surnamed Cronus. He had a pupil Diodorus, the son of Ameinias of Iasus, who was also nicknamed Cronus."  (II.111)

> "He too was a dialectician and was supposed to have been the first who discovered the arguments known as the "Veiled Figure" and the "Horned One.""  (II.111, of Diodorus Cronus)

## Book VII — the same arguments among the Stoics

> "the fallacies known as the Veiled, or Horned, No man, and The Mowers."  (VII.44, in the Stoic division of dialectic)

> "There are also certain insoluble arguments: the Veiled Men, the Concealed, Sorites, Horned Folk, the Nobodies."  (VII.82)

> "It cannot be that if two is few, three is not so likewise, nor that if two or three are few, four is not so; and so on up to ten. But two is few, therefore so also is ten."  (VII.82, the example given; Hicks's note: "Here Laertius, as the text stands, gives examples of the Sorites and the Nobody, but none of the other three fallacies, the Veiled, Concealed, Horned.")

> "If you never lost something, you have it still; but you never lost horns, ergo you have horns." Others attribute this to Eubulides."  (VII.187, among arguments Chrysippus "used to propound")

> "Of the Fallacy of “the Veiled Person,” addressed to Aristobulus, two books."  (VII.198, list of Chrysippus's works)

Relied on for: the list of seven arguments ascribed to Eubulides (II.108);
his school, the Demosthenes and Aristotle reports, his pupils (II.108–111);
the competing ascription of the Veiled and Horned arguments to Diodorus
(II.111) and of the horns argument to Chrysippus (VII.187); the Stoic
treatment of the same arguments (VII.44, 82, 198).
Context: II.106–112 is Diogenes' account of Euclides of Megara and his
successors; II.108 opens with Euclides' six dialogues. II.109–110 tells of
Alexinus' death; II.111–112 of Diodorus being unable to solve Stilpo's
questions before Ptolemy Soter. VII.187 comes from the life of Chrysippus;
the sentence "Others attribute this to Eubulides" follows the horns example
only. No book of Diogenes gives the wording of the Liar, Electra, Veiled or
Bald Head arguments.

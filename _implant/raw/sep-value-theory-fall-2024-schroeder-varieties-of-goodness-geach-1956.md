> "The word “value” doesn’t appear anywhere on this list; it is full, however, of “good”, “better”, and “best”, and correspondingly of “bad”, “worse”, and “worst”."  (§1)

The four exemplar sentences, as listed in §1: "Pleasure is good." /
"It is good that you came." / "It is good for him to talk to her." /
"That is a good knife."

> "Sentences like 2 make claims about what I’ll (again stipulatively) call goodness simpliciter; this is the kind of goodness appealed to by traditional utilitarianism."  (§1)

> "And sentences like 4 are what, following Geach [1956], I’ll call attributive uses of “good”, because “good” functions as a predicate modifier, rather than as a predicate in its own right."  (§1)

> "Claims about good simpliciter are those which have garnered the most attention in moral philosophy."  (§1.1)

> "For example, Philippa Foot [1985] gives an important but compressed argument that apparent talk about what is good simpliciter can be made sense of as elliptical talk about what is good for some unmentioned person"  (§1.1.1)

> "G.E. Moore [1903], in contrast, struggled to make sense of good-for claims."  (§1.1.1)

> "Geach and Foot both argue that it does not, on the ground that states of affairs are too thin of a kind to support attributive good claims"  (§1.1.2; "it" = good simpliciter making sense as a good state of affairs)

> "But it has been held by many moral philosophers that an inventory of what is better than what would still leave something interesting and important out: what is good."  (§1.2.3)

# Silverstein 2000 — "In Defense of Happiness": a hedonist response to the experience machine

Source: Matthew Silverstein, "In Defense of Happiness: A Response to the
  Experience Machine", *Social Theory and Practice* 26(2), 2000,
  pp. 279–300.

> "Nowadays, most philosophers entertain the idea of hedonism just long enough to dismiss it."  (Introduction)

> "Many of the most prominent philosophers of value--including James Griffin, David Brink, Stephen Darwall, and L.W. Sumner--take this thought experiment to be the definitive response to hedonism and, more broadly, to all mental state theories of wellbeing."  (Introduction)

> "I will take a close look at the thought experiment and the ways Nozick and others use it, and I will attempt to demonstrate that it does not constitute a refutation of mental state accounts of prudential value."  (Introduction)

> "Even without these doubts, though, most of us continue to share Nozick's intuitions: we remain unwilling to accept a lifetime on the experience machine."  (Nozick's Experience Machine)

> "Nozick's argument does succeed, then, in isolating the fact that we care about more than our experiences, It then concludes that there is more to well-being than how our lives feel to us from the inside."  (Nozick's Experience Machine; comma as in the source)

> "But it is unclear how one arrives at this claim merely from the fact that we care about more than happiness."  (Nozick's Experience Machine)

> "We develop a desire to track reality because, in almost all cases, the connection to reality is conducive to happiness."  (The Machine Breaks Down)

> "Thus, all of the evidence to which the experience machine argument appeals--all of our desires, including our desire to avoid the machine itself--ultimately points towards happiness as the source of prudential value. But that is precisely the doctrine of hedonism."  (The Machine Breaks Down)

> "Our intuitions and desires have been framed in response to a world that is substantively different from the world of the experience machine."  (The Machine Breaks Down)

Nozick 1989, as quoted by Silverstein (note 16: *The Examined Life*, pp. 104–5):

> "You can live your fondest dreams "from the inside."... Upon entering, you will not remember having done this; so no pleasures will get mined by realizing they are machine-produced."  (Nozick's Experience Machine; "mined" as in this text version, "ruined" in Buscicchi's quotation)

> "And so, Nozick concludes, "there is more to life than feeling happy.""  (Nozick's Experience Machine; note 18: The Examined Life, p. 106)

Relied on for: Silverstein's reading of the machine's reception, his
  concession that the machine isolates what we care about, his
  wants-vs-well-being gap, and his desire-conditioning argument that the
  intuitions point to hedonism — reported as his.
Context: Silverstein uses "happiness" for an experientialist state; he
  draws on Railton, Brandt and Hare; Crisp 2006 (p. 637, note 59) points
  to Silverstein pp. 293–6 as "a nicely stated modern version" of the
  evolutionary argument.

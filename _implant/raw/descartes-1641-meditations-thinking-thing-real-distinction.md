# Descartes 1641 — Meditations II and VI: "a thinking thing" and the real distinction of mind and body

Source: René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), Meditation II
  ("Of the Nature of the Human Mind; and that it is more easily known than
  the Body") and Meditation VI ("Of the Existence of Material Things, and of
  the Real Distinction between the Mind and Body of Man"). Translated by
  John Veitch (1853; public-domain translation), paragraph numbering as in
  the Wikisource transcription of the 1901 reprint.
Original: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Meditations_on_First_Philosophy
  (Veitch tr., public domain). Canonical locators: Meditation II, AT VII
  27–28; Meditation VI, AT VII 78 (the Wikisource copy does not carry AT
  page numbers; the AT references are the standard ones for these passages
  and were not verified against an AT edition).
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (read at
  https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Meditations_on_First_Philosophy/Meditation_II
  and .../Meditation_VI, raw wikitext)

> "Thinking is another attribute of the soul; and here I discover what
>  properly belongs to myself. This alone is inseparable from me. I am—I
>  exist: this is certain; but how often? As often as I think; for perhaps
>  it would even happen, if I should wholly cease to think, that I should at
>  the same time altogether cease to be. I now admit nothing that is not
>  necessarily true. I am therefore, precisely speaking, only a thinking
>  thing, that is, a mind (mens sive animus), understanding, or reason,
>  terms whose signification was before unknown to me."
>  (Meditation II, ¶6; Veitch tr.)

> "But what, then, am I ? A thinking thing, it has been said. But what is a
>  thinking thing? It is a thing that doubts, understands, [conceives],
>  affirms, denies, wills, refuses; that imagines also, and perceives."
>  (Meditation II, ¶8; Veitch tr.)

> "because, on the one hand, I have a clear and distinct idea of myself, in
>  as far as I am only a thinking and unextended thing, and as, on the other
>  hand, I possess a distinct idea of body, in as far as it is only an
>  extended and unthinking thing, it is certain that I, [that is, my mind,
>  by which I am what I am], is entirely and truly distinct from my body,
>  and may exist without it."  (Meditation VI, ¶9; Veitch tr.)

Relied on for: the claims that Descartes identifies the self with a
thinking thing whose existence is certain "as often as I think", and that
Meditation VI argues from clear and distinct conception of mind without
body to their being "entirely and truly distinct" — the so-called real
distinction, the reference point for later "Cartesian dualism".
Context: the Meditation II passages follow the doubt about the body and the
malignant deceiver; Descartes lists nutrition, walking and perception as
attributes that depend on the body before isolating thought. The Meditation
VI passage opens with the premise that whatever is clearly and distinctly
conceived apart can be made to exist apart "by the omnipotence of God".
Square brackets are Veitch's, marking phrases from the French edition.

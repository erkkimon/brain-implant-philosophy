# Ryle 1949 — The Concept of Mind, ch. II §3 "Knowing How and Knowing That": performances whose rules are unformulated

Source: Gilbert Ryle, The Concept of Mind (London: Hutchinson's University
  Library, 1949; the scan read is a 1951 reprint), chapter II "Knowing How
  and Knowing That", §3 "Knowing How and Knowing That", pp. 29–30 (running
  heads and page numbers visible in the OCR).
Original: https://archive.org/details/gilbert-ryle-concept-of-mind
  (Internet Archive scan; copyrighted; excerpts only). The same scan is
  used by `ryle-1949-concept-of-mind-ghost-in-the-machine.md`.
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (read in the archive.org OCR text
  "Gilbert Ryle, Concept of Mind_djvu.txt"; OCR slips in the quoted lines
  corrected and listed below)

> "Champions of this legend are apt to try to reassimilate knowing how to knowing that by arguing that intelligent performance involves the observance of rules, or the application of criteria."  (p. 29)

> "I shall argue that the intellectualist legend is false and that when we describe a performance as intelligent, this does not entail the double operation of considering and executing."  (pp. 29–30)

> "First, there are many classes of performances in which intelligence is displayed, but the rules or criteria of which are unformulated."  (p. 30)

> "He knows how to make good jokes and how to detect bad ones, but he cannot tell us or himself any recipes for them."  (p. 30; said of "The wit, when challenged to cite the maxims, or canons, by which he constructs and appreciates jokes")

> "Efficient practice precedes the theory of it; methodologies presuppose the application of the methods, of the critical investigation of which they are the products."  (p. 30)

> "The crucial objection to the intellectualist legend is this. The consideration of propositions is itself an operation the execution of which can be more or less intelligent, less or more stupid."  (p. 30)

OCR corrections made in the lines above: "gre" → "are", "whiclr" →
"which", "‘entail" → "entail", "pratticd" → "practice". Nothing else
changed.

Relied on for: Ryle's statement that some intelligent performances follow
  rules nobody has formulated; the wit who cannot tell his recipes;
  practice preceding theory; the regress objection's first sentences.
Context: The section argues against the "intellectualist legend" that
  intelligent action is always preceded by considering propositions. The
  sentence after the regress lines, quoted by Pavese (SEP "Knowledge How",
  §1) as Ryle 1949: 19 in the edition she uses, reads in this scan: "But if,
  for any operation to be intelligently executed, a prior theoretical
  operation had first to be performed and performed intelligently, it
  would be a logical impossibility for anyone ever to break into the
  circle." Ryle does not mention Polanyi here; the link between the two is
  made by later writers, not by this text.

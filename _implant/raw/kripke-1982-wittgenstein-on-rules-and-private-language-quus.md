# Kripke 1982 — Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language: the paradox, quus, the sceptical solution

  Elementary Exposition (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1982),

> "So the present paper should be thought of as expounding neither ‘Wittgenstein’s’ argument nor ‘Kripke’s’: rather Wittgenstein’s argument as it struck Kripke, as it presented a problem for him."  (p. 5)

> "In §201 Wittgenstein says, “this was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule.”"  (p. 7)

> "The ‘paradox’ is perhaps the central problem of Philosophical Investigations."  (p. 7)

> "It may be regarded as a new form of philosophical scepticism."  (p. 7)

> "perhaps in the past I used ‘plus’ and ‘+’ to denote a function which I will call ‘quus’"  (pp. 8–9; the defining formula that follows is garbled in the OCR and is not quoted here — the SEP entry "Rule-Following and Intentionality" (Miller & Sultanescu, Fall 2024, §2) summarises it: same result as addition below 57, and 5 otherwise)

> "This, then, is the sceptical paradox. When I respond in one way rather than another to such a problem as ‘68+57’, I can have no justification for one response rather than another. Since the sceptic who supposes that I meant quus cannot be answered, there is no fact about me that distinguishes between my meaning plus and my meaning quus."  (p. 21)

> "Indeed, there is no fact about me that distinguishes between my meaning a definite function by ‘plus’ (which determines my responses in new cases) and my meaning nothing at all."  (p. 21)

> "It seems that the entire idea of meaning vanishes into thin air."  (p. 22)

> "should not the sceptical problem be obvious to any reader of Goodman?"  (p. 20; with a note to Goodman, Fact, Fiction, and Forecast, ch. III §4, and "grue")

> "The relation of meaning and intention to future action is normative, not descriptive."  (p. 37)

> "Personally I am inclined to regard it as the most radical and original sceptical problem that philosophy has seen to date, one that only a highly unusual cast of mind could have produced."  (p. 60)

> "Of course he does not wish to leave us with his problem, but to solve it: the sceptical conclusion is insane and intolerable."  (p. 60)

> "Call a proposed solution to a sceptical philosophical problem a straight solution if it shows that on closer examination the scepticism proves to be unwarranted; an elusive or complex argument proves the thesis the sceptic doubted."  (p. 66)

> "A sceptical solution of a sceptical philosophical problem begins on the contrary by conceding that the sceptic’s negative assertions are unanswerable."  (p. 66)

> "The impossibility of private language emerges as a corollary of his sceptical solution of his own paradox, as does the impossibility of ‘private causation’ in Hume."  (p. 68)

> "we can say that Wittgenstein proposes a picture of language based, not on truth conditions, but on assertability conditions or justification conditions"  (p. 74)

> "Our community can assert of any individual that he follows a rule if he passes the tests for rule following applied to any member of the community."  (p. 110)

Relied on for: Kripke's statement of the paradox from PI §201; the quus
  function and the 68+57 case; the conclusion that no fact distinguishes
  meaning plus from meaning quus; straight vs. sceptical solutions;
  assertability conditions and the community; the Goodman parallel; his
  own assessment of the problem (p. 60); his disclaimer about exegesis (p. 5).
Context: chapter 2 ("The Wittgensteinian Paradox") develops the sceptic's
  challenge against candidate facts (dispositions, machines, simplicity,
  qualia, images, primitive states, Fregean senses); chapter 3 ("The
  Solution and the ‘Private Language’ Argument") gives the sceptical
  solution modelled on Hume's "Sceptical Solution of These Doubts".

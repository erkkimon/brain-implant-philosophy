# Ross 1941 — "Imperatives and Logic": the letter-box inference

> "The second possibility ot solution is no actual solution, but an evasion of the problem."  (§10, p. 61; OCR "ot" kept)

> "The logical element refers solely to the fulfilment of the demand, or rather to the indicative sentences expressing the theme of demand as real, or the demand as fulfilled."  (§10, p. 61)

> "The immediate feeling of evidence does not refer to the satisfaction of the imperative, but rather to something like the »validity» or the »existence» of the imperative, no matter how those expressions are to be understood."  (§10, p. 61)

> "That this conception is right appears, I think, from the fact that it is possible to construct inferences which are in full accord with the logic of imperatives enunciated, but which are not immediately felt to be evident, but rather evidently false."  (§10, pp. 61–62)

> "that is to say, from the imperative I(x) we may infer the imperative I(x v y), e.g. from: slip the letter into the letter-box! we may infer, slip the letter into the letter-box or burn it!"  (§10, p. 62)

> "It will be seen that, interpreted as a satisfaction-function, this inference is unimpeachable: If the first imperative is satisfied, (if the letter has been slipped into the letter-box), then the other imperative too has been satisfied (it is then true that either has the letter been slipped into the letter-box, or it has been burnt)."  (§10, p. 62)

> "But it is equally obvious that this inference is not immediately conceived to be logically valid."  (§10, p. 62)

> "Similar results are arrived at by applying the other truth-functions, but I do not find it necessary to pursue this question."  (§10, p. 62; OCR "truth-funcetions" corrected)

> "»Slip the letter into the letter-box!» is satisfied, then the imperative, »Either slip the letter into the letter-box or burn it!» is also satisfied."  (§12, table, disjunction, a. Logic of satisfaction, p. 65)

> "»Slip the letter into the letter-box!» is valid, then the compound imperative, »Either the letter is to be slipped into the letter-box, or it is to be burnt» is valid too."  (§12, table, disjunction, b. Logic of validity, p. 66)

> "A logical interpretation becomes possible also in case the logical values »validity» and »non-validity» are attributed to the imperative, so that an imperative I is said to be valid when a certain, further defined psychological state is present in a certain person, and to be non-valid when no such state is present."  (§11, p. 62)

Relied on for: the original statement of the paradox (ross-paradox.md);
  Ross's distinction between a logic of satisfaction and a logic of
  validity; his calling the satisfaction reading an "evasion".
Context: Ross reviews earlier proposals (Mally, Menger, Dubislav,
  Jørgensen, Hofstadter & McKinsey 1939) for a logic of imperatives. §9–10
  discuss the "Dubislav-Jørgensen transformation method", which Ross
  reads as a logic of satisfaction-functions; the letter example is his
  case of an inference "in full accord with the logic of imperatives
  enunciated, but which are not immediately felt to be evident, but
  rather evidently false". §12 tabulates negation, disjunction and
  implication under both logics; §13 explains the felt evidence of
  practical inferences by a tacit premise of freedom from contradiction
  between imperatives of one system, which he says holds for negation
  "but not in respect of the other logical value functions".

> "oxymoron (Rhet.) ; ' sharp-dull \ The combining in one expression of
> two terms that are ordinarily contradictory, & whose exceptional
> coincidence is therefore arresting. A cheerful pessimist ; Harmonious
> discord ; His honour rooted in dishonour stood, And faith unfaithful
> kept him falsely true."  (Technical terms, p. 613; line-break hyphens joined)

> "gentle. The gentle art. This phrase, long a favourite with anglers as
> an affectionate description of their pursuit, was cleverly used by
> Whistler in his title The Gentle Art of Making Enemies. The oxymoron was
> what made it effective ; but the journalist, aware that Whistler made a
> hit with the gentle art, & failing to see how he did it, has now, by
> rough handling on inappropriate occasions, reduced it to a Battered
> ornament"  (p. 213; line-break hyphens joined)

> "such oxymorons as The gentle art of doing something ungentle ;"  (Worn-out humour, p. 735; listed among phrases of which "we, i. e. the average adult, not only are not amused")

Relied on for: Fowler's definition (the coincidence of contraries is
"arresting"); his view that an oxymoron can be effective once and
"Battered" by repetition; his listing of worn-out oxymorons as a fault
of style.
Context: "Technical terms" is Fowler's glossary of rhetorical and
grammatical terms; "Worn-out humour" lists stock facetiae he advises
writers to avoid. Fowler does not discuss the loose "contradiction in
terms" sense in the passages read.

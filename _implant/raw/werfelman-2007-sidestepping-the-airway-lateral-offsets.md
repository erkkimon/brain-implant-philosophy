# Werfelman 2007 — "Sidestepping the Airway": strategic lateral offsets and navigation accuracy

Source: Linda Werfelman, "Sidestepping the Airway", AeroSafety World (Flight
  Safety Foundation), March 2007, pp. 40–45.
Original: https://flightsafety.org/asw/mar07/asw_mar07_p40-45.pdf
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-09 (PDF via curl, text by pdftotext)

> "Some voices in the aviation industry are challenging the traditional belief that the centerline of an airway is the safest position for an airplane."  (deck, p. 40)

> "In addition, many pilots began to question assumptions about the safety of flight on airway centerlines, recognizing that the lateral accuracy of 21st-century flight deck technology places an increasing number of airplanes exactly on the centerline."  (p. 41)

> "“Where airplanes used to be spread over a mile, they are now within a few feet of the centerline,” Voss said."  (p. 41; William R. Voss, president and CEO of Flight Safety Foundation)

> "“It was felt that these procedures would not only alleviate the [RVSM-related] wake turbulence issue but they would also introduce an additional ‘randomness’ to aircraft flight paths, and thus, the procedure would reduce the possibility of collision in the event of a vertical error,” Gardilčić said."  (pp. 41–42)

> "In other words, this would artificially degrade the accuracy of navigation systems so if there was a vertical error, aircraft would not be precisely on the centerline and possibly collide."  (p. 42; Dražen Gardilčić, ICAO Air Traffic Management Section, on the North Atlantic offset of 1 or 2 nm right of course)

> "“The use of highly accurate navigation systems, such as the global navigation satellite system (GNSS), by an increasing proportion of the aircraft population has had the effect of reducing the magnitude of lateral deviations from the route centerline and, consequently, increasing the probability of a collision should a loss of vertical separation between aircraft on the same route occur.”"  (p. 43; quoted from ICAO's Procedures for Air Navigation Services — Air Traffic Management, Doc 4444, 15.2.4, per the article's note 1)

> "Gardilčić said, however, that ICAO’s North Atlantic Systems Planning Group has recently expressed concern that not enough aircraft appear to be flying the offset procedure in the North Atlantic, “thus negating, in part, the safety benefits that could be obtained with greater participation.”"  (p. 43)

> "“The irony of the situation is … that the greater the accuracy of navigation without an offset strategy, the greater the chance is these days of a collision if ATC gets it wrong."  (p. 45; Phil Peguero, safety director at Airways New Zealand)

> "The offset achieves a controlled degrade of the navigation accuracy so that a small degree of horizontal distancing is created in case vertical application by the ANSP [air navigation services provider] has failed.”"  (p. 45; Peguero)

> "“These systems seen to work with no problem and are consistent with ICAO … standards for their use,” said Phil Peguero, safety director at Airways New Zealand."  (p. 45; "seen" sic in the source)

> "Others are discouraging wider use as unnecessary."  (p. 40)

> "The crash occurred while the two airplanes, which were being flown in opposite directions, were on the same airway and at the same altitude."  (p. 44; the Gol Boeing 737-800 / Embraer Legacy 600 collision over the Amazon, 29 September 2006)

> "If the pilots of either airplane involved in the Amazon midair collision had been using offset procedures, he said, the crash wouldn’t have occurred."  (p. 45; Capt. Rick Valdes, United Airlines, IFALPA and ALPA safety committees)

> "“We will need to consider the risk reduction and whether any new risks might be introduced, especially in busy Terminal Area airspace,” he said."  (p. 45; Richard Wright, U.K. NATS, on lateral offsets in domestic airspace)

> "“What you don’t want is pilots doing random offsets,” he said."  (p. 45; Voss)

Relied on for: the lateral form of the effect as stated by ICAO Doc 4444
(quoted in the article) and by named practitioners; the strategic lateral
offset procedure as the response, its North Atlantic origin and scope; the
reservations (offsets unnecessary per "others"; new risks in terminal
airspace per NATS; uncoordinated offsets per Voss); the Amazon 2006 case.
Context: the article is a trade-magazine report, not a study; it does not
use the phrase "navigation paradox". It reports that the offsets began after
RVSM (1997) also as a wake-turbulence measure, that NATS data showed offset
use under 10 percent against a hoped-for 67 percent, and that Frühwirth
(IFALPA) and Reed Mohn (SAS) favour wider use. ICAO Doc 4444 itself was not
read here.

# SEP "Freedom of Speech" (Howard) and "Freedom of Association" (Brownlee & Jenkins) — the democratic justifications

Source: (1) Jeffrey W. Howard, "Freedom of Speech", preamble and §2.3
  "Democracy theories"; (2) Kimberley Brownlee and David Jenkins,
  "Freedom of Association", §3.2 "Collective Associations"; both in The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N.
  Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.).

> "That there ought to be such legal protections for speech is uncontroversial among political and legal philosophers. But disagreement arises when we turn to the details."  (1, preamble)

> "It is possible to defend free speech on the noninstrumental ground that it is necessary to respect agents as democratic citizens."  (1, §2.3; Howard cites Rawls 2005)

> "Alternatively (or additionally), one can defend free speech on the instrumental ground that free speech promotes democracy, or whatever values democracy is meant to serve."  (1, §2.3)

> "Put differently, one core function of many expressive associations or “intermediate” associations is to check the power of the state."  (2, §3.2; the authors cite Craiutu 2008 and Levy 2014)

Relied on for: the encyclopedia authors' report that free speech and
  free association are defended, among other grounds, by their relation
  to democracy and to limits on state power.
Context: (1) §2 lists listener, speaker, democracy, thinker, toleration
  and instrumental theories, and §2.7 free-speech skepticism; democracy
  theories are one family among these. (2) The paragraph continues that
  to "create genuine space for democratic dissent, groups must be allowed
  to exclude" on expressive grounds (Bedi 2010, as cited).

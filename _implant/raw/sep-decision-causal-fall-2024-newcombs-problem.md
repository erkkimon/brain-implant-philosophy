## §2.1 Newcomb's Problem

> "Robert Nozick (1969) presented a dilemma for decision theory. He constructed an example in which the standard principle of dominance conflicts with the standard principle of expected-utility maximization. Nozick called the example Newcomb’s Problem after the physicist, William Newcomb, who first formulated the problem."  (§2.1)

> "Because the outcome of two-boxing is better by $T than the outcome of one-boxing given each prediction, two-boxing dominates one-boxing. Two-boxing is the rational choice according to the principle of dominance."  (§2.1; "$T" is the transparent box's thousand, "$M" the million)

> "Hence, using conditional probabilities to compute expected utilities, one-boxing’s expected utility exceeds two-boxing’s expected utility. One-boxing is the rational choice according to the principle of expected-utility maximization."  (§2.1)

> "The essential feature of Newcomb’s problem is an inferior act’s correlation with a good state that it does not causally promote."  (§2.1)

> "In realistic, medical Newcomb problems, a medical condition and a behavioral symptom have a common cause and are correlated although neither causes the other."  (§2.1)

> "Also, Allan Gibbard and William Harper (1978: Sec. 12) and David Lewis (1979) observe that a Prisoner’s Dilemma with psychological twins poses a Newcomb problem for each player."  (§2.1)

> "Arif Ahmed (2018) offers a collection of essays on Newcomb’s problem. Kenny Easwaran (forthcoming) distinguishes Newcomb-like problems according to opportunities for causal intervention."  (§2.1)

## §2.2 Stalnaker's solution; Gibbard & Harper

> "In a letter to Lewis, Stalnaker (1972) proposed a way of reconciling decision principles in Newcomb’s problem. He suggested calculating an act’s expected utility using probabilities of conditionals in place of conditional probabilities."  (§2.2)

> "Consequently, using probabilities of conditionals to compute expected utility, two-boxing’s expected utility exceeds one-boxing’s expected utility. Therefore, the principle of expected-utility maximization makes the same recommendation as does the principle of dominance."  (§2.2)

> "Gibbard and Harper (1978) elaborated and made public Stalnaker’s resolution of Newcomb’s problem. They distinguished causal decision theory, which uses probabilities of subjunctive conditionals, from evidential decision theory, which uses conditional probabilities."  (§2.2)

> "One type they called value and represented with V. It indicates news-value or auspiciousness. The other type they called utility and represented with U. It indicates efficacy in attainment of goals."  (§2.2)

## §2.3 Variants (Skyrms, Lewis)

> "Brian Skyrms (1980: Sec IIC; 1982) presented a version of causal decision theory that dispenses with probabilities of subjunctive conditionals."  (§2.3)

> "Lewis (1981) presented a version of causal decision theory that calculates expected utility using probabilities of dependency hypotheses instead of probabilities of subjunctive conditionals."  (§2.3)

> "In cases such as Newcomb’s problem, their calculations yield the same recommendations as calculations of expected utility employing probabilities of subjunctive conditionals."  (§2.3)

## §2.4 Representation theorems

> "Richard Jeffrey ([1965] 1983) presented a representation theorem for evidential decision theory, using its formula for expected utility."  (§2.4)

## §2.5 Objections

> "The most common objection to causal decision theory is that it yields the wrong choice in Newcomb’s problem. It yields two-boxing, whereas one-boxing is correct. Terry Horgan (1981 [1985]), Paul Horwich (1987: Chap. 11), and Caspar Hare and Brian Hedden (2016) for example, promote one-boxing. The main rationale for one-boxing is that one-boxers fare better than do two-boxers."  (§2.5)

> "Causal decision theorists respond that Newcomb’s problem is an unusual case that rewards irrationality. One-boxing is irrational even if one-boxers prosper. Bales (2018) rejects the argument that two-boxing is irrational"  (§2.5)

> "Some theorists hold that one-boxing is plainly rational if the prediction is completely reliable. They maintain that if the prediction is certainly accurate, then choice reduces to taking $M or taking $T. This view oversimplifies."  (§2.5)

> "Efficacy still trumps auspiciousness, as Howard Sobel (1994: Chap. 5) argues."  (§2.5)

> "A way of reconciling the two sides of the debate about Newcomb’s problem acknowledges that a rational person should prepare for the problem by cultivating a disposition to one-box."  (§2.5)

> "It may conclude that cultivating a disposition to one-box is rational although one-boxing itself is irrational."  (§2.5)

> "Another objection to causal decision theory concedes that two-boxing is the rational choice in Newcomb’s problem but rejects causal principles of choice that yield two-boxing. It seeks noncausal principles that yield two-boxing."  (§2.5)

> "Jeffrey ([1965] 1983, 2004), for the sake of parsimony, formulates decision principles that do not rely on causal relations."  (§2.5)

> "Ellery Eells (1981, 1982) contends that evidential decision theory yields causal decision theory’s recommendations but, more economically, without reliance on causal apparatus. In particular, evidential decision theory yields two-boxing in Newcomb’s problem."  (§2.5)

> "Horgan (1981 [1985]) and Huw Price (1986) make similar points."  (§2.5, of the common-cause argument that evidential decision theory yields two-boxing)

> "This defense of evidential decision theory is called the tickle defense because it assumes that an introspected condition screens off the correlation between choice and prediction."  (§2.5)

> "Decision theory should prescribe a rational choice for such agents, and evidential decision theory may not do that correctly, as Lewis (1981: 10–11) and John Pollock (2010) argue."  (§2.5)

> "Horwich (1987: Chap. 11) rejects Eells’s argument because, even if an agent knows that her choice springs from her beliefs and desires, she may be unaware of the mechanism by which her beliefs and desires produce her choice."  (§2.5)

> "Eells (1984a) constructs a dynamic version of the tickle defense to meet this objection."  (§2.5)

> "He concludes that even in cases where evidential decision theory yields the right recommendation, it does not yield it for the right reasons."  (§2.5, of Sobel 1994: Chap. 2)

> "Price (2012) proposes a blend of evidential and causal decision theory"  (§2.5)

> "Ahmed (2014a) champions evidential decision theory and advances several objections to causal decision theory. His objections assume some controversial points about rational choice, including a controversial principle for sequences of choices."  (§2.5)

## §3.6 Ratification

> "Jeffrey used ratification as a means of making evidential decision theory yield the same recommendations as causal decision theory. In Newcomb’s problem, for instance, two-boxing is the only self-ratifying option. However, Jeffrey (2004: 113n) concedes that evidential decision theory’s reliance on ratification does not make it agree with causal decision theory in all cases."  (§3.6)

> "Moreover, Joyce (2007) argues that the motivation for ratification appeals to causal relations, so that even if it yields correct recommendations using Jeffrey’s formula for expected-utility, it still does not yield a purely evidential decision theory."  (§3.6)

## §4 Related topics and concluding remarks

> "Given a typical causal model for Newcomb’s problem, knowledge of the common cause of a decision and a prediction screens off the correlation between the decision and the prediction."  (§4)

> "Wolfgang Spohn (2012) constructs for Newcomb’s problem a causal model that distinguishes a decision and its execution and argues that given the model causal decision theory recommends one-boxing."  (§4)

> "Causal decision theory is the prevailing form of decision theory among those who distinguish causal and evidential decision theory."  (§4)

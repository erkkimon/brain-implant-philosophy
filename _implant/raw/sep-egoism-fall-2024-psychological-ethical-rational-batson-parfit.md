> "Egoism can be a descriptive or a normative position. Psychological egoism, the most famous descriptive position, claims that each person has but one ultimate aim: her own welfare."  (preamble)

> "Ethical egoism claims I morally ought to perform some action if and only if, and because, performing that action maximizes my self-interest."  (preamble)

> "Rational egoism claims that I ought to perform some action if and only if, and because, performing that action maximizes my self-interest. (Here the “ought” is not restricted to the moral “ought”.)"  (preamble)

> "All forms of egoism require explication of “self-interest” (or “welfare” or “well-being”). There are three main theories."  (§1)

> "Preference or desire accounts identify self-interest with the satisfaction of one’s desires."  (§1)

> "Objective accounts identify self-interest with the possession of states (such as virtue or knowledge) that are valuable independently of whether they are desired."  (§1)

> "Hybrid accounts give a role to both desires (or pleasure) and states that are valuable independently of whether they are desired."  (§1)

> "Psychological egoism is supported by our frequent observation of self-interested behavior."  (§1)

> "A common objection to psychological egoism, made famously by Joseph Butler, is that I must desire things other than my own welfare in order to get welfare."  (§1)

> "Welfare results from my action, but cannot be the only aim of my action."  (§1)

> "My welfare might consist simply in the satisfaction of self-regarding desires."  (§1)

> "If self-interest is identified with the satisfaction of all of one’s preferences, then all intentional action is self-interested (at least if intentional actions are always explained by citing preferences, as most believe). Psychological egoism turns out to be trivially true."  (§1)

> "First, Daniel Batson and colleagues found that increased empathy leads to increased helping behaviour."  (§1)

> "He found that the altruistic hypothesis always made superior predictions."  (§1)

> "But on the whole, Batson’s experiments are very bad news for psychological egoism."  (§1; Shaver's assessment)

> "Second, Elliot Sober and David Wilson argue that evolutionary theory supports altruism."  (§1)

> "predominant egoism:” we act unselfishly only rarely, and then typically where the sacrifice is small and the gain to others is large or where those benefiting are friends, family, or favorite causes."  (§1, Kavka 1986, 64–80, as Shaver reports; the opening quotation mark falls before "predominant")

> "Ethical egoists can reply, however, that egoism generates many of the same duties to others."  (§2, the cooperation argument)

> "In dealings with others who lack these abilities, the egoist has no reason to cooperate."  (§2)

> "G. E. Moore argued that ethical egoism is self-contradictory."  (§2)

> "One might hold that “my good” should not be analyzed in terms of what Moore calls “good absolutely” but rather is a distinct concept, good-for-me, linked only to agent-relative reasons (Mackie 1976, Smith 2003)."  (§2)

> "A second argument against ethical egoism was made by H. A. Prichard. He argues that self-interest is the wrong sort of reason."  (§2)

> "According to the instrumental theory, I ought to perform some action if and only if, and because, performing that action maximizes the satisfaction of my preferences."  (§3)

> "This conflict with the instrumental theory is a major problem for rational egoism."  (§3; Shaver's assessment)

> "A different problem for rational egoism is that it appears arbitrary."  (§3)

> "In a much-quoted passage, Sidgwick claimed that rational egoism is not arbitrary:"  (§3; the passage quoted is Methods of Ethics 1907, 498)

> "Debate over rational egoism was revitalized by Parfit 1984 pts. II-III."  (§3)

> "According to the present-aim theory, I have most reason to do what maximizes the satisfaction of my present desires."  (§3)

> "Even if I have reason to do what promotes my self-interest, most think I have other reasons as well."  (§3, reporting Ewing 1965: 28)

> "Prospects for psychological egoism are dim."  (§4; Shaver's conclusion)

> "Ethical egoists do best by defending rational egoism instead."  (§4)

> "Rational egoism faces objections from arbitrariness, Nagel and Rachels, Parfit, Ewing, and evolutionary debunking. These worries are not decisive."  (§4; Shaver's assessment)

Relied on for: the three egoisms defined side by side; the three accounts
of self-interest; Butler's objection and the egoist reply; the trivial
version; Batson's findings as Shaver reports and assesses them; Sober and
Wilson; Kavka's predominant egoism; the cooperation argument; Moore's,
Prichard's and the arbitrariness objections; the instrumental and
present-aim theories; Sidgwick and Parfit; Shaver's own conclusion
(reported as his).
Context: §1 also discusses a soldier who throws himself on a grenade; §2

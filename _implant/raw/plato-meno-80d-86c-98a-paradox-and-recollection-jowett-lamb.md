## Meno's questions (80d)

> "And how will you enquire, Socrates, into that which you do not know? What will you put forth as the subject of enquiry? And if you find what you want, how will you ever know that this is the thing which you did not know?"  (80d, Jowett)

> "Why, on what lines will you look, Socrates, for a thing of whose nature you know nothing at all?  Pray, what sort of thing, amongst those that you know not, will you treat us to as the object of your search?  Or even supposing, at the best, that you hit upon it, how will you know it is the thing you did not know?"  (80d, Lamb)

Greek (80d, Burnet): "καὶ τίνα τρόπον ζητήσεις, ὦ Σώκρατες, τοῦτο ὃ μὴ οἶσθα τὸ παράπαν ὅτι ἐστίν;"

Context: immediately before, Socrates has said he does not know what virtue
is but is willing to inquire with Meno (80c–d): "And now I know not what virtue is, and you seem to be in the same case, although you did once perhaps know before you touched me. However, I have no objection to join with you in the enquiry." (Jowett)

## Socrates' reformulation (80e)

> "I know, Meno, what you mean; but just see what a tiresome dispute you are introducing. You argue that a man cannot enquire either about that which he knows, or about that which he does not know; for if he knows, he has no need to enquire; and if not, he cannot; for he does not know the very subject about which he is to enquire (Compare Aristot. Post. Anal.)."  (80e, Jowett; the parenthesis is Jowett's cross-reference, printed in the text)

> "Do you see what a captious argument you are introducing—that, forsooth, a man cannot inquire either about what he knows or about whit he does not know?"  (80e, Lamb; "whit" as printed on Perseus)

Greek (80e, Burnet): "ὁρᾷς τοῦτον ὡς ἐριστικὸν λόγον κατάγεις, ὡς οὐκ ἄρα ἔστιν ζητεῖν ἀνθρώπῳ οὔτε ὃ οἶδε οὔτε ὃ μὴ οἶδε;"
— the adjective is ἐριστικόν ("eristic"); Jowett renders it "tiresome
dispute", Lamb "captious argument".

> "MENO: Well, Socrates, and is not the argument sound?\
> SOCRATES: I think not."  (81a, Jowett)

## Recollection introduced (81a–e)

> "Some of them were priests and priestesses, who had studied how they might be able to give a reason of their profession: there have been poets also, who spoke of these things by inspiration, like Pindar, and many others who were inspired."  (81a–b, Jowett)

> "The soul, then, as being immortal, and having been born again many times, and having seen all things that exist, whether in this world or in the world below, has knowledge of them all"  (81b–c, Jowett; Lamb's 81b ends in the parallel sentence)

> "for as all nature is akin, and the soul has learned all things; there is no difficulty in her eliciting or as men say learning, out of a single recollection all the rest, if a man is strenuous and does not faint; for all enquiry and all learning is but recollection."  (81c–d, Jowett)

> "And therefore we ought not to listen to this sophistical argument about the impossibility of enquiry: for it will make us idle; and is sweet only to the sluggard; but the other saying will make us active and inquisitive."  (81d–e, Jowett)

Greek (81d, Burnet): "τὸ γὰρ ζητεῖν ἄρα καὶ τὸ μανθάνειν ἀνάμνησις ὅλον ἐστίν. οὔκουν δεῖ πείθεσθαι τούτῳ τῷ ἐριστικῷ λόγῳ"
— ἀνάμνησις (anamnesis, "recollection"); ἐριστικῷ λόγῳ again, rendered
"sophistical argument" by Jowett and "captious argument" by Lamb (81d).

> "SOCRATES: I told you, Meno, just now that you were a rogue, and now you ask whether I can teach you, when I am saying that there is no teaching, but only recollection"  (81e–82a, Jowett)

## The slave-boy (82b–85b)

> "Now observe closely whether he strikes you as recollecting or as learning from me."  (82b, Lamb)

> "Do you observe, Meno, that I am not teaching the boy anything, but merely asking him each time?"  (82e, Lamb)

The boy first says the side of the doubled square is double the side
("Clearly, Socrates, double." 82e, Lamb), then that it is three feet, then:

> "BOY: Indeed, Socrates, I do not know."  (84a, Jowett)

> "He did not know at first, and he does not know now, what is the side of a figure of eight feet: but then he thought that he knew, and answered confidently as if he knew, and had no difficulty; now he has a difficulty, and neither knows nor fancies that he knows."  (84a–b, Jowett)

> "SOCRATES: If we have made him doubt, and given him the 'torpedo's shock,' have we done him any harm?"  (84b, Jowett)

> "SOCRATES: And that is the line which the learned call the diagonal. And if this is the proper name, then you, Meno's slave, are prepared to affirm that the double space is the square of the diagonal?"  (85b, Jowett)

## Socrates' conclusions (85b–86c)

> "SOCRATES: Then he who does not know may still have true notions of that which he does not know?"  (85c, Jowett)

> "SOCRATES: And at present these notions have just been stirred up in him, as in a dream; but if he were frequently asked the same questions, in different forms, he would know as well as any one at last?"  (85c–d, Jowett)

> "Without anyone having taught him, and only through questions put to him, he will understand, recovering the knowledge out of himself?"  (85d, Lamb)

> "SOCRATES: But if he always possessed this knowledge he would always have known; or if he has acquired the knowledge he could not have acquired it in this life, unless he has been taught geometry"  (85d–e, Jowett)

> "SOCRATES: And if the truth of all things always existed in the soul, then the soul is immortal. Wherefore be of good cheer, and try to recollect what you do not know, or rather what you do not remember."  (86b, Jowett)

> "Some things I have said of which I am not altogether confident. But that we shall be better and braver and less helpless if we think that we ought to enquire, than we should have been if we indulged in the idle fancy that there was no knowing and no use in seeking to know what we do not know;--that is a theme upon which I am ready to fight, in word and deed, to the utmost of my power."  (86b–c, Jowett)

## True opinion tied down (98a)

> "therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it."  (97e–98a, Jowett)

> "and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning.  And this process, friend Meno, is recollection, as in our previous talk we have agreed."  (98a, Lamb)

Relied on for: the wording of Meno's three questions and Socrates'
two-horned reformulation; the Greek adjective ἐριστικός and how two
translators render it; recollection as Socrates' answer, introduced on the
authority of priests, priestesses and poets; the slave-boy's passage from
false confidence to perplexity to the diagonal; Socrates' inference to the
soul's immortality and his own hedge (86b–c); recollection identified with
tying down true opinions (98a).

Context: the paradox interrupts the search for a definition of virtue
(70a–80d); after 86c Meno asks to return to whether virtue can be taught,
and Socrates proceeds "by hypothesis". The geometry problem is doubling a
square of side two feet (area four); the answer is the square on the
diagonal.

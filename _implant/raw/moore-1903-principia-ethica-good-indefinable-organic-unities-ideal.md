> "If I am asked ‘What is good?’ my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter."  (ch. I §6)

> "This view I propose to call the ‘naturalistic fallacy’ and of it I shall now endeavour to dispose."  (ch. I §10)

> "whatever definition be offered, it may be always asked, with significance, of the complex so defined, whether it is itself good."  (ch. I §13)

> "The paradox, to which it is necessary to call attention, is that _the value of such a whole bears no regular proportion to the sum of the values of its parts_."  (ch. I §18)

> "The only reason I can have for aiming at ‘my own good,’ is that it is _good absolutely_ that what I so call should belong to me--_good absolutely_ that I should _have_ something, which, if I have it, others cannot have."  (ch. III §59)

> "‘right’ does and can mean nothing but ‘cause of a good result,’ and is thus identical with ‘useful’;"  (ch. V §89)

> "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects."  (ch. VI §113)

Relied on for: Moore's own statements of the indefinability of "good",
  the name "naturalistic fallacy", the open-question test, the principle
  of organic unities, his argument against egoism (ch. III §59; cf.
  Schultz, SEP "Henry Sidgwick" §3, on Moore 1903 and the dualism), the definition of "right" and the ideal.
Context: §6 continues that propositions about the good "are all of them
  synthetic and never analytic"; §10 follows an analogy with "yellow";
  §13 applies the test to "that which we desire to desire"; §18 is in the
  chapter's discussion of intrinsic value and wholes; §59 is in ch. III
  "Hedonism", on egoism; §89 opens ch. V "Ethics in Relation to Conduct";
  §113 opens the argument of ch. VI "The Ideal" and goes on to credit
  Sidgwick on the value of the mere existence of beauty.

> "The liar paradox belongs to the category of semantic paradoxes, since it is based on the semantic notion of truth. Other well-known semantic paradoxes include Grelling’s paradox, Berry’s paradox, and Richard’s paradox."  (§1.1)

> "Berry’s paradox is another paradox based on an impredicative definition, or rather, an impredicative description."  (§1.1)

> "the least number that cannot be referred to by a description containing less than 100 symbols."  (§1.1)

> "The contradiction is that this description containing 93 symbols denotes a number which, by definition, cannot be denoted by any description containing less than 100 symbols. The description is of course impredicative, since it implicitly refers to all descriptions, including itself."  (§1.1)

> "Definitions such as this which depends on a set of entities, at least one of which is the entity being defined, are called impredicative."  (§1.1, said of Grelling's "heterological")

> "though according to Roy Sorensen (2003, p. 332), the true inventor is G.G. Berry (1867–1928), the Oxford librarian to whom also the earlier mentioned Berry’s paradox is accredited."  (§1.6, on the postcard paradox)

> "In case of the semantic paradoxes, it seems that it is our understanding of fundamental semantic concepts such as truth (in the liar paradox and Grelling’s paradox) and definability (in Berry’s and Richard’s paradoxes) that are deficient."  (§2)

> "If we fully understood these concepts, we should be able to deal with them without being led to contradictions."  (§2)

Relied on for: the classification of Berry's paradox as semantic and
impredicative; the 100-symbol version; Berry's dates and description as
"the Oxford librarian"; Bolander's diagnosis (definability).
Context: §1.1 opens with the liar and Eubulides, then Grelling, Berry and
Richard; §1.4 fits Richard's paradox and the liar to "Russell's schema"
(predicates P, Q and a diagonal function δ); §2 begins "The significance
of a paradox is its indication of a flaw or deficiency in our
understanding of the central concepts involved in it." Berry is not given
a separate treatment beyond these passages.

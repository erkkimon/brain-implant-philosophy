# James 1884 — "The Dilemma of Determinism": soft determinism, the two horns, regret and possibility, the chess analogy

Source: William James, "The Dilemma of Determinism" (address to the Harvard
  Divinity Students, Unitarian Review, September 1884), reprinted in The
  Will to Believe, and Other Essays in Popular Philosophy (New York, London
  and Bombay: Longmans, Green & Co., 1897), pp. 145–183; one sentence from
  "The Will to Believe" (p. 5). Page numbers are the 1897 page markers {n}
  preserved in the Gutenberg transcription (a marker begins its page).

> "The determinism to whose seductions you are most exposed is what I have called soft determinism,--the determinism which allows considerations of good and bad to mingle with those of cause and effect in deciding what sort of a universe this may rationally be held to be."  (p. 166)

> "The dilemma of this determinism is one whose left horn is pessimism and whose right horn is subjectivism."  (p. 166)

> "We have thus clearly revealed to our view what may be called the dilemma of determinism, so far as determinism pretends to think things out at all."  (p. 166)

> "I cannot understand the belief that an act is bad, without regret at its happening. I cannot understand regret without the admission of real, genuine possibilities in the world."  (p. 175)

> "For my own part, though, whatever difficulties may beset the philosophy of objective right and wrong, and the indeterminism it seems to imply, determinism, with its alternative of pessimism or romanticism, contains difficulties that are greater still."  (p. 176)

> "It is a word of _impotence_, and is therefore the only sincere word we can use, if, in granting freedom to certain things, we grant it honestly, and really risk the game."  (p. 180, of the word 'chance')

> "Does not the admission of such an unguaranteed chance or freedom preclude utterly the notion of a Providence governing the world?"  (p. 180)

> "The expert intends to beat. But he cannot foresee exactly what any one actual move of his adversary may be."  (p. 181)

From "The Will to Believe" (1896):

> "In Pascal's Thoughts there is a celebrated passage known in literature as Pascal's wager."  (p. 5)

Relied on for: the name and the two horns of the dilemma of determinism;
why James prefers the word 'chance'; James's argument from judgments of regret to real possibilities and his
stated preference for indeterminism; the "final doubt" about Providence and
the chess analogy (the expert does not foresee each move); that "The Will to
Believe" discusses Pascal's wager.
Context: The 1897 table of contents summarises the essay as "Determinism
involves pessimism, 159. Escape via Subjectivism, 164. Subjectivism leads to
corruption, 170. A world with chance in it is morally the less irrational
alternative, 176. Chance not incompatible with an ultimate Providence, 180."
"Hard" or "merely mechanical" determinism is set aside on p. 166 as not
thinking the moral question out. The chess analogy continues with the expert
knowing "all the _possible_ moves" of the novice and the novice standing for
"us finite free agents" (see
james-1897-will-to-believe-moral-philosopher-dilemma-of-determinism.md).

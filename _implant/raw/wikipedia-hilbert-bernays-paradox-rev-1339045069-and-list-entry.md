List entry:

> "Hilbert–Bernays paradox: If there was a name for a natural number that is identical to a name of the successor of that number, there would be a natural number equal to its successor."  (List of paradoxes, "Logic", self-reference subsection)

Article, lead and History:

> "The Hilbert–Bernays paradox is a distinctive paradox belonging to the family of the paradoxes of reference. It is named after David Hilbert and Paul Bernays."  (lead)

> "The paradox appears in Hilbert and Bernays' Grundlagen der Mathematik and is used by them to show that a sufficiently strong consistent theory cannot contain its own reference functor."  (History; cites Hilbert & Bernays 1939, Springer, pp. 263–278)

> "Although it was largely unnoticed over the course of the 20th century, it has recently been rediscovered and appreciated for the distinctive difficulties it presents."  (History; cites Priest 2005, Towards Non-Being, OUP, pp. 156–178)

Article, Formulation (schemas as printed, quote marks ′ ′ and angle names < > kept):

> "(R) If a exists, the referent of the name ′a′ is identical with a"

> "(H) <h> is identical with ′(the referent of <h>)+1′"

> "(1) The referent of <h> is identical with n"

> "(3) The referent of h is identical with (the referent of h)+1"

> "(4) n is identical with n+1"

> "But (4) is absurd, since no number is identical with its successor."

Article, Solutions:

> "Since, given the diagonal lemma, every sufficiently strong theory will have to accept something like (H), absurdity can only be avoided either by rejecting the principle of naive reference (R) or by rejecting classical logic (which validates the reasoning from (R) and (H) to absurdity)."

> "On the first approach, typically whatever one says about the Liar paradox carries over smoothly to the Hilbert–Bernays paradox."  (cites Keith Simmons, "Reference and Paradox", in Beall ed., Liars and Heaps, OUP 2003, pp. 230–252)

> "solutions to the Liar paradox that reject the law of excluded middle (which is not used by the Hilbert–Bernays paradox) have denied that there is such a thing as the referent of h"  (cites Field, Saving Truth from Paradox, OUP 2008, pp. 291–293)

> "solutions to the Liar paradox that reject the law of noncontradiction (which is likewise not used by the Hilbert–Bernays paradox) have claimed that h refers to more than one object."  (cites Priest 2005)

Clarify tag on the Solutions sentence (dated May 2015):

> "Why should (1) be true, i.e. why should a number exist that satisfies the property h? For example, no natural number is designated by the name 'The number whose square is 2', but this isn't called a paradox."

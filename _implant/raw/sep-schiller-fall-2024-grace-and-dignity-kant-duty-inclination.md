# SEP Friedrich Schiller (Fall 2024) — "On Grace and Dignity" (1793) and Kant on duty and inclination

Source: Lydia L. Moland, "Friedrich Schiller", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (Fall 2024
  edition), ed. Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman; first published 2017-04-21, substantive
  revision 2021-04-26 (copyright line: "Copyright © 2021 by Lydia L. Moland"), §§2.1–2.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/schiller/ (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped, each quotation checked
  by script against the stripped text)

> "Schiller began his philosophical retreat with a thorough study of Kant and was immediately inspired by Kant’s description of human freedom."  (§2.1)

> "Grace, in short, bridges the Kantian divide: “It is in a beautiful soul that sensuousness and reason, duty and inclination are in harmony, and grace is their expression as appearance” (NA XX, 288/GD 153)."  (§2.2)

> "Once we understand the underlying unity that grace suggests, however, we can correct the excesses brought about by Kant’s rigorous separation of reason and inclination."  (§2.2, Moland's statement of Schiller's view)

> "Schiller’s response to Kant’s depiction of duty and inclination was taken up by none other than Kant himself, who in Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason devoted a footnote to clarifying his position (48–9)."  (§2.2)

> "“On Grace and Dignity” has thus been much discussed in Kant scholarship both as a possible contribution to Kantian theories of moral psychology and as supporting the possibility of a Kant-based ethics of character (Baxley 2010; Deligiorgi 2011; Winegar 2013)."  (§2.2)

Relied on for: Schiller's 1793 response to Kant's separation of duty and inclination, as Moland
reports it; Kant's footnote reply in Religion; the essay's reception in Kant scholarship.
Context: Moland quotes Schiller's 1793 letter to Körner praising Kant's "determine yourself
from within yourself" (§2.1); §2.2 continues that a "beautiful soul" can obey reason "with joy",
and that where pain forces a conflict the soul shows "dignity" rather than grace. The entry
does not quote the satirical distich on duty often attributed to Schiller (a search for
"Gewissensskrupel" and "scruple" finds no match).

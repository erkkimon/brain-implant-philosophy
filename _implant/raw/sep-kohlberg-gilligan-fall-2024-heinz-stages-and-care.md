## LeBar, "Justice as a Virtue"

> "Educational psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg was inspired by Piaget to propose a conception of moral development that postulated six stages of human moral development. In his earliest work, Kohlberg identified the highest stage of such development with a concern for justice and human rights based on universal principles."  (§2)

> "The paradigm of moral development involves judgments that are “reversible,” in the sense that each party to the issue can accept the correct judgment by reversing his or her perspective and taking up the viewpoint of the other (Kohlberg 1981)."  (§2)

> "In fact, Kohlberg was impressed by the work of Rawls, and thought that the nature of Rawls’ “original position of equality” exemplified the kind of reversibility that is paradigmatic of the highest form of moral thought (Kohlberg 1981, p. 204)."  (§2)

> "However, his approach treats utilitarianism as less cognitively advanced (more primitive) than rationalist views like Kant’s, and utilitarians (like R.M. Hare) naturally called into question the objectivity and intellectual fairness of Kohlberg’s account."  (§2)

> "More significantly, perhaps, the evidence for Kohlberg’s stage sequence was drawn from studies of boys, and when one applies the sequence to the study of young girls, it turns out that girls on average end up at a less advanced stage of moral development than boys do."  (§2)

> "In her 1982 book In a Different Voice: Psychological Theory and Women’s Development, Carol Gilligan responded to Kohlberg’s views by questioning whether a theory of moral development based solely on a sample of males could reasonably be used to draw conclusions about the inferior moral development of women."  (§2)

> "But Jean Hampton, among others, responded that Gilligan’s critique was itself a distortion, and that concerns for justice and individual rights are as significant for and in the moral lives of women as for men (Hampton 1993)."  (§2)

> "Kohlberg had offered a thought experiment about a man (“Heinz”) tempted to steal a life-saving drug to save his sick wife (Kohlberg 1981, p. 12)."  (§4)

> "Whereas boys are more likely to think of Heinz’ dilemma in terms of what is the right thing to do, girls, Gilligan argues, see the world as “a world of relationships and psychological truths where an awareness of the connection between people gives rise to a recognition of responsibility for one another” (Gillian 1983, p. 30)."  (§4; "Gillian" and "1983" as printed in the entry, whose bibliography lists Gilligan 1982 [1993])

> "Gilligan carefully frames this contrast as one between voices, not a matter of ranking of dispositions or virtues, but her work can and did provide a basis for making that sort of assessment between virtues"  (§4)

> "In some ways, Nel Nodding’s pioneering work in laying out an “ethic of care” takes such a step. Following Gilligan, she sees much ethical theory as missing a feminine voice"  (§4)

## Matthews & Mullin, "The Philosophy of Childhood"

> "Kohlberg presents a theory according to which morality develops in approximately six stages, though according to his research, few adults actually reach the fifth or sixth stages."  (§3)

> "However, Kohlberg maintained that no one skips a stage or regresses to an earlier stage."  (§3)

> "these are his basic six: the premoral (the 1st involving punishment and obedience, and the 2nd naive hedonism), the morality of conventional role conformity (the 3rd involving seeking good relations, the 4th maintaining authority) and the morality of accepted principles (the 5th involving the morality of contract, individual rights and accepted law, the 6th individual principles of conscience)."  (§3)

> "Kohlberg developed a test, which has been widely used, to determine the stage of any individual at any given time. The test requires responses to ethical dilemmas and is to be scored by consulting an elaborate manual."  (§3)

> "Gilligan argues that Kohlberg’s rule-oriented conception of morality has an orientation toward justice, which she associates with stereotypically male thinking, whereas women and girls are perhaps more likely to approach moral dilemmas with a “care” orientation."  (§3)

> "By contrast, Kohlberg’s moral-dilemma tests will assign pre-schoolers and even early elementary-school children to a pre-moral level."  (§3, contrasting Martin Hoffman's four stages of empathy)

## Norlock, "Feminist Ethics"

> "Gilligan argues that Kohlberg wrongly prioritizes a “morality of rights” and independence from others as better than, rather than merely different from, a “morality of responsibility” and intimate relationships with others (19)."  (§2.2)

> "Women, in Gilligan’s studies, were as likely to express the perspective of justice as they were to express a perspective that valued intimacy, responsibility, relationships, and caring for others, while seeing autonomy as “the illusory and dangerous quest” (Gilligan 1982, 48), in tension with the values of attachment."  (§2.2)

> "Nel Noddings’s influential work, Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education (1984), argues for the moral preferability of a care perspective"  (§2.2)

## Superson, "Feminist Moral Psychology"

> "Gilligan presented data from female and male subjects showing that by and large, females made decisions about moral dilemmas based on concrete situations in which the parties know details about each other, emphasizing feelings such as care for others, and preserving relations, while males for the most part decided on the basis of rule-following and justice while abstracting from the details of the case."  (§1)

> "This is not to say that Gilligan’s claims are free from controversy; rather, since she published the results of her study, her claims have been criticized for perpetuating sexist stereotypes (Tronto 1993), for being inaccurate both because there are not significant differences in the reasoning of males and females at different life stages (Walker 1993), and because any differences in moral reasoning have more to do with education or general cognitive development rather than gender (Greeno and Maccoby 1993), for being false because her sample size and characterizations are inadequate for drawing the conclusions she does about gendered reasoning (Luria 1993) and for invoking generalizations that can be used to support racist views (Moody-Adams 1991, 199)."  (§1)

> "Amy reasons from the care perspective, seeing morality as being responsive to others’ needs, not hurting others, and being in service to them."  (§1, reporting Hampton 1993's reading of "Gilligan’s much-discussed case of Amy and Jake")

> "Jake, on the other hand, a representative male moral reasoner, reasons from the justice perspective, seeing morality as a set of traffic rules that amount to pursuing one’s own interests without interfering with the interests of others."  (§1, same)

> "Hampton argues that neither is the ideal moral view, but that Amy suffers more because she is highly exploitable."  (§1)

## Machery & Stich, "The Moral/Conventional Distinction"

> "Elliot Turiel (1938–) was a student of Lawrence Kohlberg (1927–1987), who was, in turn, influenced by the work of Jean Piaget (1896–1980)."  (§3)

> "It is not the case that young children think of all normative rules in the same way; their normative cognition is not “undifferentiated”. Normative cognition in young children is much more subtle, more varied, and more complex than Kohlberg and Piaget had portrayed it."  (§3, Machery and Stich reporting Turiel's findings)

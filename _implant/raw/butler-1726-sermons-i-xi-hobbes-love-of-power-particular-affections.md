# Butler 1726 — Fifteen Sermons I and XI: the footnote against Hobbes ("only the love of power"), particular affections as one's own, "Happiness does not consist in self-love"

> "Cautions of being deceived with outward show, he retires within himself to see exactly what that is in the mind of man from whence this appearance proceeds; and, upon deep reflection, asserts the principle in the mind to be only the love of power, and delight in the exercise of it."  (Sermon I, footnote {2}; "he" is "a man of learning ... writing a grave book upon _human nature_", keyed by Butler's note {2a} to Hobbes)

> "Every particular affection, even the love of our neighbour, is as really our own affection as self-love; and the pleasure arising from its gratification is as much my own pleasure as the pleasure self-love would have from knowing I myself should be happy some time hence would be my own pleasure."  (Sermon XI)

> "Happiness does not consist in self-love. The desire of happiness is no more the thing itself than the desire of riches is the possession or enjoyment of them."  (Sermon XI)

Relied on for: Butler's objection to the reduction of benevolence to
self-love, in his words; his naming of Hobbes. Already excerpted
elsewhere: Sermon XI on immoderate self-love "disappointing itself"
(`butler-1726-sermon-xi-self-love-defeats-itself.md`).
Context: the footnote continues by asking whether the reader "could ...
be thoroughly satisfied that what is commonly called benevolence or
good-will was really the affection meant"; the Sermon I body argues "there
is a natural principle of _benevolence_ in man". Sermon XI argues that
there is "no peculiar rivalship or competition between self-love and
benevolence".

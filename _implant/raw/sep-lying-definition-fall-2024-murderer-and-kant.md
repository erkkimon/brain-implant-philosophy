# SEP The Definition of Lying and Deception (Fall 2024) — the would-be murderer, Kant's thief, who holds what

Source: James Edwin Mahon, "The Definition of Lying and Deception", *Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy* (Fall 2024 edition), ed. Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman; first published
  2008-02-21, substantive revision 2015-12-25 (copyright line: "Copyright © 2015 by James
  Edwin Mahon"), §§2.2, 2.3.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/lying-definition/ (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched and read)

> "Surely, for example, it is possible to lie to a would-be murderer, whether it is impermissible, as some absolutist deontologists maintain (Augustine 1952; Aquinas 1972 (cf.  MacIntyre 1995b); Kant 1996 (cf. Mahon 2006); Newman 1880; Geach 1977; Betz 1985; Pruss 1999; Tollefsen 2014), or permissible (i.e., either optional or obligatory), as consequentialists and moderate deontologists maintain (Constant 1964; Mill 1863; Sidgwick 1981; Bok 1978; MacIntyre 1995a; cf. Kagan 1998)."  (§2.3)

> "It has been objected that these moral deceptionist definitions are unduly narrow and restrictive (Bok 1978)."  (§2.3)

On Grotius's definition (L10):

> "violating that person’s right to exercise liberty of judgment. (Grotius 2005)"  (§2.3)

On Donagan's definition (L11):

> "To lie =df to freely make a believed-false statement to another fully responsible and rational person, with the intention that that other person believe that statement to be true"  (§2.3)


> "It is also not possible to lie to “a would-be murderer who threatens your life if you will not tell him where his quarry has gone” (Donagan 1977, 89), and in general when you are acting under duress in any way"  (§2.3)

On Grotius's definition (L10), quoting Bok:

> "“speaking falsely to those—like thieves—to whom truthfulness is not owed cannot be called lying” (Bok 1978, 14)."  (§2.3)

On Kant's thief example (Lectures on Ethics):

> "If the victim were to make the untruthful statement, “I have no money,” Kant says that this is not a lie, “for the other knows that… he also has no right whatever to demand the truth from me” (Kant 1997, 203; but see Mahon 2009)."  (§2.2)

> "Charles Fried also holds that lying requires an assertion and a breach of faith, but he rejects L6, arguing that it is possible for the victim to lie to the thief in Kant’s example (Fried 1978, 55 n1)."  (§2.2)

Relied on for: Mahon's two-family classification of positions on lying to a would-be murderer
and its named holders; the definitional route (Donagan, Grotius/Bok) on which such a statement
is not a lie; Kant's thief example as Mahon reports it.
Context: the murderer passage is part of an objection to "moral deceptionist" definitions of
lying (Grotius, Donagan), which make the permissibility of the statement part of whether it is a
lie; Mahon's point there is definitional (one can lie to a murderer, whatever its permissibility).

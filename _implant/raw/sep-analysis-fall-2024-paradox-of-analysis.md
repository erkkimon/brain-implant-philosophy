> "At the heart of all of them, too, lie the philosophical problems raised by Meno’s paradox, which anticipates what we now know as the paradox of analysis, concerning how an analysis can be both correct and informative"  (main entry, §2)

> "In his Essay on Philosophical Method (1933), for example, he criticizes Moorean philosophy, and develops his own response to what is essentially the paradox of analysis (concerning how an analysis can be both correct and informative), which he recognizes as having its root in Meno’s paradox."  (main entry, §5; "he" = R. G. Collingwood)

> "But in its general form what we have here is the paradox of analysis."  (s6 §4, after Moore's open-question argument)

> "(Although the problem itself goes back to the paradox of inquiry formulated in Plato’s Meno, and can be found articulated in Frege’s writings, too, the term ‘paradox of analysis’ was indeed first used in relation to Moore’s work, by Langford in 1942.)"  (s6 §4; link marker "{Quotation}" dropped)

> "Then either ‘A’ and ‘C’ have the same meaning, in which case the analysis expresses a trivial identity; or else they do not, in which case the analysis is incorrect. So it would seem that no analysis can be both correct and informative."  (s6 §4)

> "At the very least, it seems to cry out for a distinction between two kinds of ‘meaning’, such as the distinction between ‘sense’ and ‘reference’ that Frege drew, arguably precisely in response to this problem (see Beaney 2005; 2017, ch. 3)."  (s6 §4)

> "An analysis might then be deemed correct if ‘A’ and ‘C’ have the same reference, and informative if ‘C’ has a different, or more richly articulated, sense than ‘A’."  (s6 §4)

> "In his own response, when the paradox was put to him in 1942, Moore talks of the analysandum and the analysans being the same concept in a correct analysis, but having different expressions. But he admitted that he had no clear solution to the problem (RC, 666). And if this is so, then it is equally unclear that no definition of ‘good’—whether naturalistic or not—is possible."  (s6 §4)

> "In the former case it is pointless to equate them by means of a definition: this is ‘an obvious circle’; in the latter case it is wrong."  (s1, Frege, RH 319–20 / FR 225–6, tr. Beaney; Frege's 1894 review of Husserl)

> "In using the word to be explained, I either think clearly everything I think when I use the defining expression: we then have the ‘obvious circle’; or the defining expression has a more richly articulated sense, in which case I do not think the same thing in using it as I do in using the word to be explained: the definition is then wrong."  (s1, same passage)

> "What matters to the former is the sense of the words, as well as the ideas which they fail to distinguish from the sense; whereas what matters to the latter is the thing itself: the Bedeutung of the words."  (s1, same passage; "the former" = psychological logicians, "the latter" = mathematicians)

Context: In s6 §4 the paradox is introduced as the general form of Moore's
  open-question argument against defining "good" (Principia Ethica). The
  Frege passage replies to Husserl's objections, in Philosophie der
  Arithmetik (1891), to Frege's Grundlagen definitions; the bibliography
  marks RH (1894) as "[statement of the paradox of analysis]" and lists
  Dummett 1987 "Frege and the Paradox of Analysis", Beaney 2005 "Sinn,
  Bedeutung and the Paradox of Analysis" and Nelson 2008 "Frege and the
  Paradox of Analysis".

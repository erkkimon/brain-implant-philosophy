> "he was responsible for much of the diplomatic, if not also of the financial, work of the reign, though the ingenious method of extortion popularly known as “Morton’s fork” seems really to have been the invention of Richard Fox (q.v.), who succeeded to a large part of Morton’s influence."  ("Morton, John")

> "His financial work brought him a less enviable notoriety, though a curious freak of history has deprived him of the credit which is his due for “Morton’s fork.” The invention of that ingenious dilemma for extorting contributions from poor and rich alike is ascribed as a tradition to Morton by Bacon; but the story is told in greater detail of Fox by Erasmus, who says he had it from Sir Thomas More, a well-informed contemporary authority."  ("Fox, Richard"; line breaks of the printed column joined)

Context: The Morton article states that Morton "succeeded Bourchier as
  archbishop of Canterbury in 1486 and Alcock as lord chancellor in 1487".
  The Fox article continues with Tyndale's "somewhat malicious saying about
  Fox" that "he would sacrifice his father to save his king".

Relied on for: Pollard's assessment that the fork was Fox's invention;
  the dates of Morton's offices.

# Leibniz 1686, Discourse on Metaphysics §II — against those who hold the principles of goodness arbitrary (Montgomery tr.)

> "II. Against those who hold that there is in the works of God no goodness, or that the principles of goodness and beauty are arbitrary."  (§II heading)

> "In saying, therefore, that things are not good according to any standard of goodness, but simply by the will of God, it seems to me that one destroys, without realizing it, all the love of God and all his glory; for why praise him for what he has done, if he would be equally praiseworthy in doing the contrary?"  (§II)

> "Where will be his justice and his wisdom if he has only a certain despotic power, if arbitrary will takes the place of reasonableness, and if in accord with the definition of tyrants, justice consists in that which is pleasing to the most powerful?"  (§II)

> "Besides it seems that every act of willing supposes some reason for the willing and this reason, of course, must precede the act."  (§II)

> "This is why, accordingly, I find so strange those expressions of certain philosophers who say that the eternal truths of metaphysics and Geometry, and consequently the principles of goodness, of justice, and of perfection, are effects only of the will of God. To me it seems that all these follow from his understanding, which does not depend upon his will any more than does his essence."  (§II)

Relied on for: Leibniz's objections to the view that things are good
"simply by the will of God": praise of God emptied, justice as tyranny,
every willing presupposing a reason; his placing of the principles of
goodness in God's understanding rather than will.
Context: §I defines God as "an absolutely perfect being"; §II follows. The
"certain philosophers" who make eternal truths depend on God's will are not
named in §II; Leibniz does not mention the Euthyphro here. Hare (SEP 2024,
§4) reports that Descartes "located the source of moral law ... in God's
will".

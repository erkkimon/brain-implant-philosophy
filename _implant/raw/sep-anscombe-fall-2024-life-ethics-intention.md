## Preamble and §1 Life

> "Gertrude Elizabeth Margaret Anscombe was one of the most important philosophers of the twentieth century."  (preamble)

> "She was born in Limerick, Ireland 18 March 1919 to Allen Wells Anscombe and Gertrude Elizabeth Anscombe (née Thomas)."  (§1)

> "Philippa Foot, with whom she developed a deep friendship her entire life (Mac Cumhaill & Wiseman 2022)."  (§1)

> "Anscombe also became one of Wittgenstein’s good friends and then, after his death in 1951, the chief translator of his work into English."  (§1)

> "In 1956 she publicly opposed Oxford University’s decision to award an honorary degree to Harry Truman, whom she considered a murderer for intentionally killing the civilians of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan."  (§1)

> "Anscombe died in Cambridge on 5 January 2001."  (§1)

## §2 Moral and Political Philosophy

> "Some of the ideas in this pamphlet were later developed in “War and Murder” (WAM) (1972), written for a Christian venue."  (§2)

> "He was a murderer, because “choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder” (MTD, 66)."  (§2)

> "She distinguished choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your end, which is always murder, from choosing to do something else which you know as a statistical certainty will kill the innocent."  (§2)

> "But while pacifism is misguided, so too is the view that the state or its officers may do anything to defend itself."  (§2)

> "the distinction between the intended and the merely foreseen is “absolutely essential” to Christian ethics"  (§2)

> "Each of Anscombe’s three theses is bold; taken together, they are shocking."  (§2)

> "Anscombe notes that despite their many differences, “every one of the best known English academic moral philosophers” thinks that killing the innocent is sometimes right, such as when the foreseeable consequences of not killing them would be catastrophic."  (§2)

> "Anscombe identifies this move as the difference between the Utilitarianism of John Stuart Mill, who rejected the thought that it could ever be apt to calculate whether to kill an innocent person, and the consequentialism (a term she invented) of later English philosophers."  (§2)

> "But Anscombe instead held that the consequentialist view of responsibility ultimately rests on a false view of what it is to act intentionally."  (§2)

> "MMP touched a nerve with the philosophers she targeted. One reason for this was the rather dismissive tone she took in some of her criticisms."  (§2)

> "Consequentialism, as she explains, is not directly a doctrine about what makes an action right – this meaning came about only later in the recent history of philosophy."  (§2)

> "The devout Catholic bomber secures by a “direction of intention” that any shedding of innocent blood that occurs is “accidental.”"  (§2, block quotation of WAM, 59)

> "This teaching strikes Anscombe as quite absurd."  (§2)

> "Her call to give moral philosophy a rest until we better understand the philosophy of psychology was addressed to us all, not just to a subset of moral philosophers."  (§2)

> "she brusquely and perhaps uncharitably attacks Butler, Hume, Kant, Bentham, and Mill"  (§2)

> "Anscombe’s thought here resembled that of her contemporaries Philippa Foot and Iris Murdoch."  (§2)

> "No doubt Anscombe’s claims in MMP inspired some philosophers to develop modern versions of what’s now known as virtue ethics, a theory designed to be a full-fledged alternative to Kantianism, existentialism, utilitarianism, and other totalizing theories."  (§2)

> "Anscombe herself did not advocate this development"  (§2)

> "Crisp 2004 suspected that she was surreptitiously arguing for the superiority of a God-based moral philosophy over any secular moral philosophy, even one like Aristotle’s."  (§2)

> "In a sense, Crisp is right."  (§2)

## §4 Action Theory

> "Anscombe’s Intention is one of the classics of 20th century philosophy."  (§4)

> "contemporary theories of action are often categorized as either Anscombean or Davidsonian"  (§4)

> "Perplexed by defenders of Truman she came to the conclusion that they had failed to understand the nature of his actions, and it was this that led her to write Intention, in which she pointed out that in doing one thing (moving one’s hand) one may intentionally be doing another (directing the death of human beings). (Haldane 2000, 1020)"  (§4, block quotation of Haldane)

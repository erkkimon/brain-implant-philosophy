> "Augustine of Hippo was perhaps the greatest Christian philosopher of Antiquity and certainly the one who exerted the deepest and most lasting influence."  (§0 (preamble))

> "Augustine (Aurelius Augustinus) lived from 13 November 354 to 28 August 430. He was born in Thagaste in Roman Africa (modern Souk Ahras in Algeria)."  (§1)

> "Ca. 373 Augustine became a “hearer” (auditor) of Manicheism, a dualistic religion with Persian origins"  (§1)

> "In 383 he moved to Milan, then the capital of the western half of the Empire, to become a publicly paid professor of rhetoric of the city"  (§1)

> "ended in summer 386, when Augustine converted to ascetic Christianity and gave up both his chair of rhetoric and his further career prospects."  (§1)

> "Augustine was baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387"  (§1)

> "In 391 he was, apparently against his will, ordained a priest in the diocese of the maritime city of Hippo Regius (modern Annaba/Bône in Algeria)."  (§1)

> "About five years later (ca. 396) he succeeded the local bishop."  (§1)

> "Augustine’s life ended when the Vandals besieged Hippo"  (§1)

> "about 100 books, 300 letters, and 600 sermons have survived."  (§2)

> "freedom of choice and human responsibility (De libero arbitrio, begun in 388 and completed perhaps as late as 395)"  (§2)

> "De natura boni (399, a concise anti-Manichean argument for the doctrine that evil is a privation of goodness rather than an independent substance)"  (§2)

> "The Confessiones (ca. 396–400; Toom 2020), probably his most original work, is “philosophy in autobiography” (Mann 2014) rather than an autobiography in a modern sense."  (§2)

> "The monumental apologetic treatise De civitate dei (begun in 412, two years after the sack of Rome, and completed in 426; Meconi 2021) argues that happiness can be found neither in the Roman nor the philosophical tradition but only through membership in the city of God whose founder is Christ."  (§2)

> "At first, he accepted the dualist solution of the Manicheans, which freed God from the responsibility for evil but compromised his omnipotence"  (§7.5)

> "After having encountered the books of the Platonists, Augustine rejected the existence of an evil substance and endorsed the Neoplatonic view (argued, e.g., in Plotinus, Enneads I.8) that evil is in fact unsubstantial and a privation or corruption of goodness."  (§7.5)

> "Therefore, an evil will has no “efficient” but only a “deficient” cause, which is none other than the will’s spontaneous defection from God."  (§7.5)

> "In De libero arbitrio, free will appears as the condition of possibility of moral goodness and hence as a great good itself; but as it is not an absolute good (which is God alone) but only an intermediate one, it is liable to misuse and, hence, also the source of moral evil"  (§7.4)

> "By c. 400 CE, Augustine had come to the conclusion that our ability to make choices was seriously impaired by the fallen condition of humankind and that it made little sense to talk about free will without reference to grace."  (§7.4)

> "War results from sin and is the privileged means of satisfying lust for power (ib. 18.2; 19.7)."  (§8)

> "Nevertheless, Augustine wrote a letter to refute the claim that Christianity advocated a politically impracticable pacifism (Letter 138; Tornau 2019)."  (§8)

> "To be truly just according to Augustinian standards, a war would have to be waged for the benefit of the adversary and without any vindictiveness, in short, out of love of neighbor, which, in a fallen world, seems utopian (Letter 138.14)."  (§8)

> "Wars may however be relatively just if they are defensive and properly declared (cf. Cicero, De officiis 1.36–37) or commanded by a just authority even short of the special case of the wars of the People of Israel that were commanded by God himself (Contra Faustum 22.74–78)."  (§8)

> "Augustine’s most famous anti-skeptical argument is what is commonly called his “cogito-like” argument because it is similar to (and probably inspired) the Cogito of Descartes (Matthews 1992; Menn 1998; Fuchs 2010)."  (§5.1)

> "Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) takes issue with what he considers Augustine’s view of language and language acquisition throughout his analysis of language in the Philosophical Investigations."  (§11)

> "Hannah Arendt (1906–1975) wrote her doctoral dissertation on Augustine’s philosophy of love and blamed him, not uncommonly for her epoch, for degrading love of neighbor to an instrument of personal happiness."  (§11)

> "In the seventeenth century Descartes’ cogito was soon recognized as an Augustinian idea and triggered a refreshed interest in Augustine’s epistemology."  (§11)

> "Augustine’s impact on later philosophy is as enormous as it is ambivalent"  (§11)

> "Thomas Aquinas and others had little interest in Augustine’s Platonism"  (§11)

> "These views, deeply at variance with the ancient philosophical and cultural tradition, provoked however fierce criticism in Augustine’s lifetime and have, again, been vigorously opposed in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries from various (e.g., humanist, liberal, feminist) standpoints."  (§0 (preamble))

> "Martin Luther (1483–1546) agrees with Augustine on the absolute gratuitousness of grace but does not follow the Augustinian (and scholastic) ideal of intellectus fidei and makes faith in the Gospel the decisive condition of salvation."  (§11)

> "The variety of Protestantism that was inaugurated by Jean Calvin (1509–1564) accepts double predestination."  (§11)

> "even, in the case of Nietzsche, outright contempt."  (§11)

> "Augustine tells us that at the age of eighteen Cicero’s (now lost) protreptic dialogue Hortensius enflamed him for philosophy (Confessiones 3.7)"  (§4)

> "conversion was greatly furthered by his Neoplatonic readings (ib. 7.13) as well as by the letters of Paul"  (§4)

> "From this point of view, Socrates is closer to Paul than to Nero, even though his virtue will not bring him happiness, i.e., eternal bliss."  (§7.2)

> "Augustine therefore defines sin as “the will to keep or pursue something unjustly” (De duabus animabus 15)."  (§7.4)

> "Original sin transformed our initial ability not to sin into an inability not to sin; grace can restore ability not to sin in this life and will transform it into inability to sin in the next"  (§7.4)

> "Anselm’s proof of the existence of God develops the argument of De libero arbitrio, bk. 2"  (§11)

> "Augustine therefore rejects Plotinus’ view that prime matter is equivalent to prime evil"  (§7.5)

> "Augustine comes closer than any earlier philosopher to positing will as a faculty of choice that is reducible neither to reason nor to non-rational desire."  (§7.4)

> "is—though of course committed to the truth of biblical revelation"  (preamble)

> "The first of these, written in Cassiciacum in 386/7, deal with traditional topics such as skepticism (Contra Academicos), happiness (De beata vita), providence and evil (De ordine) and the immortality of the soul (Soliloquia, De immortalitate animae)."  (§2)

> "(e.g., Contra Faustum Manichaeum, around 400)"  (§2)

> "Another example of a non-deceptive untruthful statement is what has been called an “altruistic lie” (Fallis 2009, 50; cf. Augustine 1952, 57), such as when a speaker makes an untruthful statement to a hearer whom he believes distrusts him, in order that the hearer will believe something that the speaker believes to be true."  (§1.4)

> "For Simple Non-Deceptionists (Augustine 1952 (cf. Griffiths 2003, 31); Aquinas 1952; Shibles 1985), there is nothing more to lying than making an untruthful statement."  (§2.4)

> "This position is not defended by contemporary philosophers."  (§2.4)

> "These definitions imply that something is permissible iff (if and only if) its negation is not obligatory, impermissible iff its negation is obligatory,"  (§2; the definition is PE p =df ¬OB¬p, "The most prevalent approach is to take OB as primitive")

> "This principle states that whenever something is obligatory, then everything that is a logical consequence is also obligatory (“inherits” that status)."  (§6.3, on OB-RM: if ⊢ p → q then ⊢ OB p → OB q)

> "The earliest and most well-known challenge to RM is Ross’s Paradox (Ross 1941)."  (§6.3)

> "Some defend RM and blame the Ross paradox on an elementary confusion (Føllesdal & Hilpinen 1971) or cite pragmatic features to explain away the puzzle (Castañeda 1981): no one would, e.g., merely utter the statement that John ought to mail or burn the letter, knowing that in fact John ought to just mail the letter."  (§6.3)

> "However, since these paradoxes all at least appear to depend on OB-RM, a natural solution to the problems is to undercut the paradoxes by rejecting OB-RM itself."  (§6.3; LaTeX \(\OB\)-RM rendered OB-RM)

> "This sentence may be used by an authority to provide permission on the spot or it may be used by a passerby to report on an already existing norm (e.g., a standing municipal regulation)."  (§7, on "You may park here for one hour")

> "See Lemmon 1962b; Kamp 1974, 1979."  (note 79, attached to the view that an authority granting a permission "thereby makes what it said true")

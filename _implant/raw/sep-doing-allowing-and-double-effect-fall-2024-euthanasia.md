# SEP "Doing vs. Allowing Harm" (Woollard) §1 and "Doctrine of Double Effect" (McIntyre) §§2, 5 — Rachels's cases and their critics; pain relief and terminal sedation

## A. Doing vs. Allowing Harm

Source: Fiona Woollard, "Doing vs. Allowing Harm", The Stanford
  Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri
  Nodelman (eds.); first published 2002-05-14, substantive revision
  2021-07-07 ("Copyright © 2021 by Fiona Woollard"). Preamble and §1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/doing-allowing/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive page fetched with curl, text extracted and read)

> "If not, there should be no moral objection to active euthanasia in circumstances where passive euthanasia is permissible"  (preamble)

> "James Rachels (1975) provides a classic example of the contrast strategy."  (§1)

> "Rachels invites us to agree that Smith’s behavior is no worse than Jones’s. He then concludes that killing per se is no worse than letting die per se, and that if typical killings are worse than typical lettings die that must be because of other factors."  (§1)

> "Rachels’ argument depends on two claims: (1) the two cases are exactly alike except that one is a killing and the other a letting die; (2) that there is no moral difference between the cases. Both these claims have been challenged."  (§1)

> "Scott Hills (2018) challenges (1). He argues that the cases are not properly equalised because Jones has the ability to either kill his cousin or to let him die, while Smith does not have the option to merely let his cousin die."  (§1)

> "Kamm claims that it would be permissible to kill Smith to bring the child back to life but impermissible to kill Jones for this reason (Kamm 2007, 17)."  (§1)

> "Shelly Kagan argues that this inference assumes that “if a factor has genuine moral relevance, then for any pair of cases, where the given factor varies while others are held constant, the cases in that pair will differ in moral status” (Kagan 1988)."  (§1)

Bibliography entry: "Rachels, James, 1975, “Active and Passive Euthanasia,” New England Journal of Medicine, 292: 78–86." (Crossref gives pp. 78–80; see `rachels-1975-active-and-passive-euthanasia.md`.)

## B. Doctrine of Double Effect

Source: Alison McIntyre, "Doctrine of Double Effect", The Stanford
  Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri
  Nodelman (eds.); first published 2004-07-28, substantive revision
  2023-07-17 ("Copyright © 2023 by Alison McIntyre"). §2 (examples), §5.1,
  §5.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/double-effect/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive page fetched with curl, text extracted and read)

> "A doctor who intends to hasten the death of a terminally ill patient by injecting a large dose of morphine would act impermissibly because he intends to bring about the patient’s death. However, a doctor who intended to relieve the patient’s pain with that same dose and merely foresaw the hastening of the patient’s death would act permissibly."  (examples, "Euthanasia vs Pain relief that hastens death")

> "Double effect is cited as a justification for the view that it is permissible to administer opioid drugs to relieve the suffering of a terminally ill patient, foreseeing that the hastening of death will occur as a side effect."  (§5.1)

> "If one believes that this is permissible, then double effect is not needed to explain its permissibility."  (§5.1)

> "Physicians and researchers have insisted repeatedly that it is a myth that opioids administered for pain relief can be expected to hasten death (Sykes and Thorns, 2003 provide a review of a large number of studies supporting this claim)."  (§5.1)

> "In a U.S. Supreme Court decision that rejected arguments for permitting patients with terminal illness to request assistance from a physician in ending their lives (Vacco et al. v. Quill et al., 117 S.Ct. 2293 (1997), Chief Justice William Rehnquist, writing for the majority, invoked double effect not only as a justification for the administration of pain-relieving drugs that might hasten death but also as a justification for the practice known as terminal sedation or continuous sedation."  (§5.2)

> "Timothy E. Quill, M.D., Rebecca Dresser, J.D, and Dan W. Brock, Ph.D. argue that double effect, if applied consistently, would rule out the practice of terminal sedation."  (§5.2)

> "“Unlike the use of high-dose opioids to relieve pain, with death as a possible but undesired side effect, terminal sedation inevitably causes death, which in many cases is what the patient desires."  (§5.2, quoting Quill, Dresser and Brock 1997)

> "The belief that doctors can cite double effect reasoning to justify actions that hasten death tends to obscure rather than clarify these important issues in palliative care."  (§5.2, McIntyre's assessment)

Context: Woollard's §1 surveys "the contrast strategy" for testing the
  doing/allowing distinction; Kagan's objection concerns the "Additive
  Assumption". McIntyre's §5 argues in her own voice that the
  end-of-life applications of double effect rest on three assumptions,
  the second of which she calls "false"; §6 discusses whether double
  effect is one principle or "many loosely related exceptions".

Chignell, "The Ethics of Belief":

> "The central question in the debate is whether there are norms of some sort governing our habits of belief-formation, belief-maintenance, and belief-relinquishment."  (preamble)

> "Are they instrumental norms grounded in contingent ends that we set for ourselves? Are they categorical norms grounded in ends set for us by the very nature of our intellectual or moral capacities?"  (preamble)

> "(Clifford's Principle) “It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence.”"  (§1)

Murphy, "Concepts of Disease and Health":

> "Boorse (1977, 1997) insists that the notion of normality in biomedical concepts is statistical — how things usually are in a reference class, but this view faces the problem of specifying the reference classes in an informative way."  (§4.3)

> "As Ereshefsky (2009) puts it, Boorse assumes that statistical normality coincides with the kind of normality that medicine cares about, but this looks wrong."  (§4.3)

> "Statistically, a textbook heart, for example, may be very rare indeed."  (§4.4)

Relied on for: the epistemic sense of "norm" (norms of belief, Clifford's
principle); the statistical sense of "norm/normal" (Boorse) and its
critics (Ereshefsky, Wachbroit), as Murphy reports them.
Context: Chignell's §1 reports Clifford's 1877 shipowner story and James's
reply; Murphy's §4.3 contrasts Boorse's statistical normality with
Wachbroit's idealised normality ("Wachbroit (1994, 588) argues
convincingly ..." is Murphy's assessment).

> "On the standard account, defenders of altruism insist that, sometimes at least, people are motivated by an ultimate desire for the well-being of another person, while defenders of egoism maintain that all ultimate desires are self-interested."  (Source 1, §2)

> "Batson (1991: 58) labels this response “empathy” which he characterizes as “an other-oriented emotional reaction to seeing someone suffer”, and calls the traditional idea that empathy leads to altruism the empathy-altruism hypothesis."  (Source 1, §5)

> "Over and over again, in well designed and carefully conducted experiments, Batson and his associates have produced results which are clearly compatible with the predictions of the empathy-altruism hypothesis, as set out in Table 5, and clearly incompatible with the predictions of the aversive-arousal reduction hypothesis, as set out in Table 4."  (Source 1, §5.2; the authors' assessment)

> "they report three studies indicating that empathy, though it does occur, is not playing any causal role in the process that leads to helping."  (Source 1, §5.3; "they" are Cialdini et al.; the entry's sentence prints the year as "(1977)" here, while its bibliography and earlier text give 1997)

> "pointed out a number of methodological problems."  (Source 1, §5.3; the subject is "Batson et al. (1997)")

> "When they conducted a pair of experiments that avoided these methodological problems, the role of empathy in producing helping behavior was clearly evident."  (Source 1, §5.3)

> "Their findings were “unambiguously supportive” (2018: 504) of the link between empathy and helping behavior."  (Source 1, §5.3; McAuliffe et al. 2018, 680 participants, pre-registered)

> "Pending new evidence or a plausible new egoistic explanation of the existing evidence, we seem forced to accept this improbable hypothesis as true."  (Source 1, §5.4, quoting Batson 2011: 160)

> "Batson’s research program is compelling, and he certainly has shown that the empathy-altruism hypothesis is “in the hunt”, but his findings are not conclusive."  (Source 1, §5.4; the authors' assessment)

> "There is a family of egoistic hypotheses invoking beliefs in supernatural punishments—or supernatural rewards—that still needs to be ruled out before we accept the “improbable hypothesis” as true."  (Source 1, §5.4; the authors' "divine punishment hypothesis")

> "The term is used as the contrary of “self-interested” or “selfish” or “egoistic”—words applied to behavior that is motivated solely by the desire to benefit oneself."  (Source 2, preamble)

> "A single motive cannot be characterized in both ways; but a single act can be undertaken from both motives."  (Source 2, §1.1)

> "According to a doctrine called “psychological egoism”, all human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest."  (Source 2, §2.1)

> "That strategy is often attributed to the Greek and Roman philosophers of antiquity—Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, and the Epicureans (see Annas 1993)."  (Source 2, §4; "That strategy" makes "self-interested motivation fundamental", holding "that we should be altruistic because it is in our interest to be so moved")

Relied on for: the egoism/altruism contrast in psychology; Batson's
hypothesis and findings, reported as his and his colleagues'; Cialdini et
al.'s challenge and Batson et al.'s reply; the replication by McAuliffe et
al.; Doris, Stich and Walmsley's own assessment; Kraut's definition of
altruism as the contrary of self-interested, mixed motives, and the
eudaimonist strategy.
Context: Source 1 §5 tests the empathy-altruism hypothesis against
egoistic alternatives (social punishment, aversive-arousal reduction,
self-other merging); §5.3 reports Aron et al. and Lerner on self-other
merging and May (2011b) against it. Source 2 §4 contrasts the ancient
strategy with a modern "impartial and impersonal" approach.

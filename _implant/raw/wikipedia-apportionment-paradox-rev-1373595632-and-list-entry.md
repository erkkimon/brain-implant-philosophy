> "Apportionment paradox: Some systems of apportioning representation can have unintuitive results due to rounding"  (List of paradoxes, "Decision theory")

> "Alabama paradox: Increasing the total number of seats might shrink one bloc's seats."  (List, sub-entry)

> "New states paradox: Adding a new state or voting bloc might increase the number of votes of another."  (List, sub-entry)

> "Population paradox: A fast-growing state can lose votes to a slow-growing state."  (List, sub-entry)

> "An apportionment paradox is a situation where an apportionment—a rule for dividing discrete objects according to some proportional relationship—produces results that violate notions of common sense or fairness."  (article lead)

> "Certain quantities, like milk, can be divided in any proportion whatsoever; others, such as horses, cannot—only whole numbers will do. In the latter case, there is an inherent tension between the desire to obey the rule of proportion as closely as possible and the constraint restricting the size of each portion to discrete values."  (lead)

> "The method for apportionment used during this period, originally put forth by Alexander Hamilton, but vetoed by George Washington and not adopted until 1852, was as follows:"  ("Alabama paradox"; cites Stein 2008, p. 228, not read here)

> "After the 1880 census, C. W. Seaton, chief clerk of the United States Census Bureau, computed apportionments for all House sizes between 275 and 350, and discovered that Alabama would get eight seats with a House size of 299 but only seven with a House size of 300."  ("Alabama paradox"; cites Stein 2008, pp. 228–231, not read here)

> "First, the fair share of each state is computed, i.e. the proportional share of seats that each state would get if fractional values were allowed."  ("Alabama paradox", the Hamilton method, step 1)

> "Second, each state receives as many seats as the whole number portion of its fair share."  (step 2)

> "Third, any state whose fair share is less than one receives one seat, regardless of population, as required by the United States Constitution."  (step 3)

> "Fourth, any remaining seats are distributed, one each, to the states whose fair shares have the highest fractional parts."  (step 4; section cites Stein 2008, p. 228, not read here)

> "The following is a simplified example (following the largest remainder method) with three states and 10 seats and 11 seats."  ("Alabama paradox"; table: populations A 6, B 6, C 2; fair shares with 10 seats 4.286, 4.286, 1.429 giving seats 4, 4, 2; with 11 seats 4.714, 4.714, 1.571 giving 5, 5, 1)

> "Observe that state C's share decreases from 2 to 1 with the added seat."  ("Alabama paradox")

> "The Alabama paradox gave rise to the axiom known as house monotonicity, which says that, when the house size increases, the allocations of all states should weakly increase."  ("Alabama paradox")

> "In 1900, Virginia lost a seat to Maine, even though Virginia's population was growing more rapidly."  ("Population paradox"; cites Stein 2008, pp. 231–232, not read here)

> "However, divisor methods such as the current method do not."  ("Population paradox"; tagged "citation needed", March 2024)

> "The New State paradox gave rise to the axiom known as coherence, which says that, whenever an apportionment rule is applied to a subset of the states, with the subset of seats allocated to them, the outcome should be the same as in the overall solution for all states."  ("New states paradox"; no reference given for the sentence)

> "This resulted in a crisis of confidence in the apportionment rules procedure as well as a bitter debate within the House, as it was unclear which of these apportionments—the one calculated from scratch, or the one calculated after the previous Census—should be considered the "correct" one."  ("New states paradox"; cites Stein 2008, pp. 232–233, and Caulfield 2010)

> "In 1982, two mathematicians, Michel Balinski and Peyton Young, proved that any method of apportionment that does not violate the quota rule will result in paradoxes whenever there are four or more parties (or states, regions, etc.)."  ("Balinski–Young theorem"; cites Fair Representation 1982 and 2001)

> "It is of note that any method of apportionment free of the Population Paradox will always be free of Alabama Paradox. The converse is not true, however."  ("Balinski–Young theorem")

> "In general, the response from mathematicians has been to abandon the quota rule as the less-important property, accepting that apportionment errors may sometimes slightly exceed one seat."  ("Balinski–Young theorem"; no reference given for the sentence)

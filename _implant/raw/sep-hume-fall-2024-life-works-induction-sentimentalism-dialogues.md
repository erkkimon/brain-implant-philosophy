> "Generally regarded as one of the most important philosophers to write in English, David Hume (1711–1776) was also well known in his own time as an historian and essayist."  (preamble)

> "Although Hume’s more conservative contemporaries denounced his writings as works of scepticism and atheism, his influence is evident in the moral philosophy and economic writings of his close friend Adam Smith."  (preamble)

> "Kant reported that Hume’s work woke him from his “dogmatic slumbers” (Prolegomena, Introduction) and Jeremy Bentham remarked that reading Hume “caused the scales to fall” from his eyes (“A Fragment on Government”, chapter 1, paragraph 36, footnote 2)."  (preamble)

> "Today, philosophers recognize Hume as a thoroughgoing exponent of philosophical naturalism, as a precursor of contemporary cognitive science, and as the inspiration for several of the most significant types of ethical theory developed in contemporary moral philosophy."  (preamble)

> "Born in Edinburgh, Hume spent his childhood at Ninewells, his family’s modest estate in the border lowlands."  (§1)

> "In 1734, when he was only 23, he began writing A Treatise of Human Nature."  (§1)

> "Book I, “Of the Understanding”, and Book II, “Of the Passions”, appeared anonymously in 1739. The next year saw the publication of Book III, “Of Morals”, as well as his anonymous “Abstract” of Books I and II."  (§1)

> "When he applied for the Chair of Ethics and Pneumatical (“Mental”) Philosophy at Edinburgh in 1745, his reputation provoked vocal and ultimately successful opposition. Six years later, he stood for the Chair of Logic at Glasgow, only to be turned down again. Hume never held an academic post."  (§1)

> "In 1748, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding appeared, covering the central ideas of Book I of the Treatise and his discussion of liberty and necessity from Book II."  (§1)

> "In 1751, he published An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals, a “recasting” of Book III of the Treatise, which he described as “incomparably the best” of all his work (MOL 10)."  (§1)

> "Published in six volumes between 1754 and 1762, his History was a bestseller well into the next century, giving him the financial independence he had long sought."  (§1)

> "In 1775, Hume was diagnosed with intestinal cancer. Before his death in 1776, he arranged for the posthumous publication of his most controversial work, the Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, and composed a brief autobiography, “My Own Life”."  (§1)

> "Hume’s apparent disavowal of the Treatise and his regard for the Enquiries raise a question about how we should read his work."  (§2)

> "It is not unreasonable to conclude that Hume’s recasting of the Treatise was designed to address this issue, which suggests that we might understand him best by reading both works, despite their differences, together."  (§2)

> "Any attempt to infer (2) from (1) by a probable inference will be viciously circular—it will involve supposing what we are trying to prove."  (§5.1)

> "Hume calls his constructive account of causal inference a “sceptical solution” to the “sceptical doubts” he raised in the critical phase of his argument."  (§5.2)

> "Hume follows his sentimentalist predecessor, Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746), in building his moral theory around the idea of a spectator who approves or disapproves of people’s character traits and motives."  (§7)

> "Hume has two sets of opponents: the self-love theorists and the moral rationalists. He became the most famous proponent of sentimentalism."  (§7)

> "Clarke, Hume’s central rationalist opponent, appeals to reason to explain almost every aspect of morality."  (§7.1)

> "One way of understanding Hume’s project is to see it as an attempt to naturalize Hutcheson’s moral sense theory."  (§7.1)

> "Hume’s greatest achievement in the philosophy of religion is the Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, which is generally regarded as one of the most important and influential contributions to this area of philosophy."  (§8)

> "Although all three characters say very Humean things at one time or another, Philo’s views are consistently the closest to Hume’s."  (§8.1)

> "Some take Philo—and, by implication, Hume—to be outing himself as a closet theist. Others conclude that, since he holds all the cards at this point, he can afford to be conciliatory."  (§8.4)

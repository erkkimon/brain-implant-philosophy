# Hume 1779 — Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, Parts X–XI: "Epicurus's old questions"

Source: David Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779), Part X
  (speaker Philo) and Part XI, Project Gutenberg eBook #4583 (release
  2003-10-01, updated 2020-12-28).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4583/pg4583.txt
  (public domain). Russell (SEP, "Hume on Religion", Fall 2024, §5) cites
  the old-questions passage as "D, 10.25/198".
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain text fetched with curl; quotations copied
  from it; the Gutenberg text prints the part headings as "PART 10",
  "PART 11")

> "EPICURUS's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"  (Part X, Philo)

> "Why is there any misery at all in the world? Not by chance surely. From some cause then. Is it from the intention of the Deity? But he is perfectly benevolent. Is it contrary to his intention? But he is almighty. Nothing can shake the solidity of this reasoning, so short, so clear, so decisive; except we assert, that these subjects exceed all human capacity, and that our common measures of truth and falsehood are not applicable to them"  (Part X, Philo)

> "I will allow, that pain or misery in man is compatible with infinite power and goodness in the Deity, even in your sense of these attributes: What are you advanced by all these concessions? A mere possible compatibility is not sufficient. You must prove these pure, unmixed, and uncontrollable attributes from the present mixed and confused phenomena, and from these alone."  (Part X, Philo)

> "There may four hypotheses be framed concerning the first causes of the universe: that they are endowed with perfect goodness; that they have perfect malice; that they are opposite, and have both goodness and malice; that they have neither goodness nor malice. Mixed phenomena can never prove the two former unmixed principles; and the uniformity and steadiness of general laws seem to oppose the third. The fourth, therefore, seems by far the most probable."  (Part XI, Philo)

Relied on for: Hume's three-question restatement attributed by Philo to
Epicurus; Philo's concession that misery is "compatible" with the divine
attributes, and his shift to inferring the attributes from the phenomena
(the move the page records next to the evidential problem); the four
hypotheses of Part XI, which Tooley (SEP §3.3) reads as an indirect
inductive argument.
Context: the Dialogues are a dialogue among Cleanthes, Demea and Philo;
the words are Philo's, and which speaker (if any) voices Hume's own view
is not settled by the text. The three questions follow Philo's catalogue
of human and animal misery; Demea and Cleanthes reply later in Part X.

## Shoemaker §2.5 "The Identity Doesn't Matter View"

> "Derek Parfit was among the first contemporary theorists to explore the relation between identity and ethics explicitly, first in his seminal early 1970s articles, “Personal Identity” and especially “Later Selves and Moral Principles,” and then in his restatement and development of the view in Part III of his 1984 book Reasons and Persons (from which the present exposition is taken)."  (§2.5)

> "He is a “reductionist,” according to which the facts about persons and personal identity consist in more particular facts about brains, bodies, and series of interrelated mental and physical events (Parfit 1984, 210–211)."  (§2.5)

> "By way of explanation, consider the case Parfit uses in support of his claim that identity is not what matters: fission (Ibid., 254–255)."  (§2.5)

> "Everything that matters in ordinary survival (or nearly everything), therefore, is preserved in fission, despite the fact that the identity relation is not."  (§2.5)

> "In Reasons and Persons, Parfit is officially agnostic on the proper approach (he claims that arguments for both stances are defensible, yet also can be defensibly denied; see Parfit 1984, 311–312)."  (§2.5)

> "He calls it the Moderate Claim (Parfit 1984, 311)."  (§2.5)

> "Thus, I may be justified in caring much less about my MLS than about my tomorrow's self."  (§2.5; MLS = "Much Later Self")

> "Great imprudence like this, in other words, would be immoral (Parfit 1984, 318–320)."  (§2.5)

> "Parfit's theory has often been called “revisionary,” in part because of moves like this one (see, e.g., Rovane 1998, 11; Martin 1998, 15)."  (§2.5)

> "This suggests, then, that Parfit's view is less revisionary than revelatory:"  (§2.5; Shoemaker's own suggestion)

> "While there are plausible alternative reactions to fission that maintain the importance of the identity relation (see, e.g., Lewis 1976, Sider 2001a) – and such views will be explored later – for now it is important to see what Parfit's version would mean, if anything, for our practical concerns."  (§2.5)

> "Parfit asks the first-person question, “What matters to me in fission?”"  (§2.5, contrasting Schechtman's Anthropological View)

> "Schechtman, on the other hand, asks the third-person question, about how such fission products would be treated, which allows for their being creatures who are indeed different from us."  (§2.5)

## Shoemaker §1 and the ethics of separateness

> "(And Reid's objection in particular anticipates Derek Parfit's “Extreme Claim,” to be discussed later.)"  (§1)

> "The objection he is concerned to refute is Rawls' famous “separateness of persons” charge, the contention that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons, because it controversially jettisons interpersonal distributive principles in exactly the way we uncontroversially jettison them intrapersonally (Rawls 1971, 22–27)."  (§ on personal identity and moral theory; "he" is Parfit)

> "The success of arguments for this conclusion actually depends on the specific version of reductionism being advanced."  (same section; Shoemaker cites Jeske 1993 and Brink 1997a for the replies)

> "It is the non-identity problem, first discussed in detail in Parfit 1984, 351–379."  (§ on practical applications)

## Olson §§1, 5

> "Identity itself has no practical importance. (Sosa (1990) and Merricks (2022) argue for the importance of identity; Parfit (1971, 1984: 215, 1995) and Martin (1998) argue against.)"  (§1)

> "Fission is death. (Shoemaker 1984: 85, Parfit 1984: 207; 2012: 6f., Unger 1990: 265)."  (§5, stating the "non-branching view")

> "This leads Parfit and others to say that that is precisely what we ought to prefer. We have no reason to want to continue existing, at least for its own sake."  (§5)

> "(Lewis 1976 and Parfit 1976 debate whether the multiple-occupancy view can preserve the conviction that identity is what matters practically.)"  (§5)

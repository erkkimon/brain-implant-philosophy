# Aristotle, De Caelo II.13 (295b) — Anaximander's indifference argument and the man equally hungry and thirsty

> "but there are some, Anaximander, for instance, among the ancients, who say that the earth keeps its place because of its indifference."  (II.13; 295b10–16 per Melamed & Lin)

> "Motion upward and downward and sideways were all, they thought, equally inappropriate to that which is set at the centre and indifferently related to every extreme point; and to move in contrary directions at the same time was impossible: so it must needs remain still. This view is ingenious but not true."  (II.13)

> "But suppose that the centre is not its place, and that the reason of its remaining there is this necessity of indifference-on the analogy of the hair which, it is said, however great the tension, will not break under it, if it be evenly distributed, or of the men who, though exceedingly hungry and thirsty, and both equally, yet being equidistant from food and drink, is therefore bound to stay where he is-even so, it still remains to explain why fire stays at the extremities."  (II.13; 295b32 per Zupko)

Relied on for: the man equally hungry and thirsty, equidistant from food
and drink, as an analogy that Aristotle reports in stating the
"indifference" account of why the earth stays at the centre; Aristotle's
own verdict on that account ("ingenious but not true"), attributed.
Context: Aristotle's objections to the indifference theory: it would make
fire rest at the centre too; the earth moves to the centre, not only
rests there; and a body indifferently related to every point would move
"not as a mass but in fragments". The hungry man (grammar "the men who
... is" as in the Stocks text) is one of two analogies, with the evenly
stretched hair. The passage concerns physical equilibrium, not choice.

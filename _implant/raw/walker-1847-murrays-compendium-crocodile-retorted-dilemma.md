# Walker 1847 (Murray's Compendium of Logic) — the crocodile as a retorted dilemma

Source: Richard Murray, Compendium of Logic, with commentary by John
  Walker, new edition (London: Longman, Brown, Green, and Longmans; Dublin: Cumming and Ferguson, 1847), Part III ch. VIII
  (dilemma) and Walker's commentary on it, p. 159 (running head).
Original: https://archive.org/details/murrayscompendi00murrgoog (Google
  scan; public domain). Excerpts only.
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (archive.org OCR text murrayscompendi00murrgoog_djvu.txt
  read; quotations copied from it, line-break hyphens joined, one OCR
  character noted)

> "A dilemma becomes invalid, 1. if all the parts together do not contain the whole ; for then the major of the disjunctive syllogism will be false ; 2. if the remotion of any part be not certain ; for then the simple syllogism fails, which was to confirm the remotion of any part; 3. if it can be retorted;"  (Murray's text, ch. VIII)

> "for an argument, which proves things contradictory, proves nothing, as in the following instance."  (Murray, introducing Protagoras and Euathlus)

> "Chrysippus' Sophism of the Crocodile"  (Walker's commentary, p. 159; OCR prints a stray "^" after "Crocodile")

> "which Lucian laughs at in his Vit. Auct., and the point of which seems to have escaped the editors, was probably a similar instance of a dilemma that might be retorted."  (Walker, p. 159)

> "A crocodile promised to restore a child which he had seized, if the father should tell him truly whether he would restore it or not."  (Walker, p. 159)

> "and then proceeds to argue that, if he had told truth, he must have his child, according to the crocodile's promise ; and if he had not told truth, the crocodile must restore his child, as it was false that he would not restore it."  (Walker, p. 159, of the father, after he replies that the child will not be restored)

> "It is easy to see how the crocodile might retort this dilemma, and prove that in either case he must keep the child. If I mistake not, a similarly ludicrous instance may be found in Don Quixote."  (Walker, p. 159)

Relied on for: Walker's reading of the crocodile as a retortable dilemma
parallel to Protagoras and Euathlus; the father's dilemma and the
crocodile's retort as he states them; Murray's rule that a retortable
dilemma "proves nothing".
Context: Murray's ch. VIII on the dilemma lists three ways a dilemma is
invalid and illustrates the third with Protagoras and Euathlus; Walker's
commentary adds the crocodile. Walker says the father fears that a guess of
restoration would be falsified by the crocodile keeping the child. Cited in
Wikipedia's "Crocodile dilemma" as "Murray's Compendium of logic", p. 159.

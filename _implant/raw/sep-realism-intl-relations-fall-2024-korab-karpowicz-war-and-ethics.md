# SEP "Political Realism in International Relations" (Korab-Karpowicz) — realism's scepticism about ethics in war, the Melian Dialogue, classical realism

> "The negative side of the realists’ emphasis on power and self-interest is often their skepticism regarding the relevance of ethical norms to relations among states. National politics is the realm of authority and law, whereas international politics, they sometimes claim, is a sphere without justice, characterized by active or potential conflict among states, where ethical standards do not apply."  (preamble)

> "Not all realists, however, deny the presence of prescriptive ethics in international relations."  (preamble)

> "The classical realists do not reject the possibility of moral judgment in international politics. Rather, they are critical of moralism—abstract moral discourse that does not take into account political realities."  (preamble)

> "Among its founding fathers, Thucydides, Machiavelli and Hobbes are the names most usually mentioned."  (preamble)

> "They say that considerations of right and wrong have “never turned people aside from the opportunities of aggrandizement offered by superior strength” (chap. 1 par. 76)."  (§1.1.1, the Athenians in Thucydides)

> "In the words of the Athenian envoys at Melos, without any common authority that can enforce order, “the independent states survive [only] when they are powerful” (5.97)."  (§1.1.1)

> "His ideas concerning righteousness in war were carried further in the writings of the Christian thinkers St. Augustine and St. Thomas Aquinas."  (§1.2, of Cicero)

> "Realism is thus more than a static, amoral theory, and cannot be accommodated solely within a positivist interpretation of international relations."  (§4)

Relied on for: the realist position on the just-war page, in its radical
  and classical forms, with Thucydides' Athenians as its classical
  statement; the Cicero–Augustine–Aquinas line.
Context: the entry is about international-relations theory generally, not
  just war; §1.1.2 presents the Melian Dialogue as "the classic debate
  between the idealist and realist views" and says Thucydides' own position
  "cannot be deduced" from the Athenians' speeches alone; §1.2 presents
  Machiavelli as challenging the moral tradition; the concluding section is
  the author's view that realism is "a practical and evolving theory".

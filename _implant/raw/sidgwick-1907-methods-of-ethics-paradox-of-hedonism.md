# Sidgwick 1907 — The Methods of Ethics: the "fundamental paradox of Hedonism"

Source: Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, 7th edition (London:
  Macmillan, 1907; first edition 1874), Book I, chapter iv ("Pleasure and
  Desire"), §2; Book II ("Egoistic Hedonism"), chapter iii ("Empirical
  Hedonism—Continued"), §2.
Original: Project Gutenberg eBook #46743 (released 2014-09-01), transcribed
  from the 7th edition: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46743
  (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (plain text fetched from
  https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46743/pg46743.txt; chapter and
  section locators read from the headings in the text; the Gutenberg text
  carries no page numbers)

> "but whether there are no desires and aversions which have not pleasures and pains for their objects--no conscious impulses to produce or avert results other than the agent’s own feelings."  (I.iv.2, stating "the question at issue")

> "A man who maintains throughout an epicurean mood, keeping his main conscious aim perpetually fixed on his own pleasure, does not catch the full spirit of the chase; his eagerness never gets just the sharpness of edge which imparts to the pleasure its highest zest."  (I.iv.2)

> "Here comes into view what we may call the fundamental paradox of Hedonism, that the impulse towards pleasure, if too predominant, defeats its own aim."  (I.iv.2)

> "This effect is not visible, or at any rate is scarcely visible, in the case of passive sensual pleasures."  (I.iv.2)

> "In all kinds of Art, again, the exercise of the creative faculty is attended by intense and exquisite pleasures: but it would seem that in order to get them, one must forget them"  (I.iv.2)

> "We could not pursue pleasure at all, unless we had desires for something else than pleasure; for pleasure consists in the satisfaction of just these “disinterested” impulses."  (I.iv.2, Sidgwick's statement of Butler's view)

> "Butler has certainly over-stated his case, so far as my own experience goes; for many pleasures,--especially those of sight, hearing and smell, together with many emotional pleasures,--occur to me without any perceptible relation to previous desires,"  (I.iv.2; footnote marker [49] omitted)

> "it would certainly seem that the systematic pursuit of pleasure is in some danger of defeating itself: it is therefore important to consider carefully how far this is really the case."  (II.iii.2, stating the objection he answers)

> "In a previous chapter I have, after Butler, laid stress on the difference between impulses that are, strictly speaking, directed towards pleasure, and ‘extra-regarding’ impulses which do not aim at pleasure"  (II.iii.2; footnote marker [120], "Book i. chap. iv.", omitted)

> "I should not, however, infer from this that the pursuit of pleasure is necessarily self-defeating and futile; but merely that the principle of Egoistic Hedonism, when applied with a due knowledge of the laws of human nature, is practically self-limiting;"  (II.iii.2)

> "a rational method of attaining the end at which it aims requires that we should to some extent put it out of sight and not directly aim at it."  (II.iii.2)

> "I have before spoken of this conclusion as the ‘Fundamental Paradox of Egoistic Hedonism’; but though it presents itself as a paradox, there does not seem to be any difficulty in its practical realisation, when once the danger indicated is clearly seen."  (II.iii.2)

> "it might seem difficult to execute the practical paradox of attaining pleasure by aiming at something else."  (II.iii.2)

> "For the reaction of our activities upon our emotional nature is such that we may commonly bring ourselves to take an interest in any end by concentrating our efforts upon its attainment."  (II.iii.2)

Relied on for: the name "fundamental paradox of Hedonism" and its first
  statement (I.iv.2); Sidgwick's restriction to active rather than passive
  sensual pleasures; his report and qualification of Butler; his reply that
  Egoistic Hedonism is "practically self-limiting" rather than
  self-defeating (II.iii.2).
Context: I.iv is titled "Pleasure and Desire"; the Butler passage follows
  Sidgwick's remark that Butler "regards the exact opposite of what Mill
  thinks so obvious" as "a necessary truth"; the chase, business, study and art are his examples of
  "pleasures of pursuit". II.iii.2 answers the objection (which he
  attributes to T. H. Green's *Prolegomena to Ethics*) that the systematic
  pursuit of pleasure defeats itself; the second paragraph goes on to say
  that every agent already has impulses other than the desire for pleasure.

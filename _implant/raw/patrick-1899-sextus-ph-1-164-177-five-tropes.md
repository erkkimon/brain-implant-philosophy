# Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.164–177 — "The Five Tropes" (M. M. Patrick tr., 1899)

Source: Mary Mills Patrick, Sextus Empiricus and Greek Scepticism
  (Cambridge: Deighton Bell & Co.; London: George Bell & Sons, 1899), a
  Bern doctoral thesis (1897) "accompanied by a Translation from the Greek
  of the First Book of the 'Pyrrhonic Sketches' by Sextus Empiricus";
  translation ch. XV "The Five Tropes", sections 164–177 (section numbers
  printed in the margin). Patrick states she used "the Greek text of
  Immanuel Bekker, Berlin, 1842".
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/17556 (Project Gutenberg
  eBook #17556, public domain). Excerpts only.
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain-text file pg17556.txt read; quotations

  breaks).

> "The later Sceptics, however, teach the following five Tropes of [Greek: epoche]: first, the one based upon contradiction; second, the _regressus in infinitum_; third, relation; fourth, the hypothetical; fifth, the _circulus in probando_."  (PH I.164)

> "The one based upon the _regressus in infinitum_ is that in which we say that the proof brought forward for the thing set before us calls for another proof, and that one another, and so on to infinity, so that, not having anything from which to begin the reasoning, the suspension of judgment follows."  (I.166)

> "The one based upon hypothesis is illustrated by the Dogmatics, when in the _regressus in infinitum_ they begin from something that they do not found on reason, but which they simply take for granted without proof."  (I.168)

> "The Trope, _circulus in probando_, arises when the thing which ought to prove the thing sought for, needs to be sustained by the thing sought for, and as we are unable to take the one for the proof of the other, we suspend our judgment in regard to both."  (I.169)

> "For if the one who makes the hypothesis is worthy of confidence, we should in every case be no less worthy of confidence in making a contrary hypothesis."  (I.173)

> "If it is absurd to assume the thing questioned, it is also absurd to assume that upon which it rests."  (I.174)

> "Therefore the intellectual also is brought under the five Tropes, and consequently it is necessary to suspend the judgment altogether with regard to every thing that is brought before us."  (I.177)

> "They set them forth, not to throw out the ten Tropes, but in order to put to shame the audacity of the Dogmatics in a variety of ways, by these Tropes as well as by those."  (I.177)

From Patrick's own commentary (ch. on the Tropes, same eBook):

> "Sextus nowhere mentions the author of these Tropes. Diogenes, however, attributes them to Agrippa, a man of whom we know nothing except his mention of him."

Relied on for: Sextus' list and definitions of the five modes in a
public-domain translation; the claim of universal application (I.169–177);
the reply to hypothesis by a contrary hypothesis (I.173); Sextus' remark
that the five do not "throw out" the ten (I.177), which differs from
Hicks's note on Diogenes (see
`diogenes-laertius-9-88-89-five-modes-of-agrippa-hicks.md`); Patrick's
note that Sextus does not name Agrippa.

Context: I.165 (contradiction/disagreement) and I.167 (relation) are the
two non-formal modes; I.170–177 apply the modes to "sensible" and
"intellectual" objects in turn. Patrick's "contradiction" renders the
Greek diaphōnia, which other translators give as "disagreement" or
"dispute".

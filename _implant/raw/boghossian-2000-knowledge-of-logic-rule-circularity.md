> "This is at least part of the moral both of Lewis Carroll's 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles', in Mind 1898 and of Wittgenstein's discussion of rule-following in Philosophical Investigations (Oxford: Blackwell, 1958)."  (p. 230 n. 2; the year "1898" is as printed)

> "As a number of considerations reveal, S's disposition to reason in accordance with MPP and his belief that MPP is truth-preserving are distinct kinds of state."  (p. 230; the sentence n. 2 is attached to)

> "This brings us, then, to the inferential path. Here there are a number of distinct possibilities, but they would all seem to suffer from the same master difficulty: in being inferential, they would have to be rule-circular."  (p. 231)

> "And many philosophers have maintained that a rule-circular justification of a rule of inference is no justification at all."  (p. 231)

> "As is clear, however, this justification for MPP must itself take at least one step in accord with MPP."  (p. 231; of the truth-table argument for modus ponens)

> "At a minimum, then, the sceptical context discloses that a rule-circular argument for MPP would beg a sceptic's question about MPP and would, therefore, be powerless to quell his doubts about it."  (p. 246)

> "A grossly circular argument is guaranteed to succeed, no matter what proposition it is attempting to rationalize."  (p. 246)

Relied on for: Boghossian's link of Carroll's moral to the distinction
between a disposition to reason by MPP and the belief that MPP is
truth-preserving; the rule-circularity problem; the sceptical-context point.
Context: Boghossian asks whether belief in the validity of modus ponens
(MPP) can be justified, inferentially or non-inferentially; he goes on to
defend a restricted form of rule-circular justification and an
"entitlement" to reason by MPP grounded in meaning ("Rule-Circular"
section and "(L)" later in the chapter, not excerpted).

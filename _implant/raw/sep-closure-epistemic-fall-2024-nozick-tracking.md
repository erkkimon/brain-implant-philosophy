> "By contrast, Fred Dretske and Robert Nozick reject K and therefore GK as well."  (§1; K is the single-premise knowledge closure principle)

> "In addition to rejecting K and GK, they deny knowledge closure across instantiation and simplification, but not across equivalence (Nozick 1981: 227–229):"  (§1)

> "According to Nozick, to know p is, very roughly (and ignoring his thoroughly discredited fourth condition for knowledge, criticized, e.g, in Luper 1984 and 2009 and in Kripke 2011), to have a belief p which meets the following condition (‘BT’ for belief tracking): (BT) were p false, S would not believe p."  (§2.1)

> "Second, Nozick avoided the very juxtaposition Dretske discussed by restating his account to make reference to the methods via which we come to believe things (Hawthorne 2005)."  (§2.1)

> "(BMT) were p false, S would not believe p via M."  (§2.1)

> "According to Dretske and Nozick, skepticism is appealing because skeptics are partially right."  (§5.2)

> "At best, Dretske and Nozick have provided a way of dealing with the latter."  (§5.2; "the latter" is indiscernability (Cartesian) skepticism)

Relied on for: Nozick's tracking condition and method-relative version;
  his denial of closure; the tracking diagnosis of skepticism; Luper's
  assessments (the "thoroughly discredited" fourth condition; "At best").
Context: Luper reports Kripke's red-barn objection (Kripke 2011, "Nozick on
  Knowledge", Philosophical Troubles, 162–224) and Dretske's comparison of
  their views. The bibliography gives Nozick, R., 1981, Philosophical
  Explanations, and Luper (ed.) 1987, The Possibility of Knowledge: Nozick

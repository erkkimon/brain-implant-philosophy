> "In these writings the barriers to faith are seen as pride and selfishness, since faith in Christ requires a radical willingness to love the neighbor, a category which even includes one’s enemies."  (Lippitt & Evans, SEP "Søren Kierkegaard" §3.3.1)

> "However, Kierkegaard’s most significant text on love is the signed Works of Love, a series of fifteen “deliberations”, typically on a key New Testament passage on love. A central issue in this text is the love that is at stake in the command to love our neighbors as ourselves."  (§3.3.2)

> "The form of love that for Kierkegaard is at the root of all true love is Kjerlighed, which in many of its uses can reasonably be understood as “neighbor-love” and as a form of agape—provided we understand this as a quality that human beings are capable of manifesting (albeit with a divine source), not just a quality of God himself."  (§3.3.2)

> "“The neighbor” includes everyone, and we are to strive to love all—including our enemies. This is so counter-intuitive to the “natural” human, Kierkegaard claims, that it needs to be commanded (WL 24–5, SKS 9, 32–3)."  (§3.3.2)

> "Furthermore, the duty to love must be grounded in divine authority; no human authority is sufficient."  (§3.3.2)

> "Thus, it will not suffice to say that he simply attacks Christian or Kantian or European or utilitarian morality — though he certainly at times attacks all of these."  (Leiter, SEP "Nietzsche’s Moral and Political Philosophy" §1.1)

> "This point is even more explicit in The Antichrist, where Nietzsche notes that Christian morality “has waged deadly war against this higher type of man; it has placed all the basic instincts of his type under ban” (5, emphasis added)."  (§1.3)

> "What the Gospels make instinctive is precisely the reverse of all heroic struggle, of all taste for conflict: the very incapacity for resistance is here converted into something moral: ("resist not evil!"--the most profound sentence in the Gospels, perhaps the true key to them), to wit, the blessedness of peace, of gentleness, the inability to be an enemy."  (Nietzsche, The Antichrist §29, Mencken tr.)

> "This "bearer of glad tidings" died as he lived and taught--not to "save mankind," but to show mankind how to live. It was a way of life that he bequeathed to man: his demeanour before the judges, before the officers, before his accusers--his demeanour on the cross. He does not resist; he does not defend his rights; he makes no effort to ward off the most extreme penalty--more, he invites it.... And he prays, suffers and loves with those, in those, who do him evil...."  (§35)

> "The very word "Christianity" is a misunderstanding--at bottom there was only one Christian, and he died on the cross."  (§39)

> "It is an error amounting to nonsensicality to see in "faith," and particularly in faith in salvation through Christ, the distinguishing mark of the Christian: only the Christian way of life, the life lived by him who died on the cross, is Christian...."  (§39)

Relied on for: Kierkegaard's reading of the love commandment in Works of
Love (1847) as reported by Lippitt and Evans; Nietzsche's contrast between
Jesus' practice and later Christianity, quoted from the text, and Leiter's
statement of what Nietzsche's critique targets.
Context: §29 opens with Nietzsche's rejection of Renan's "hero" and
"genius" readings of "the type of Jesus"; §39 calls what followed "bad
tidings," a Dysangelium, and continues "Not faith, but acts". The SEP
Kierkegaard sentences are from the section on love that also lists the
secondary literature (R. Hall 2000, Ferreira 2001, Lippitt 2013a and
others).

> "The title of this chapter is ambiguous. When we call a state of things ‘ideal’ we may mean three distinct things, which have only this in common: that we always do mean to assert, of the state of things in question, not only that it is good in itself, but that it is good in itself in a much higher degree than many other things."  (ch. VI §110)

> "By this is meant the best state of things conceivable, the Summum Bonum or Absolute Good."  (ch. VI §110)

> "But this conception may be quite clearly distinguished from a second, namely, (2) that of the best possible state of things in this world."  (ch. VI §110)

> "It is in this sense that Utopias are said to be Ideals."  (ch. VI §110)

> "But, thirdly, we may mean by calling a state of things ‘ideal’ merely (3) that it is good in itself in a high degree."  (ch. VI §110)

> "Such an assertion is made by Kant when he tells us that his ‘Kingdom of Ends’ is the ideal."  (ch. IV §67)

Relied on for: Moore's three senses of "ideal" (the Absolute Good; the best
possible state, the "Human Good"; good in itself in a high degree), and his
reading of Kant's Kingdom of Ends.
Context: §110 opens ch. VI; Moore says the chapter is "principally
concerned" with sense (3), as the way to the question "What things are
goods or ends in themselves?". §67 lists metaphysical systems that assert
something supersensible to be perfectly good (Stoics, Spinoza, Kant, and
"modern writers" on the true self) before arguing against them.

# SEP "Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese Philosophy" (Wong) — xin 心 as heart-mind and its relation to the body

Source: David B. Wong, "Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese Philosophy", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N.
  Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first published 2023-07-10. Preamble, §1,
  §1.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/chinese-mind/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read; section
  numbers are SEP's own)

> "The nearest equivalent to the English term for mind in the classical
>  period is xin 心, which originated as a picture of the heart in human
>  beings and animals and directs body's behavior. Since ethical guidance
>  in Chinese thought arises from both the cognitive function of the mind
>  and the affective states attributed to the heart, xin is frequently
>  translated as 'heart-mind'. [...] The xin is credited with thinking si
>  思, understanding míng 明, knowing zhi 知, intention zhi 志, felt moods
>  and/or emotions qing 情, and desire yu 欲."  (preamble)

> "Comparative interpreters frequently address whether the heart-mind is
>  conceived as being separate or ontologically distinct from the body as
>  they are, for example, in Cartesian dualism. The dominant view in
>  scholarship is that Chinese thought is not dualistic in any fashion.
>  The minority view is that it does not fall into ontological dualism,
>  and that it is all the better for avoiding it, but other forms of
>  dualism may fairly be attributed to Chinese thought."  (§1)

> "Unlike Descartes' mind, xin's qi can interact with both the body's qi
>  and the wider qi filling the space between Heaven and earth. [...] The
>  heart-mind is not only the seat of cognition but also of emotions and
>  desires, which consist of qi. Chinese philosophers do not explain
>  behavior by a combination of cognitive and affective factors."  (§1.2)

> "We conclude the Chinese view is a genuine alternative to substance
>  dualism, differing from reductive materialism in a way arguably
>  compatible with modern physics."  (§1.2)

Relied on for: the claims that classical Chinese philosophy's nearest
term for mind, xin, unites cognition and affect ("heart-mind"); that the
dominant scholarly reading finds no Cartesian-style dualism in classical
Chinese thought while a minority (Slingerland) attributes a weaker
distinction; and that the entry itself concludes the view is "a genuine
alternative to substance dualism" — all as reported by the SEP entry,
which is the citable source here; the classical texts were not consulted.
Context: the entry covers roughly the 6th to 3rd centuries BCE; §1.1
presents the holistic "bodyheartminding" reading of Ames and Jullien, and
§1.2 Edward Slingerland's critique that pre-Qin texts show "a strong folk
belief in a distinction of some sort" that "might not be the Western
ontological, substance dualism". §2 (normative conceptions of the person
in the Analects, Mozi, Daodejing, Mengzi, Zhuangzi, Xunzi) is not
excerpted. Ellipses omit the sentence "This translation will be used
here" and the sentence on human unity with the world as an organic whole.

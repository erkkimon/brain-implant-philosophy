# James 1907 — Pragmatism: dedication to Mill, the pragmatic method, Peirce, truth as verification, God as hypothesis

Source: William James, Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of
  Thinking (New York: Longmans, Green & Co., 1907). Dedication; Lecture II
  "What Pragmatism Means"; Lecture VI "Pragmatism's Conception of Truth";
  Lecture VIII "Pragmatism and Religion". The Gutenberg text has no page
  markers; locators are lectures.

> "To the Memory of John Stuart Mill from whom I first learned the pragmatic openness of mind and whom my fancy likes to picture as our leader were he alive to-day."  (dedication)

> "The pragmatic method is primarily a method of settling metaphysical disputes that otherwise might be interminable."  (Lecture II)

> "The pragmatic method in such cases is to try to interpret each notion by tracing its respective practical consequences. What difference would it practically make to anyone if this notion rather than that notion were true? If no practical difference whatever can be traced, then the alternatives mean practically the same thing, and all dispute is idle."  (Lecture II)

> "It was first introduced into philosophy by Mr. Charles Peirce in 1878. In an article entitled 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear,' in the 'Popular Science Monthly' for January of that year"  (Lecture II)

> "TRUE IDEAS ARE THOSE THAT WE CAN ASSIMILATE, VALIDATE, CORROBORATE AND VERIFY. FALSE IDEAS ARE THOSE THAT WE CANNOT."  (Lecture VI)

> "The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth HAPPENS to an idea. It BECOMES true, is MADE true by events."  (Lecture VI)

> "'The true,' to put it very briefly, is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as 'the right' is only the expedient in the way of our behaving."  (Lecture VI)

> "On pragmatistic principles, if the hypothesis of God works satisfactorily in the widest sense of the word, it is true."  (Lecture VIII)

Relied on for: the dedication to Mill; the pragmatic method and its
attribution to Peirce (1878); the verification account of truth and the
"expedient" formula; the conditional on the hypothesis of God.
Context: The method passage follows the squirrel-and-tree anecdote in
Lecture II; the capitalised sentences in Lecture VI are capitalised in the
source. Directly after the Lecture VIII sentence James adds that
"experience shows that it certainly does work". The preface dates the
lectures to the Lowell Institute, Boston (November–December 1906) and
Columbia University (January 1907).

> "Professor Gödel, I’m fascinated by your incompleteness theorem. I have a new proof based on the Berry paradox that I’d like to tell you about.” Gödel said, “It doesn’t matter which paradox you use.”"  (p. 1, Chaitin's account of a 1974 phone call)

> "And Bertrand Russell when he published this paradox had a footnote saying that it was suggested to him by Mr G. G. Berry. So it ended up being called the Berry paradox even though it was published by Russell."  (p. 2)

> "“the first positive integer that cannot be specified in less than a billion words”."  (p. 3)

> "By definition this integer is supposed to take a billion words to specify, but I just specified it using much less than a billion words! That’s the Berry paradox."  (p. 3)

> "“the first positive integer that can be provedFAS to have the property that it cannot be specifiedUTM by a computer program with less than N bits”."  (p. 6; FAS = formal axiomatic system, UTM = universal Turing machine, subscripts in the original)

> "is much much smaller than N for all sufficiently large N! Thus for such N our FAS cannot enable us to exhibit any numbers that require programs more than N bits long. This is my information-theoretic incompleteness result that I wanted to discuss with Gödel."  (p. 7; the subject is a program of "log2 N + cFAS" bits)

> "You start running through all possible proofs in the formal axiomatic system in size order. You apply the proof-checking algorithm to each proof. And after filtering out all the invalid proofs, you search for the first proof that a particular positive integer requires at least an N-bit program."  (p. 7)

> "the central idea that can be extracted from my version of the Berry paradox, which is to define the program-size complexity of something to be the size in bits of the smallest program that calculates it."  (p. 8)

> "I have developed a general theory dealing with program-size complexity that I call algorithmic information theory (AIT)."  (p. 8)

Relied on for: Chaitin's account of the name; his version in words and in
bits; the shape of his incompleteness argument (search through proofs; a
program of log2 N + c bits); program-size complexity as extracted from the
paradox, in Chaitin's words.
Context: the lecture pairs Gödel's move from "This statement is false" to
"This statement is unprovable" with Chaitin's move from "specified" to
"proved to have the property that it cannot be specified"; pp. 7–8 list
three further results about Ω; p. 9 sets out questions, with Chaitin's
own answers in parentheses. Chaitin says (p. 1) he had the idea in 1970.

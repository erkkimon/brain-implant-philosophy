# Kreps & Wilson 1982 — Selten's chain-store game, the unravelling argument, and the reputation effect under incomplete information

> "But this phenomenon is not observed in some formal game-theoretic analyses of finite games, such as Selten’s finitely repeated chain-store game or in the finitely repeated prisoners’ dilemma."  (abstract, p. 253)

> "We reexamine Selten’s model, adding to it a “small” amount of imperfect (or incomplete) information about players’ payoffs, and we find that this addition is sufficient to give rise to the “reputation effect” that one intuitively expects."  (abstract, p. 253)

> "The intuitive appeal of this line of reasoning has, however, been called the “chain-store paradox” by Selten [24], who demonstrates that it is not supported in a straightforward game-theoretic model."  (p. 253; "this line of reasoning" is Scherer's "demonstration effect" of price cutting, quoted from Scherer 1980, p. 338)

> "What is lacking, apparently, is a plausible mechanism that connects behavior in otherwise independent markets."  (p. 254)

> "If rivals perceive the slightest chance that an incumbent firm might enjoy “rapacious responses,” then the incumbent’s optimal strategy is to employ such behavior against its rivals in all, except possibly the last few, in a long string of encounters."  (p. 254)

> "The entrant moves first, electing either to enter or to stay out. Following entry, the monopolist chooses either to acquiesce or to fight."  (p. 254)

Fig. 1 (p. 255), "Selten's chain-store game": entrant stays out —
entrant 0, monopolist a; monopolist acquiesces — entrant b, monopolist 0;
monopolist fights — entrant b − 1, monopolist −1; "We consider the case
that a > 1 and 0 < b < 1." (p. 255)

> "In the last stage the monopolist will not fight because there are no later entrants to demonstrate for."  (p. 255)

> "This logic can be repeated, unraveling from the back: In each stage entry and acquiescence will occur. To be precise, this is the unique perfect Nash equilibrium of the game; cf. Selten [22, 23, 24]."  (p. 255; the scan's text layer reads "acquiescencewill" and "241")

> "Apparently, this model is inadequate to justify Scherer’s prediction that reputational effects will play a role."  (p. 255)

> "Our contention is that this inadequacy arises because the model does not capture a salient feature of realistic situations."  (p. 256; the next sentence credits the contention first to Rosenthal [17])

> "In particular, this issue of the Journal includes a companion article by Milgrom and Roberts [13] that explores many of the issues studied here in models that are richer in institutional detail."  (p. 254)

> "As for the second, the models of Milgrom and Roberts [13] have continua of types of monopolists, so this does not seem crucial to our conclusions."  (p. 276)

> "In the parlance of game theory, for these effects to disappear, payoffs must be common knowledge."  (p. 276)

> "The reader may object that in order to obtain the reputation effect, we have loaded the deck."  (p. 276)

> "The power of reputation seems to be positively related to its fragility."  (p. 276)

> "If this is so, then the game-theoretic analysis of this type of game comes down eventually to how one picks the initial incomplete information. And nothing in the theory of games will help one to do this."  (p. 276)

> "The paradoxical result in Selten’s analysis is due to the complete and perfect information formulation that Selten uses. In a more realistic formulation of the game, the intuitive outcome will be predicted by the game-theoretic analysis."  (p. 276, reporting Rosenthal 1981)

> "But, as Rosenthal notes, the weakness in this approach is the ad hoc assessment of entrants’ behavior."  (p. 276, on the Decision Analysis approach and Macgregor 1979)

> "But we have made ad hoc assumptions about their information, and we have found that small changes in those assumptions greatly influence the play of the game."  (p. 277)

> "If deterrence is the objective, the appearance and not the reality of ex post optimal predation may be what is important."  (p. 277)

Relied on for: the stage game and its payoffs; the backward-induction
("unravelling") argument and the unique perfect equilibrium as Kreps &
Wilson state Selten's result; the tie to the finitely repeated
prisoner's dilemma; the incomplete-information (reputation) response and
its authors' own caveats; Rosenthal's prior contention as they report it;
Milgrom & Roberts as their companion paper.
Context: Kreps & Wilson open with Scherer's informal claim about
predatory pricing in multimarket firms, present Selten's game as showing
that the claim fails in a complete-information model, then add a small
prior probability that the monopolist is a "strong" type that prefers to
fight (Fig. 2, Section 3) and a two-sided version (Section 4).

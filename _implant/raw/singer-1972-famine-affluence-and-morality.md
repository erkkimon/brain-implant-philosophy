# Singer 1972 — "Famine, Affluence, and Morality": the drowning child, the strong and moderate principles, duty and charity, objections, postscript

Source: Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality", Philosophy & Public
  Affairs 1(3): 229–243 (1972) (volume/issue/pages as in the SEP
  bibliographies of Hurka 2024 and Jollimore 2021; the copy read heads

> "As I write this, in November 1971, people are dying in East Bengal from lack of food, shelter, and medical care."  (opening ¶)

> "I shall argue that the way people in relatively affluent countries react to a situation like that in Bengal cannot be justified; indeed, the whole way we look at moral issues - our moral conceptual scheme - needs to be altered, and with it, the way of life that has come to be taken for granted in our society."  (¶3)

> "In arguing for this conclusion I will not, of course, claim to be morally neutral."  (¶4)

> "I begin with the assumption that suffering and death from lack of food, shelter, and medical care are bad."  (¶5; p. 231 per Timmerman's premise list)

> "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it."  (¶6; p. 231 per Timmerman 2015, p. 205)

> "if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything morally significant, we ought, morally, to do it."  (¶6 (the qualified form))

> "if I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning in it, I ought to wade in and pull the child out. This will mean getting my clothes muddy, but this is insignificant, while the death of the child would presumably be a very bad thing."  (¶6; p. 231 per Hurka SEP §2 and Timmerman p. 205)

> "It makes no moral difference whether the person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know, ten thousand miles away."  (¶7)

> "the principle makes no distinction between cases in which I am the only person who could possibly do anything and cases in which I am just one among millions in the same position."  (¶7)

> "If we accept any principle of impartiality, universalizability, equality, or whatever, we cannot discriminate against someone merely because he is far away from us (or we are far away from him)."  (¶8)

> "There would seem, therefore, to be no possible justification for discriminating on geographical grounds."  (¶8)

> "Should I consider that I am less obliged to pull the drowning child out of the pond if on looking around I see other people, no further away than I am, who have also noticed the child but are doing nothing?"  (¶9; p. 233 per Timmerman p. 206)

> "One has only to ask this question to see the absurdity of the view that numbers lessen obligation."  (¶9)

> "The traditional distinction between duty and charity cannot be drawn, or at least, not in the place we normally draw it."  (duty-and-charity ¶)

> "On the contrary, we ought to give the money away, and it is wrong not to do so."  (duty-and-charity ¶)

> "It may be possible to redraw the distinction between duty and charity in some other place."  (following ¶)

> "One objection to the position I have taken might be simply that it is too drastic a revision of our moral scheme."  (first objection ¶)

> "J. O. Urmson suggests that the imperatives of duty, which tell us what we must do, as distinct from what it would be good to do but not wrong not to do, function so as to prohibit behavior that is intolerable if men are to live together in society."  (first objection ¶; note 3: Urmson, 'Saints and Heroes', 1958, p. 214)

> "If this is an explanation of our common distinction between duty and supererogation, however, it is not a justification of it."  (following ¶)

> "It has been argued by some writers, among them Sidgwick and Urmson, that we need to have a basic moral code which is not too far beyond the capacities of the ordinary man, for otherwise there will be a general breakdown of compliance with the moral code."  (Sidgwick–Urmson ¶)

> "This would seem to be an empirical question, although a very difficult one."  (Sidgwick–Urmson ¶)

> "It follows from some forms of utilitarian theory that we all ought, morally, to be working full time to increase the balance of happiness over misery."  (second objection ¶)

> "Given the present conditions in many parts of the world, however, it does follow from my argument that we ought, morally, to be working full time to relieve great suffering of the sort that occurs as a result of famine or other disasters."  (second objection ¶)

> "I cannot see, though, why it should be regarded as a criticism of the position for which I have argued, rather than a criticism of our ordinary standards of behavior."  (second objection ¶)

> "whatever a man has in superabundance is owed, of natural right, to the poor for their sustenance."  (Aquinas, ST II-II q. 66 a. 7, as quoted by Singer (tr. J. G. Dawson, 1948, per note 4))

> "It is sometimes said that overseas aid should be a government responsibility, and that therefore one ought not to give to privately run charities."  (practical points ¶1)

> "I do not, of course, want to dispute the contention that governments of affluent nations should be giving many times the amount of genuine, no-strings-attached aid that they are giving now."  (practical points)

> "I agree, too, that giving privately is not enough, and that we ought to be campaigning actively for entirely new standards for both public and private contributions to famine relief."  (practical points)

> "Another, more serious reason for not giving to famine relief funds is that until there is effective population control, relieving famine merely postpones starvation."  (practical points)

> "we ought to give until we reach the level of marginal utility - that is, the level at which, by giving more, I would cause as much suffering to myself or my dependents as I would relieve by my gift."  ('how much' ¶; p. 241 per Hurka SEP §1)

> "I should also say that the strong version seems to me to be the correct one."  ('how much' ¶)

> "I proposed the more moderate version - that we should prevent bad occurrences unless, to do so, we had to sacrifice something morally significant - only in order to show that, even on this surely undeniable principle, a great change in our way of life is required."  ('how much' ¶)

> "I now think that there is a serious case for saying that if a country refuses to take any steps to slow the rate of its population growth, we should not give it aid."  (Postscript)

> "my view is that the aid should be of whatever type is most effective."  (Postscript)

> "while I am myself inclined toward a utilitarian view"  (Postscript)

> "to produce an argument which would appeal not only to utilitarians, but also to anyone who accepted the initial premises of the argument, which seemed to me likely to have a very wide acceptance."  (Postscript)

> "Arthur is right to say that people could take the opposite view without being obviously irrational."  (Postscript)

> "But I do agree that the weak principle is nonetheless too weak, because it makes it too easy for the duty of benevolence to be avoided."  (Postscript)

> "In either case, I would argue against Arthur's view that we are morally entitled to give greater weight to our own interests and purposes simply because they are our own."  (Postscript)

> "Granted, in normal circumstances, it may be better for everyone if we recognize that each of us will be primarily responsible for running our own lives and only secondarily responsible for others."  (Postscript)

> "For the principle takes, firstly, no account of proximity or distance."  (¶7)

> "It may convince us, unless we notice that it is based on a hypothetical premise, although the conclusion is not stated hypothetically."  (numbers ¶ (the £5 argument; the currency sign is lost in the copy read))

> "This, however, is not a moral ultimate, but a secondary principle that derives from consideration of how a society may best order its affairs, given the limits of altruism in human beings."  (Postscript)

> "as John Arthur has shown in "Rights and the Duty to Bring Aid" (included in this volume), something more needs to be said about the notion of "moral significance.""  (Postscript)

> "J. O. Urmson, "Saints and Heroes," in Essays in Moral Philosophy, ed. Abraham I. Melden (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1958), p. 214. For a related but significantly different view see also Henry Sidgwick, The Methods of Ethics, 7th ed. (London: Dover Press, 1907), pp. 220-1, 492-3."  (note 3)

> "Philosophy and Public Affairs, vol. 1, no. 1 (Spring 1972), pp. 229-243 [revised edition]"  (heading of the copy read)

Relied on for: the case, the strong and moderate principles, the
distance and numbers replies, the duty/charity claim, Singer's own replies
to the "too drastic" and utilitarian-overdemand objections and to the
government-responsibility and population points, his preference for the
strong version, and the Postscript's concessions to and disagreements with
Arthur.
Context: written November 1971 about the East Bengal refugee crisis. The
principle is introduced right after the assumption that suffering and
death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad; the pond case is
offered as "An application of this principle". The Postscript was written
after the Bangladesh crisis ("now of historical interest only").

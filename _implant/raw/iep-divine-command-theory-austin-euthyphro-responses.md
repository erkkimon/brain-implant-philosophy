## Framing (§3)

> "The dialogue between Socrates and Euthyphro is nearly omnipresent in philosophical discussions of the relationship between God and ethics."  (§3)

> "Socrates asks Euthyphro the now philosophically famous question that he and any divine command theorist must consider: “Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?” (p. 14)."  (§3; Austin quotes Grube's translation, Plato 1981)

> "For our purposes, it will be useful to rephrase Socrates’ question."  (§3)

> "Socrates can be understood as asking “Does God command this particular action because it is morally right, or is it morally right because God commands it?”"  (§3)

> "The problem for this response to Socrates’ question, then, is that God’s commands and therefore the foundations of morality become arbitrary, which then allows for morally reprehensible actions to become morally obligatory."  (§3, first horn)

> "If God commands a particular action because it is morally right, then ethics no longer depends on God in the way that Divine Command Theorists maintain."  (§3, second horn)

> "God is no longer the author of ethics, but rather a mere recognizer of right and wrong."  (§3)

> "God is no longer sovereign over the entire universe, but rather is subject to a moral law external to himself."  (§3)

> "John Arthur (2005) puts the point this way: “If God approves kindness because it is a virtue and hates the Nazis because they were evil, then it seems that God discovers morality rather than inventing it” (20, emphasis added)."  (§3)

> "Hence, the advocate of a Divine Command Theory of ethics faces a dilemma: morality either rests on arbitrary foundations, or God is not the source of ethics and is subject to an external moral law, both of which allegedly compromise his supreme moral and metaphysical status."  (§3)

## Bite the bullet — Ockham (§4a)

> "In Super 4 Libros Sententiarum, William of Ockham states that the actions which we call “theft” and “adultery” would be obligatory for us if God commanded us to do them."  (§4a; reference list: "William of Ockham. Super 4 Libros Sententiarum II, 19.")

> "Most people find this to be an unacceptable view of moral obligation, on the grounds that any theory of ethics that leaves open the possibility that such actions are morally praiseworthy is fatally flawed."  (§4a)

> "However, as Robert Adams (1987) points out, a full understanding of Ockham’s view here would emphasize that it is a mere logical possibility that God could command adultery or cruelty, and not a real possibility."  (§4a)

> "Given this, Ockham himself was surely not prepared to inflict suffering on others if God commanded it."  (§4a, Austin's inference)

## Human nature — Clark and Poortenga, after Aquinas (§4b)

> "Another response to the Euthyphro Dilemma which is intended to avoid the problem of arbitrariness is discussed by Clark and Poortenga (2003), drawing upon the moral theory of Thomas Aquinas."  (§4b)

> "The defender of this type of response to the Euthyphro Dilemma, to avoid the charge of arbitrariness, should explain why God created us with the nature that we possess, rather than some other nature."  (§4b)

## Alston (§4c)

> "Alston’s argument is that if we interpret these statements correctly, a theist can in fact grasp both horns of this putative dilemma."  (§4c)

> "But this trivialization is not what we mean when we assert that God is morally good."  (§4c, the first horn's difficulty as Austin states it)

> "One response is to say that God is subject to moral principles in the same way that he is subject to logical principles, which nearly all agree does not compromise his sovereignty (See The Omnipotence Objection below)."  (§4c)

> "Alston prefers a different option, however, and argues that we can think of God himself as the supreme standard of goodness."  (§4c)

> "But is not arbitrariness still present, insofar as it seems that it is arbitrary to take a particular individual as the standard of goodness, without reference to the individual’s conformity to general principles of goodness?"  (§4c, the objection Austin raises to Alston)

> "In response, Alston points out that there must be a stopping point for any explanation."  (§4c)

> "And Alston’s view is that it is no more arbitrary to invoke God as the supreme moral standard than it is to invoke some supreme moral principle."  (§4c)

Austin quotes Alston 1990, p. 315: "And no moral obligations attach to God, assuming, as we are here, that God is essentially perfectly good."  (§4c)

## Modified divine command theory — Adams (§4d)

> "Adams argues that a modified divine command theorist “wants to say…that an act is wrong if and only if it is contrary to God’s will or commands (assuming God loves us)” (121)."  (§4d; Adams 1987)

> "Moreover, Adams claims that the following is a necessary truth: “Any action is ethically wrong if and only if it is contrary to the commands of a loving God” (132)."  (§4d)

> "It is not possible for a loving God to command cruelty for its own sake."  (§4d, Austin's statement of the modified theory's claim)

> "Morality, for the modified divine command theorist, is ultimately grounded in the perfect nature of God."  (§4d)

## Omnipotence and omnibenevolence (§7a–b)

> "Some would argue that this implication is inconsistent with the belief that God is omnipotent."  (§7a)

> "His view is that “to sin is to fall short of a perfect action; which is repugnant to omnipotence” (Ibid)."  (§7a, Aquinas, ST I q. 25 a. 3)

> "On Divine Command Theory, it problematically appears that God’s goodness consists in God doing whatever he wills to do. This problem has been given voice by Leibniz (1951), and has recently been discussed by Quinn (1978), Wierenga (1989), Alston (1989), and Wainright (2005)."  (§7b)

> "In response, divine command theorists have argued that they can still make sense of God’s goodness, by pointing out that he possesses traits which are good as distinguished from being morally obligatory."  (§7b)

> "In his discussion of the omnipotence of God, Thomas Aquinas responds to this understanding of omnipotence, and argues that it is misguided."  (§7a; Summa, First Part, Q. 25, a. 3)

> "whichever option a modified divine command theorist chooses, the modification at issue is aimed at avoiding both horns of the Euthyphro Dilemma."  (§4d; re-checked 2026-09-27 against https://iep.utm.edu/divine-c/)

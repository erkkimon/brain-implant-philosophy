## Excerpts

> "two types especially became clear to me which I have termed the Introversion and the Extraversion Types."  (Introduction, p. 9)

> "there exist thinking , feeling, sensation, and intuition. If one of these functions habitually prevails, a corresponding type results. I therefore discriminate thinking, feeling, sensation, and intuitive types."  (Introduction, p. 14; the scan's spacing "thinking ," kept)

> "I did not carry out the distinction outlined above, but identified the thinking type with the introvert and the feeling type with the extravert. A deeper elaboration of the problem proved this combination to be untenable."  (Introduction, p. 14; the sentence opens "In two former communications concerning psychological types," with a footnote naming the 1913 paper in Archives de Psychologie and Collected Papers on Analytical Psychology, 1916)

> "I have devoted the last chapter of this book to the definitions of my psychological conceptions."  (Introduction, p. 14)

> "Tertullian is a classical representative of the introverted thinking type."  (ch. I, p. 22)

> "Origen is a classical example of the extraverted type."  (ch. I, p. 25)

> "It is entirely characteristic that Tertullian should perform the sacrificium intellectus, whereas Origen is led to the sacrificium phalli"  (ch. I, p. 25)

> "By nominalism one understands that school which asserted that the so-called universalia, namely the generic or universal concepts, such as beauty, goodness, animal, man, etc., are nothing but nomina (names) or words"  (ch. I §4, p. 37)

> "Realism, on the contrary, affirms the existence of the universalia ante rem, namely, that the universal concepts have existence in themselves after the manner of the Platonic ideas."  (ch. I §4, p. 37)

> "the radical difference between nominalism and realism is not purely a logical and intellectual distinction but also a psychological one, which in the last resort amounts to a typical difference of psychological attitude to the object as well as to the idea."  (ch. I §4, p. 66)

> "Friedrich Schiller seems to have been the first to have made any considerable attempt at a conscious discrimination of typical attitudes"  (ch. II, p. 87)

> "it is a state of introspection, of inner contemplation towards the dream world of eternal ideas : it is therefore a state of introversion ."  (ch. III, p. 180, on the Apollonian; the scan's spacing kept)

> "if we were to content ourselves with the analogy, we should acquiesce in a limitation of outlook that does violence to Nietzsche’s ideas"  (ch. III, p. 180)

> "As I have already pointed out, realism corresponds with the principle of introversion, nominalism With extraversion."  (ch. VIII, p. 374; "With" capitalised in the scan)

> "These associations prompt us to regard the tender-minded as introverted"  (ch. VIII, p. 374)

> "In my view, therefore, James’ “tender-minded” and “tough-minded” are manifestly but a one-sided terminology, and at bottom conceal a certain prejudice."  (ch. VIII, p. 382)

> "James’ characterization deals with those same types which I have termed the introverted and the extraverted."  (ch. VIII, p. 382)

> "I distinguish four basic functions in all, two rational and two irrational — viz. thinking and feeling , sensation and intuition. I can give no a priori reason for selecting just these four as basic functions ; I can only point to the fact that this conception has shaped itself out of many years’ experience."  (ch. XI, def. 22 "Function", p. 547)

> "In presenting this, Jung’s crowning work, to the English-speaking world"  (Translator's Preface, p. i, Baynes)

Relied on for: the work's division into chapters and the definitions chapter; the two attitudes and four functions; Jung's revision of his earlier identification of thinking/introvert and feeling/extravert; the chapters on Tertullian and Origen, nominalism and realism, Schiller, Nietzsche's Apollonian/Dionysian and James's tender-/tough-minded; Jung's statement that he gives no a priori reason for the four functions.
Context: ch. VIII quotes James's *Pragmatism* (1907) two-column list (tender-minded: rationalistic, intellectualistic, idealistic, optimistic, religious, free-willist, monistic, dogmatical; tough-minded: empiricist, sensationalistic, materialistic, pessimistic, irreligious, fatalistic, pluralistic, sceptical) before mapping it to introversion/extraversion. Ch. III quotes Nietzsche's *Birth of Tragedy* and then says the analogy is limited because Nietzsche's Dionysian is "affective sensation" rather than differentiated feeling.

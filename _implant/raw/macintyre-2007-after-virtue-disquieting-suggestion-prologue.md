Chapter 1, "A Disquieting Suggestion":

> "Imagine that the natural sciences were to suffer the effects of a catastrophe."  (ch. 1, opening sentence)

> "The hypothesis which I wish to advance is that in the actual world which we inhabit the language of morality is in the same state of grave disorder as the language of natural science in the imaginary world which I described."  (ch. 1)

> "What we possess, if this view is true, are the fragments of a conceptual scheme, parts which now lack those contexts from which their significance derived."  (ch. 1)

> "It is the aim of this book to make that thought available to radicals, liberals and conservatives alike."  (ch. 1)

Prologue to the Third Edition (2007):

> "But what those philosophers in fact provided were several rival and incompatible accounts, utilitarians competing with Kantians and both with contractarians,"  (Prologue, p. viii, of the Enlightenment moral philosophers)

> "When I wrote After Virtue, I was already an Aristotelian, but not yet a Thomist,"  (Prologue)

> "I have been accused of nostalgia and of idealizing the past."  (Prologue)

> "presuppose the falsity of relativism."  (Prologue, of the claims of rival traditions of enquiry)

> "something that I have never been. I see no value in community as such"  (Prologue, of being called a communitarian)

Relied on for: the chapter structure of After Virtue (the Enlightenment
project, "Nietzsche or Aristotle?"); the disquieting suggestion in
MacIntyre's own words; his 2007 statements that he became a Thomist after
the book, that he rejects relativism, and that he is not a communitarian.
Context: ch. 1 imagines a world where natural science survives only in
fragments after a catastrophe and proposes that morality is in that state
now; the Prologue answers twenty-five years of criticism (nostalgia,
relativism, communitarianism) and reports the turn to Aquinas.

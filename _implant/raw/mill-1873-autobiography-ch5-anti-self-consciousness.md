# Mill 1873 — Autobiography, chapter V: happiness attained by not making it the direct end

Source: John Stuart Mill, *Autobiography* (London: Longmans, Green, Reader,
  and Dyer, 1873), chapter V ("A Crisis in My Mental History. One Stage
  Onward", covering 1826–1832).
Original: Project Gutenberg eBook #10378 (released 2003-12-01):
  https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10378 (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (plain text fetched from
  https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10378/pg10378.txt; chapter read from
  the heading)

> "In the first place, they led me to adopt a theory of life, very unlike that on which I had before I acted, and having much in common with what at that time I certainly had never heard of, the anti-self-consciousness theory of Carlyle."  (ch. V)

> "I never, indeed, wavered in the conviction that happiness is the test of all rules of conduct, and the end of life. But I now thought that this end was only to be attained by not making it the direct end."  (ch. V)

> "Those only are happy (I thought) who have their minds fixed on some object other than their own happiness; on the happiness of others, on the improvement of mankind, even on some art or pursuit, followed not as a means, but as itself an ideal end. Aiming thus at something else, they find happiness by the way."  (ch. V)

> "Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so. The only chance is to treat, not happiness, but some end external to it, as the purpose of life."  (ch. V)

> "This theory now became the basis of my philosophy of life. And I still hold to it as the best theory for all those who have but a moderate degree of sensibility and of capacity I for enjoyment; that is, for the great majority of mankind."  (ch. V; "capacity I for" as in the Gutenberg text)

Relied on for: Mill's statement that happiness is attained by not making it
  the direct end, while remaining the "test of all rules of conduct"; his
  link to Carlyle; the scope he gives the theory.
Context: "The experiences of this period" are Mill's depression of 1826 and
  its gradual lifting, narrated in the paragraphs before; the passage opens
  "The experiences of this period had two very marked effects on my
  opinions and character"; the second effect is the "internal culture of
  the individual".

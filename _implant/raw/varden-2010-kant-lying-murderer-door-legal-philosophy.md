> "Kant’s example of lying to the murderer at the door has been a cherished source of scorn for thinkers with little sympathy for Kant’s philosophy and a source of deep puzzlement for those more favorably inclined."  (p. 403)

> "In this paper, I argue that Kant’s discussion of lying to the murderer at the door has been seriously misinterpreted."  (p. 403)

> "we can make sense of why lying to the murderer, although a wrong, is not to wrong the murderer, why we become responsible for the bad consequences of the lie, and finally why lying is to do wrong in general"  (p. 404)

> "The Nazis, however, did not represent a public authority on Kant’s view and consequently there is no duty to abstain from lying to Nazis."  (p. 404)

> "Kant never discusses first-personal ethics (universalizable maxims and actions from duty) in this paper."  (p. 406)

On the traditional reading:

> "When viewed this way the “Supposed Right to Lie,” including passages like the one quoted above, is seen as accomplishing two goals: it simply repeats how one ought never to lie as the maxim of lying cannot be universalized, and it cashes out the implications of this moral principle with regard to people’s enforceable rights and duties"  (p. 405)

Against three sympathetic defences (refuse to answer; answer "yes" but bar entry; say "I do not
know"):

> "Yet, it is clear that these two responses do not permit us to conclude that we can lie to the murderer at the door."  (p. 407)

> "Hence, lying is wrong in general as it is inconsistent with a world of rightful interaction, even when—as is generally the case—it is not a wrong of justice against another, particular person."  (p. 409)

> "His basic claim is that if a person chooses to stay out of the interaction between the murderer and his potential victim by telling the truth to the potential murderer, then a public court of justice cannot punish her for having done so."  (p. 410)

> "the homeowner also becomes responsible for the unintended, yet bad consequences of the lie"  (p. 410)

Note 5 (p. 419), on related readings:

> "Sussman, Jacob Weinrib (“The Juridical Significance of Kant’s ‘Supposed Right to Lie’,” Kantian Review 13, no. 1 [2008]: 141–70) and Allen W. Wood, Ch. 14, “Lies,” in Kantian Ethics (New York: Cambridge University Press, 2008), 240–58) agree with me on the importance of reading Kant’s remarks on lying to the murderer at the door from the point of view of the Doctrine of Right."  (p. 419, n. 5)

> "Sussman defends lying in cases of self-defense"  (p. 419, n. 5)

Relied on for: the "traditional reading" vs. the Doctrine of Right reading; the claim that the
essay concerns public justice, not ethics; Varden's rejection of three sympathetic escapes;
her Nazi-officer analysis; her report of Sussman 2009, Weinrib 2008 and Wood 2008 as sharing
the juridical starting point.
Context: Varden quotes the essay in Gregor's translation (8:425, 8:426, 8:427). She describes
the "traditional reading" as taking the essay through the Groundwork's universalizability test
(p. 405). Her conclusion on Nazis rests on Kant's distinction between a despot and a sovereign
(pp. 414–415): "the Nazi would not count as representing a public authority" (p. 415).

# Butler, Karpowitz & Pope 2017 — Fenno's paradox and the spillover question

Source: Daniel M. Butler, Christopher F. Karpowitz and Jeremy C. Pope,
  "Who Gets the Credit? Legislative Responsiveness and Evaluations of
  Members, Parties, and the US Congress", Political Science Research and
  Methods 5(2), April 2017, pp. 351–366 (published online 2016).
Original: https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2015.83 (DOI verified by content
  negotiation 2026-10-08) (copyrighted; excerpts only; the PDF is free to
  read at Cambridge Core)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (publisher PDF through pdftotext; pages are the
  journal's)

> "Voters in the United States are highly dissatisﬁed with Congress. In November 2013, Gallup reported that approval had fallen to single digits for the ﬁrst time since tracking began in 1974."  (p. 351)

> "Such low levels of approval, which have become a perennial issue for Congress (Patterson and Magleby 1992), have important consequences for both the members of Congress (MCs) and the democratic process."  (p. 351)

> "Indeed, the heart of Fenno’s famous paradox is that constituents evaluate their individual representative and the institution differently because constituents use “different standards of judgment,” holding MCs to a less exacting standard than they do for the broader institution (Fenno 1975, 278; see also Cook 1979; Parker and Davidson 1979; Ripley et al. 1992)."  (p. 353)

> "Rather than getting members to campaign for the institution (Fenno 1978), they would simply need to get members to abstain from campaigning against the institution."  (p. 353)

> "Fenno (1975) famously wrote, “We do, it appears, love our congressmen. On the other hand, it seems equally clear that we do not love our Congress.” This observation has rightly come to be known as Fenno’s paradox because Congress is merely the aggregation of its individual members."  (p. 363, "Discussion")

> "Overall, we ﬁnd that constituents who received a response from their own MC evaluate that representative more positively than those who did not receive a response, but legislator responsiveness does not predict evaluations of the MC’s political party or the Congress."  (abstract, p. 351)

> "Their legislative responsiveness appears to have a small spillover effect on institutions. However, when we employ a unique panel design that controls for prior levels of opinion and avoids recall bias, we ﬁnd no evidence of spillover effects."  (abstract, p. 351)

> "If citizens use their individual member’s actions as a heuristic to form evaluations of Congress or the party institutions, then we should expect to see member’s positive actions exerting some inﬂuence on citizens’ evaluations of those institutions."  (p. 363)

> "There is no spillover effect from member responsiveness."  (p. 363)

> "Our research focused on one issue area—immigration."  (p. 364)

> "Even if legislators never ran for Congress by running against the institution, perhaps we would still observe Fenno’s paradox because there is no spillover effect and because members have little incentive to proactively promote Congress as a whole."  (p. 364)

> "Fenno, Richard. 1975. ‘If, As Ralph Nader Says, Congress is the Broken Branch, How Come We Love Our Congressmen So Much?’. In Norman J. Ornstein (ed.), Congress in Change: Evolution and Reform, 277–87. New York: Praeger."  (references, p. 365)

Relied on for: Fenno's 1975 sentence as quoted by these authors; their
reading of the paradox ("different standards of judgment", Fenno 1975,
278); their assessment that the name is apt; the Gallup figure as they
report it; their panel finding and its stated limits.
Context: the cross-sectional 2008 CCES data they examine first show "a
small spillover effect" (abstract); the panel of letter-writers (students,
one issue) shows none. Bibliography: Fenno 1975 in Ornstein (ed.),
Congress in Change: Evolution and Reform, pp. 277–87, Praeger; Fenno 1978,
Homestyle, Little, Brown; Cook 1979, LSQ 4(1):43–52; Parker and Davidson
1979, LSQ 4(1):53–61.

> "Egalitarianism is a trend of thought in political philosophy. An egalitarian favors equality of some sort: People should get the same, or be treated the same, or be treated as equals, in some respect."  (preamble)

> "An alternative view expands on this last-mentioned option: People should be treated as equals, should treat one another as equals, should relate as equals, or enjoy an equality of social status of some sort."  (preamble)

> "Egalitarian doctrines tend to rest on a background idea that all human persons are equal in fundamental worth or moral status. So far as the Western European and Anglo-American philosophical tradition is concerned, one significant source of this thought is the Christian notion that God loves all human souls equally."  (preamble)

> "Egalitarianism is a protean doctrine, because there are several different types of equality, or ways in which people might be treated the same, or might relate as equals, that might be thought desirable."  (preamble)

> "In this exercise, we seek to undo the effect of brute luck but not option luck, that former being luck that falls on you in ways beyond your power to control and the latter being luck that you can avoid, and maybe reasonably avoid, by voluntary choice."  (§3.5)

> "The Dworkin proposal is noteworthy for its integration of themes of equality and personal responsibility in a single conception."  (§3.5)

> "In a slogan, one might assert an ideal of equality of opportunity for welfare."  (§3.6)

> "One is the idea that in an egalitarian society people should relate to one another as equals or should enjoy the same fundamental status (and also perhaps the same rank and power)."  (§4)

> "From the standpoint of the relational equality versions of egalitarianism, equality of condition doctrines get the moral priorities backward."  (§4)

> "Elizabeth Anderson and Samuel Scheffler both affirm versions of relational equality and from this standpoint criticize the family of views that Anderson calls “luck egalitarian,” whose foremost exponents are perhaps G. A. Cohen and Ronald Dworkin"  (§4)

> "According to Anderson, the luck egalitarian holds that unchosen and uncourted inequalities ought to be eliminated and that chosen and courted inequalities should be left standing"  (§4)

> "One is that the luck egalitarian wrongly takes the aim of egalitarian justice to be achieving an equal distribution of stuff rather than egalitarian solidarity and respect among members of society."  (§4)

> "Another is that the policy recommendations implied by luck egalitarian principles are too harsh in their dealings with people who fare badly but are deemed personally responsible for their plight"  (§4)

> "“the proper negative aim of egalitarian justice is not to eliminate the impact of brute luck from human affairs, but to end oppression, which by definition is socially imposed” (Anderson 1999)"  (§4)

> "The luck egalitarian critic of this relational equality ideal is committed only to rejecting it as a proposal for the domain of moral right."  (§4)

> "Relational equality advocates usually advance their equality ideal as a rival to other understandings of equality including luck egalitarianism."  (§4)

> "What has been called the “leveling-down objection” suggests a reason to answer in the negative: everyone's being equal is not valuable for its own sake."  (§6)

> "If equality is non-instrumentally valuable, then the situation that obtains after leveling down must be in at least one respect better than the preceding situation: the new situation is more equal. The objection simply is that it is counterintuitive to claim that in any respect increasing equality by making someone worse off and no one else better off is improving the situation."  (§6)

> "As stated, the leveling down objection invites the reply by the egalitarian that the situation after leveling down is indeed in one respect better than the situation previously obtaining: after all, we now have greater equality or a closer approximation to a condition of equality. We may have a standoff of conflicting moral intuitions."  (§6)

> "By a halo effect, the goodness of 2 appears to shine on 1."  (§6)

> "The values that might be confounded with equality include sufficiency, priority, and desert."  (§6)

> "Perhaps the problem is not that the poor have less than the rich, but rather that the poor do not have enough—a sufficient level of resources to provide a good life or a reasonable prospect of a good life."  (§6.1)

> "A potential problem for the sufficiency doctrine is that it may not be possible non-arbitrarily to specify a line of sufficiency such that moving a person just across that line has great moral significance."  (§6.1)

> "Suppose that for any person, the lower her lifetime well-being score—the amount of well-being she gains or is now expected to gain over the course of her life—the more morally valuable it would be to secure an incremental gain of well-being for her (or to avoid a small loss). This is the root idea of prioritarianism."  (§6.2)

> "Prioritarianism is not vulnerable to the leveling down objection, so if one finds that objection decisive against any position that takes equality of any sort to be non-instrumentally valuable, priority looks to be an ethically attractive alternative."  (§6.2)

> "For example, if benefit is identified with the Rawlsian idea of a primary social good, and one takes prioritarianism beyond its limit to maximin, one then has the Rawlsian conception, that justice in most general terms is bringing about the best possible outcome for the very worst off."  (§6.2)
> "(Cohen 1989, Anderson 1999, Scheffler 2010, chapters 7 and 8, Dworkin 2000, and for responses to criticisms, Dworkin 2003 and 2010 and Arneson 2004)"  (§4)

Relied on for: the definition and protean character of egalitarianism; Dworkin's brute/option luck
  distinction; Arneson's equal opportunity for welfare; relational equality (Anderson, Scheffler)
  and its criticisms of luck egalitarianism; the levelling-down objection, the egalitarian reply
  and the "halo effect" diagnosis; sufficiency and priority as alternatives; maximin as the limit
  of prioritarianism.

Context: Arneson is himself a named participant (Arneson 1989, 2000, 2004 are cited in the entry);
  §6 asks whether equality is valuable per se and presents sufficiency, priority and desert as

  the entry to Parfit 1991, Temkin 1993 and Holtug 2010.

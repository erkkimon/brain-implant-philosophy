# Aristotle, Rhetoric I.2, 1356a1–13 — the three proofs furnished by the speech

Source: Aristotle, Rhetoric (Ars Rhetorica), Book I, chapter 2, §§3–4
  (Bekker 1356a1–13), translated by J. H. Freese (Loeb Classical Library
  193, 1926).
Original: http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0060:book=1:chapter=2
  (Perseus Digital Library; the 1926 Freese translation)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25

> "Now the proofs furnished by the speech are of three kinds. The first
>  depends upon the moral character of the speaker, the second upon putting
>  the hearer into a certain frame of mind, the third upon the speech itself,
>  in so far as it proves or seems to prove."  (I.2.3, 1356a1–4)

> "The orator persuades by moral character when his speech is delivered in
>  such a manner as to render him worthy of confidence; […] But this
>  confidence must be due to the speech itself, not to any preconceived idea
>  of the speaker's character; for it is not the case, as some writers of
>  rhetorical treatises lay down in their 'Art,' that the worth of the orator
>  in no way contributes to his powers of persuasion; on the contrary, moral
>  character, so to say, constitutes the most effective means of proof."
>  (I.2.4, 1356a4–13)

> "Rhetoric then may be defined as the faculty of discovering the possible
>  means of persuasion in reference to any subject whatever."  (I.2.1,
>  1355b25–26; added 2026-09-25)

Relied on for: the textual claim that Aristotle counts the speaker's
character (ēthos) as one of the three artistic proofs and calls it, in this
translation, "the most effective means of proof"; and for the attributive
claim that he reports an opposing view held by earlier writers of
rhetorical handbooks.
Context: §§5–6 go on to the hearer's emotions (pathos) and the argument
itself (logos); they are not reproduced.

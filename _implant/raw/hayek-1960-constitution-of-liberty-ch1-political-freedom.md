# Hayek 1960 — The Constitution of Liberty, ch. 1: personal freedom set apart from "political freedom"; Hamowy's editorial note

> "The state in which a man is not subject to coercion by the arbitrary will of another or others"  (ch. 1 §1, p. 58; a footnote marker "3" follows "others" in the print)

> "The time-honored phrase by which this freedom has often been described is therefore “independence of the arbitrary will of another.”"  (ch. 1 §1, p. 59)

> "It is what is commonly called “political freedom,” the participation of men in the choice of their government, in the process of legislation, and in the control of administration."  (ch. 1 §2, p. 61)

> "But a free people in this sense is not necessarily a people of free men; nor need one share in this collective freedom to be free as an individual."  (ch. 1 §2, pp. 61–62)

> "Clearly, a slave will not become free if he obtains merely the right to vote, nor will any degree of “inner freedom” make him anything but a slave—however much idealist philosophers have tried to convince us to the contrary."  (ch. 1, p. 71)

> "the right . . . to the franchise is essential to liberty; and a citizen excluded from it is unfree."  (ch. 1 n. 11, p. 62, Hayek quoting Harold J. Laski, Liberty in the Modern State, new ed. 1948, p. 48, as one who would call the unenfranchised unfree)

> "It was not the main thesis of my book that “freedom may be defined as the absence of coercion.”"  (Hayek 1961, "Freedom and Coercion", New Individualist Review 1, quoted in Hamowy's Introductory Essay, p. 13)

> "in which I argue that Hayek’s claim that the rule of law is a sufficient condition for a free society is untenable inasmuch as the criteria he posits for the rule of law are, if analyzed, consistent with a regime that even Hayek would regard as clearly unfree."  (Hamowy, Introductory Essay, n. 28, p. 11, on his own "Freedom and the Rule of Law in F. A. Hayek", Il Politico 36, 1971: 349–76)

Relied on for: Hayek's definition of individual freedom and his
  separation of it from "political freedom"; Laski as the contrary view
  Hayek names; Hamowy's criticism as Hamowy states it.
Context: §1 says that Hayek will use "individual" or "personal" freedom
  for his sense and avoid "civil liberty" because it is "too liable to be
  confused with what is called “political liberty”". Hayek's n. 10 traces
  the distinction between ruling-and-being-ruled and living as one
  chooses to Aristotle, Politics 6.3. Hamowy's 1961 critique ("Hayek's
  Concept of Freedom: A Critique", New Individualist Review 1: 28–31) is
  cited in the foreword, p. xi n. 1, and was not read here.

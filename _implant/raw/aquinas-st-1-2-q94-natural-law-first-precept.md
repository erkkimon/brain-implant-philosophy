# Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q. 94 a. 2 and a. 4 — the first precept of natural law and its universality

Source: Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94 ("Of the natural
  law"), a. 2 ("Whether the natural law contains several precepts, or only
  one?") respondeo and reply to obj. 1; a. 4 ("Whether the natural law is

> "Hence this is the first precept of law, that "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided." All other precepts of the natural law are based upon this"  (a. 2, respondeo)

> "Wherefore according to the order of natural inclinations, is the order of the precepts of the natural law."  (a. 2, respondeo)

> "thus man has a natural inclination to know the truth about God, and to live in society"  (a. 2, respondeo, the third inclination)

> "Reply to Objection 1. All these precepts of the law of nature have the character of one natural law, inasmuch as they flow from one first precept."  (a. 2, ad 1)

> "Consequently we must say that the natural law, as to general principles, is the same for all, both as to rectitude and as to knowledge."  (a. 4, respondeo)

> "thus formerly, theft, although it is expressly contrary to the natural law, was not considered wrong among the Germans, as Julius Caesar relates (De Bello Gall. vi)."  (a. 4, respondeo)

Relied on for: the first precept, the order of precepts following the
  order of natural inclinations (self-preservation; what nature taught all
  animals; knowing the truth about God and living in society), the unity
  of natural law, and its sameness in general principles but possible
  failure in detailed conclusions.
Context: a. 2 compares the precepts of natural law to the self-evident
  first principles of speculative reason, whose first is that "the same
  thing cannot be affirmed and denied at the same time". The three
  inclinations are to self-preservation (shared with all substances), to
  "sexual intercourse, education of offspring and so forth" (shared with
  animals), and the rational inclination quoted. a. 4 says the detailed
  conclusions may fail "in some few cases" because "the reason is
  perverted by passion, or evil habit, or an evil disposition of nature".
  a. 5–6 treat change in the natural law and whether it can be blotted out
  of the heart.

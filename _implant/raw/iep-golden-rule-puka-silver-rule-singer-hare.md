# IEP "The Golden Rule" (Puka) — versions, silver rule, scope, Singer's and Hare's analyses

  §3, §6, §7 (author named in the entry's "Author Information" block).

> "The most familiar version of the Golden Rule says, “Do unto others as you would have them do unto you.”"  (preamble)

> "Its corollary, the so-called “silver rule,” focuses on restraint and non-harm: “do nothing to others you would not have done to you.”"  (§1)

> "The golden rule is closely associated with Christian ethics though its origins go further back and graces Asian culture as well."  (§1)

> "Yet the “love thy neighbor” maxim far exceeds the golden rule in its moral expectations. It stresses loving identification with others while the golden rule merely advises equal treatment."  (§1)

> "The golden rule is much-reputed for being the most culturally universal ethical tenet in human history."  (§3)

> "No fair reading of Levitticus XIX: 18 would term its statement the golden rule, not in our modern sense, first stated in Matthew 7:12."  (§3; spelling as in the entry)

> "For philosophers, however, even a clarified or unbiased depiction of the golden rule cannot overcome its shortfalls in specificity and decisiveness."  (§6)

> "It has been claimed, in fact, that the rule’s logic was designed for this generalization across cases, situations, and all varieties of societies (Singer (1963) and Hare (1975))."  (§6)

> "These interpretations are highly unlikely judging from the rule’s strikingly ethnic origin and design function, as a bottom-up approach makes clear."  (§6)

> "Singer argues further that the golden rule is a procedural principle, directing us through a process—perspective-taking, either real or imaginary, for example—to generate morally salient action directives."  (§6)

> "R. M. Hare basically places the golden rule in the company of the Kantian and Utilitarian theories, or his own “universal prescriptivism.”"  (§6)

> "Notably, no grounding is offered for this claim—for the switch from “would have” to “wish” or “glad,” as if these were obviously the same ideas."  (§6)

> "The greatest help that the golden rule’s common sense might seek from philosophy is a conceptual analysis of the “as you would have” notion (Matthew 7:12)."  (§7)

> "A related misinterpretation puts us in another’s position with our particular interests in tact, asking ourselves what we in particular would prefer."  (§7; "in tact" as in the entry)

Relied on for: the positive ("golden") and negative ("silver") forms as
Puka names them; Puka's distinction between the rule and "love thy
neighbor"; the claim of cultural universality as a reputation he reports;
his reading of Leviticus 19:18; his report of Singer (1963) and Hare (1975)
as generalizing the rule, his assessment of that reading, and his
objection to Hare's "wish"/"glad" rendering; the "as you would have"
interpretation problem.
Context: Puka works "bottom-up" from common practice and argues the rule's
original scope was small-group and in-group (§§1–2); the entry's reference
list gives Singer, M. (1963) "The Golden Rule", Philosophy 38 (146):
293–314; Hare, R. M. (1975) "Abortion and the Golden Rule", Philosophy and
Public Affairs 4 (3): 201–222; and Wattles, The Golden Rule (Oxford
University Press; printed "1966" in the entry, 1996 per Crossref).

# SEP "Vagueness", "Sorites Paradox", "The Analysis of Knowledge", "Self-Knowledge" — what four encyclopedia entries report of Timothy Williamson (epistemicism, margin for error, knowledge first, anti-luminosity)

## A. Vagueness (Sorensen)

Source: Roy Sorensen, "Vagueness", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published Sat Feb 8, 1997; substantive revision Thu Jun 16, 2022
  (copyright line: "Copyright © 2022 by Roy Sorensen").
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/vagueness/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped,
  each quotation checked against that text)

> "Epistemicists accept this astonishing consequence. They stand by classical logic and conclude that vagueness is a form of ignorance."  (§ on epistemicism)

> "Timothy Williamson (1994) traces our ignorance of the threshold for childhood to “margin for error” principles."  (same section)

> "Otherwise, one is right by luck. Given that there is a threshold, we would be ignorant of its location. Under a use theory of meaning, the threshold depends on many speakers. Williamson characterizes this threshold as sharing the unpredictability of long-term weather forecasts."  (same section)

> "Debate over Williamson’s margin for error principle draws us deep into epistemology and modality (Yli-Vakkuri 2016)."  (same section)

> "If nothing steps in to make a proposition true, then it is false. Or so opines Timothy Williamson."  (same section, on stipulation and meaning)

Bibliography entry as printed: "Williamson, Timothy, 1994, Vagueness,
London: Routledge."

Relied on for: epistemicism as classical logic plus ignorance (Sorensen's
report); Williamson 1994 as the source of the margin-for-error explanation
of ignorance of the threshold; the use-theory / weather-forecast point;
Yli-Vakkuri 2016 as a place the principle is debated.
Context: the sentence between the second and third quotation gives the
principle in the child example ("If one knows that an n-day-old human
being is a child, then that human being must also be a child when n + 1
days old", LaTeX in the original). "astonishing" is Sorensen's word for the
consequence that there is a last day of childhood. Sorensen is himself an
epistemicist (Sorensen 1988, 2001 are listed as epistemicist works in the
Hyde & Raffman entry, section B).

## B. Sorites Paradox (Hyde & Raffman)

Source: Dominic Hyde & Diana Raffman, "Sorites Paradox", The Stanford
  Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition); first published Fri Jan
  17, 1997; substantive revision Mon Mar 26, 2018. §§3.2, 5.1, 5.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/sorites-paradox/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped)

> "According to Williamson (e.g., 1994b: 184), meaning supervenes on use; in other words, the locations of the sharp boundaries of a vague term are a function of speakers’ dispositions to use it as they do."  (§3.2)

> "The virtues and the appeal of the epistemic theory are significant, and it has earned its share of supporters. At the same time, the view may be hard to accept."  (§3.2)

> "Even its proponents grant that epistemicism is intuitively implausible; and it seems to multiply mysteries."  (§3.2)

> "Nevertheless, epistemicism may have the feel of a “just so” story."  (§3.2)

> "Retention of classical logic and bivalence is supposed to be a chief advantage of the epistemic approach over other views (e.g., Williamson 1992: 162)."  (§3.2)

> "Williamson reports this concern others might have, “the epistemic view of vagueness sets truth-conditions floating unacceptably free of our dispositions to assent and dissent” (1994a: 205)."  (§5.1)

> "The answer, Williamson suggests, lies in the fact that truth and falsity are not symmetrical notions. Falsity obtains in the absence of truth, so where there is symmetry at the level of use, falsity reigns. Whether this response succeeds is debated in Burgess 2001 and Weatherson 2003."  (§5.1)

> "As already noted in connection with supervaluationism, theories that abandon bivalence have been charged with having to reject the required Tarskian constraint on truth encapsulated in his T-schema: ‘p’ is true if and only if p."  (§5.2)

> "The rejection of bivalence in the context of the T-schema is said to lead to absurdity (Williamson 1994a: ch. 7; see Wright 1994 for criticism)."  (§5.2)

Bibliography entries as printed: Williamson 1992, "Vagueness and
Ignorance", Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society, Supplementary Volumes,
66: 145–177, doi:10.1093/aristoteliansupp/66.1.145 (DOI resolved
2026-09-27; Crossref gives pages 145–178, published-print 1992-07-01, and
lists the symposium co-author Simons); 1994a, Vagueness, London: Routledge;
1994b, "Definiteness and Knowability", in Horgan (ed.) 1994: 171–191; 1996,
"What Makes it a Heap?", Erkenntnis 44(3): 327–339, doi:10.1007/BF00167662
(resolved 2026-09-27).

Relied on for: the meaning-supervenes-on-use claim; the symmetry reply;
the T-schema argument against non-bivalent theories and Wright 1994's
criticism; the entry authors' assessments ("intuitively implausible" as
reported of proponents, "just so" story), attributed to Hyde & Raffman.
Context: the "just so" sentence is preceded by Raffman 2014: 10's
reformulation "vague terms have unknowable sharp boundaries that are fixed
by an unknowable function of their unknowable patterns of use", and by the
explanations offered for the ignorance (inexact knowledge; "the data are
infinite", Williamson 1994b: 184–185).

## C. The Analysis of Knowledge (Ichikawa & Steup)

Source: Jonathan Jenkins Ichikawa & Matthias Steup, "The Analysis of
  Knowledge", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition);
  first published Tue Feb 6, 2001; substantive revision Tue Mar 7, 2017.
  §11 "Knowledge First" and the safety discussion.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/knowledge-analysis/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped)

> "In recent work, especially his 2000 book Knowledge and Its Limits, Timothy Williamson has argued that the project of analyzing knowledge was a mistake."  (§11)

> "As Williamson puts it, we should put “knowledge first”."  (§11)

> "This, for instance, is how Timothy Williamson characterizes safety. He writes, in response to a challenge by Alvin Goldman:"  (safety section, introducing the Williamson 2009: 305 block quotation)

> "There is no very straightforward argument for this conclusion; its case consists largely in the attempted demonstration of the theoretical success of the knowledge first stance."  (§11)

> "Knowledge is the most general factive mental state."  (§11, first of two claims "Williamson accepts")

> "And Williamson (2000: 126) is explicit in endorsement of a safety requirement on knowledge—just not one that serves as part of an analysis."  (§11)

Bibliography entry as printed: "Williamson, Timothy, 2000, Knowledge and
its Limits, Oxford: Oxford University Press. doi:10.1093/019925656X.001.0001"
(DOI resolved 2026-09-27: ISBN 019925656X, OUP).

Relied on for: the knowledge-first thesis and its locus (2000); the
"most general factive mental state" claim; safety as a necessary condition
not an analysis; the entry authors' characterisation of how the case is
made.
Context: the second claim Williamson accepts is the evidence thesis
"S knows that p if and only if S’s total evidence includes the proposition
that p" (as printed, with S and p italic). Earlier (§ on safety) the entry
quotes Williamson 2009: 305 that one "may have to decide whether safety
obtains by first deciding whether knowledge obtains, rather than vice
versa".

## D. Self-Knowledge (Gertler)

Source: Brie Gertler, "Self-Knowledge", The Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition); first published Fri Feb 7, 2003;
  substantive revision Tue Nov 9, 2021. Section on acquaintance.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/self-knowledge/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped)

> "These arguments are responses to Williamson’s (2000) “anti-luminosity” argument, which seeks to establish that no mental states are luminous."  (acquaintance section)

Relied on for: the anti-luminosity argument (2000) and its critics
Weatherson 2004 ("Luminous Margins") and Duncan 2018.
Context: the preceding sentence: some philosophers argue "that at least
some mental states, such as intense sensations, may be “luminous”: that
is, that being in a state of that kind may ensure that one can know that
one is (Weatherson 2004; Duncan 2018)".

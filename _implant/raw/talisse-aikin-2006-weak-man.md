# Talisse & Aikin 2006 — two forms of the straw man: representation and selection

Source: Robert Talisse and Scott F. Aikin (2006), "Two Forms of the Straw
  Man", Argumentation 20(3): 345–352.
Original: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10503-006-9017-8
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (full text read in the Wayback Machine capture of
  the authors' Academia.edu posting,
  http://web.archive.org/web/20260217215840/https://www.academia.edu/654363/Two_Forms_of_the_Straw_Man
  — the page carries the typeset Springer text with running heads; page
  breaks located from those heads. DOI verified by content negotiation:
  Argumentation 20(3) 345–352, issued 2006-11-10.)

> "ABSTRACT: The authors identify and offer an analysis of a new form of
>  the Straw Man fallacy, and then explore the implications of the
>  prevalence of this fallacy for contemporary political discourse."
>  (p. 345)

> "According to a widely accepted characterization, one commits the straw
>  man fallacy when one misrepresents an opponent's position in a way that
>  imputes to it implausible commitments, and then refutes the
>  misrepresentation instead of the opponent's actual view."  (p. 345)

> "Specifically, we will introduce a distinction between two forms of the
>  straw man fallacy: the form of representation, and the form of
>  selection."  (p. 345)

> "On the standard analysis of the fallacy, the straw man occurs in an
>  adversarial argumentative context between two speakers (A and B), where
>  the proponent (A) represents her opponent's (B's) position in an
>  inaccurate way which facilitates or strengthens A's case against B."
>  (p. 346)

> "Consider that some proponent A can misrepresent the current dialectical
>  situation not with regard to what her current opponent B says, but with
>  regard to the variety and strength of opposition to her view, of which
>  B may only be a questionably relevant representative."  (p. 346)

> "A has erected a straw man, but she has not misrepresented the objection
>  she seeks to refute; instead, she has erected a straw man by selecting
>  a relatively weak version of, or inept spokesman for, the opposition to
>  her view. Hence there are two forms of the straw man: the
>  representation form, which is captured in the standard analysis of the
>  fallacy, and the selection form."  (p. 347)

> "By contrast, in the selection form of the straw man argument, A
>  correctly presents B's argument and legitimately refutes it, but she
>  fails to countenance stronger objections from other sources."  (p. 348)

> "It is our view that the selection form of the straw man is among the
>  most prevalent forms of fallacious argumentation at work in
>  contemporary popular political discourse."  (p. 349)

Relied on for: the two-form analysis of the straw man (representation vs
selection); the selection form as the origin of what later literature
calls the "weak man"; the claim that it is prevalent in political
discourse is the authors' own.
Context: A short paper. §I gives the standard textbook characterization
and announces the distinction; §II develops the selection form with the
A/B/C schema (A refutes B's weak objection while a stronger objection from
C exists) and distinguishes it from hasty generalization; §III argues the
selection form depends on audience ignorance of the opposition, and its
"only correction is education" in Mill's sense; §IV applies it to popular
political books. The phrase "weak man" does NOT occur anywhere in this
paper — the authors call it "the selection form"; the label "weak man" is
first attached to it in Aikin & Casey 2011 (see
aikin-casey-2011-hollow-man.md). The words "fallacious" and "fallacy" in
the quotations are the authors' own.

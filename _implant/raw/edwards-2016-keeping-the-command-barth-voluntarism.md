# Edwards 2016 — Keeping the Command: Barth's moral theology and voluntarism; critics and defenders

Source: James Andrew Edwards, "Keeping the Command: Karl Barth's Moral Theology in Dialogue with Late Medieval Scholasticism", PhD thesis, Wycliffe College / Toronto School of Theology, University of St. Michael's College, 2016 (© James Andrew Edwards 2016). Page numbers as printed in the PDF.

> "Due to its dogged persistence in using the language of “divine command,” Karl Barth’s moral theology has, until recent years, been accused of being overly “voluntarist.”"  (abstract, p. ii)

> "Recent scholars of Barth’s work, however, have attempted to exonerate his work, often by way of positive comparisons with the moral theology of Thomas Aquinas. Both perspectives, however, are inaccurate."  (abstract, p. ii; Edwards's assessment)

> "By weighting the discussion of the origin of goodness on the side of the divine will (rather than reason or nature), it is claimed that voluntarism places an inordinate and dangerous emphasis upon divine power, insufficient attention is granted to human freedom, and ethics are bound to an irrational and arbitrary command."  (p. 5; the charge as Edwards reports it)

> "interpreters of Barth’s ethics have either dismissed him due to his voluntarism or defended him from accusations of voluntarism."  (p. 6)

> "Biggar admits that Barth’s ethics have a strong voluntarist strain, but he goes on to demonstrate that this strain lacks consistency and that a rationalist strain appears occasionally—particularly in the later “special” ethics of creation and reconciliation."  (p. 9, on Nigel Biggar, The Hastening that Waits, 1993)

> "William Schweiker, after presenting a cursory overview of the history of voluntarism, claims that many such traditional positions (such as Barth’s) are “counterintuitive” from the perspective of contemporary ethics."  (pp. 8–9)

> "McKenny’s Barth is one who struggles and goes to great length to be consistently non-voluntarist."  (p. 12, on Gerald McKenny, The Analogy of Grace)

> "The outline of Barth’s ethical corpus appeared in his summer 1928 lectures on ethics (hereafter the Münster Ethics), later published as simply Ethics."  (p. 89)

> "Barth would follow the same structure (or “Way of Theological Ethics”) throughout his Church Dogmatics—God, creation, reconciliation, redemption—placing one section on the divine command at the end of each volume."  (p. 89)

> "With the Church Dogmatics left incomplete at the time of Barth’s death in"  (p. 89) / "1968, reconciliation ethics were never finished and redemption ethics never begun."  (p. 90; the sentence runs across the page break)

> "The good of human action consists in the fact that it is determined by the divine command."  (p. 1, epigraph quoting Barth, Church Dogmatics II/2, 547)

> "What this God determines is not a whim, and what He presents to man to do or not do is not the product of the fickle caprices of a tyrant whose right consists in his might. . . . God is faithful and constant."  (p. 104, quoting Barth, CD II/2, 676–77)

> "Schweiker’s concern is that an ethics of divine command cannot validate itself from within its own internal logic, but must also “assert that God is good or loving. Yet that claim, strictly speaking, cannot be formulated within the discourse of divine commands.”"  (p. 104, quoting Schweiker 1998, p. 247)

> "When God wills something from and for man, when man’s will is claimed by God, there can be no question of an arbitrary and purposeless control which God can exercise just because He is God and therefore superior to man."  (p. 106, quoting Barth, CD II/2, 562)

> "his adoption of Kierkegaard’s “infinite qualitative distinction” should be sufficient evidence for that."  (p. 189)

> "Barth’s own words regarding “an ancestral line which runs back through Kierkegaard to Luther and Calvin, and so to Paul and Jeremiah.” Quoted in Thomas F. Torrance, Karl Barth, 46."  (p. 15, n. 34)

> "A shared “grammar” of divine command both “from above” and “from below” is demonstrated to exist in the moral theologies of John Duns Scotus, William of Ockham, and Karl Barth."  (abstract, p. ii)

> "With the exception of Matheny, the only attention paid to Barth’s ethics prior to 1993 was critical."  (p. 8)

> "That Word of God is understood precisely as a command that claims humans as created by God, reconciled by God, and redeemed by God."  (p. 90, on the prolegomena of the Münster Ethics)

> "Barth’s theological voluntarism does not rely upon an irrational good defined by the whims of an arbitrary God."  (p. 104)

Relied on for: the place of ethics in the Church Dogmatics; Barth's own words from CD II/2 as Edwards quotes them (Bromiley–Torrance translation, Edinburgh: T & T Clark, 1957); the critics (Schweiker, Davaney, Case-Winters, Willis) and defenders (Biggar, McKenny, Rose, Webster) as Edwards reports them; Edwards's own thesis that Barth is a voluntarist in a non-arbitrary sense (attributed); Kierkegaard ties.
Context: Edwards defends the voluntarist tradition (Scotus, Ockham) and argues Barth belongs to it; he reads the CD II/2 passages as showing that Barth's voluntarism "does not rely upon an irrational good defined by the whims of an arbitrary God" (p. 104).

# Nozick 1974 — rights, side constraints, separateness of persons, the entitlement theory, Wilt Chamberlain, taxation and the framework for utopia (Anarchy, State, and Utopia)

Source: Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (New York: Basic Books,
  1974; Oxford: Blackwell reprint, ISBN 0-631-19780-X): Preface p. ix;
  ch. 3 "Moral Constraints and the State" pp. 29–33; ch. 7 "Distributive
  Justice" pp. 151, 161, 163, 169; ch. 10 "A Framework for Utopia" pp. 333–334.
Original: ISBN 0-631-19780-X; scan at
  https://archive.org/details/robert-nozick-anarchy-state-and-utopia
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (read in the archive.org OCR text of that scan; page
  numbers from the running heads. Words hyphenated across a line break are
  silently rejoined, e.g. "con- senting" as "consenting"; OCR "NDIVIDUALS"
  under a drop capital restored to "Individuals"; nothing else changed.)

> "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)."  (Preface, p. ix)

> "Our main conclusions about the state are that a minimal state, limited to the narrow functions of protection against force, theft, fraud, enforcement of contracts, and so on, is justified; that any more extensive state will violate persons’ rights not to be forced to do certain things, and is unjustified; and that the minimal state is inspiring as well as right."  (Preface, p. ix)

> "With reluctance, I found myself becoming convinced of (as they are now often called) libertarian views, due to various considerations and arguments."  (Preface, p. ix)

> "The question of whether these side constraints are absolute, or whether they may be violated in order to avoid catastrophic moral horror, and if the latter, what the resulting structure might look like, is one I hope largely to avoid."  (p. 30 n., footnote to p. 29)

> "Side constraints express the inviolability of other persons."  (p. 32)

> "But there is no social entity with a good that undergoes some sacrifice for its own good. There are only individual people, different individual people, with their own individual lives."  (pp. 32–33)

> "The complete principle of distributive justice would say simply that a distribution is just if everyone is entitled to the holdings they possess under the distribution."  (p. 151)

> "Is he entitled to this income? Is this new distribution D2, unjust? If so, why?"  (p. 161)

> "The socialist society would have to forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults."  (p. 163)

> "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor."  (p. 169)

> "The framework for utopia that we have described is equivalent to the minimal state."  (p. 333)

> "How dare any state or group of individuals do more. Or less."  (p. 334, the book's last sentence)

Relied on for: the opening claim about rights; the book's three main
  conclusions; Nozick's report of his own reluctant conversion; the
  side-constraint view and its unresolved "catastrophic moral horror"
  question; the separateness-of-persons argument; the entitlement principle;
  the Wilt Chamberlain question; "capitalist acts"; taxation and forced
  labour; the equivalence of the framework for utopia and the minimal state.
Context: p. 151 states the three-part inductive definition (acquisition,
  transfer, "No one is entitled to a holding except by (repeated)
  applications of 1 and 2") and then adds rectification. The Wilt
  Chamberlain case (pp. 160–164, section "How Liberty Upsets Patterns")
  starts from "your favorite" distribution D1: one million people each pay
  twenty-five cents, Chamberlain "winds up with $250,000". The forced-labour
  sentence (p. 169) is followed by "Some persons find this claim obviously
  true" and "Others find the claim absurd."

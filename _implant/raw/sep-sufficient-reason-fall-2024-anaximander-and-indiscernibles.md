# SEP "Principle of Sufficient Reason" (Melamed & Lin) — the PSR stated, Anaximander via De Caelo, Archimedes' balance, medieval freedom of indifference

Source: Yitzhak Y. Melamed & Martin Lin, "Principle of Sufficient Reason",
  The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N.
  Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first published 2010-09-14, substantive
  revision 2023-06-14. §§1, 3.3, 5.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/sufficient-reason/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read)

> "For every fact \(F\), there must be a sufficient reason why \(F\) is the case."  (§1, formulation (1))

> "If you accept an unrestricted form of the Principle of Sufficient Reason (= PSR), you will require an explanation for any fact, or in other words, you will reject the possibility of any brute, or unexplainable, facts."  (§1)

> "Anaximander, one of the earliest of the pre-Socratics, is usually credited—on the basis of Aristotle’s de Caelo, (b12 295b10–16)—with being the first to make use of it."  (§5)

> "Since he concludes from this that it does not move, we can assume that Anaximander believes that motion in the absence of a reason is impossible."  (§5)

> "Archimedes assumes that if there is a balance such that there is no difference between the weights and distances on either side, then neither side of the balance can move up or down. This is sometimes taken to be an implicit application of the PSR and is cited by Leibniz in his correspondence with Clarke as a precedent for his own principle."  (§5)

> "God, on the mainstream medieval view, enjoys freedom of indifference with respect to his creation."  (§5)

> "Thus there is no sufficient reason for why God created what he did and the PSR slips from prominence until its early modern revival at the hands of Spinoza and Leibniz."  (§5)

> "Leibniz also thinks that the PSR rules out the possibility that there could be two or more indistinguishable, that is, indiscernible, things (A VI, iv, 1541/AG 42)."  (§3.3)

Relied on for: a statement of the PSR; the Melamed–Lin reading of the De
Caelo indifference argument as an early use of the PSR; the Archimedean
balance as a precedent Leibniz cited (their report); their report that on
the medieval view God has freedom of indifference; Leibniz's derivation of
the identity of indiscernibles from the PSR (their report).
Context: The Anaximander paragraph opens §5 "The PSR before Spinoza and
Leibniz"; the medieval sentence follows their account of Abelard, whose
"opinion was rejected as heresy". The entry does not discuss Buridan's
ass by name.

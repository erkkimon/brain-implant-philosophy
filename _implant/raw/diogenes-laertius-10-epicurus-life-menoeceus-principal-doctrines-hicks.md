## Life, death, writings

> "Epicurus, son of Neocles and Chaerestrate, was a citizen of Athens of the deme Gargettus, and, as Metrodorus says in his book On Noble Birth, of the family of the Philaidae."  (X.1)

> "He was born, according to Apollodorus in his Chronology, in the third year of the 109th Olympiad, in the archonship of Sosigenes, on the seventh day of the month Gamelion, in the seventh year after the death of Plato."  (X.14; Hicks's footnote gives "341 B.C.")

> "When he was thirty-two he founded a school of philosophy, first in Mitylene and Lampsacus, and then five years later removed to Athens, where he died in the second year of the 127th Olympiad, in the archonship of Pytharatus, at the age of seventy-two; and Hermarchus the son of Agemortus, a Mitylenaean, took over the School."  (X.15)

> "Epicurus died of renal calculus after an illness which lasted a fortnight: so Hermarchus tells us in his letters."  (X.15)

> "On this blissful day, which is also the last of my life, I write this to you. My continual sufferings from strangury and dysentery are so great that nothing could augment them; but over against them all I set gladness of mind at the remembrance of our past conversations."  (X.22, letter to Idomeneus)

> "Epicurus was a most prolific author and eclipsed all before him in the number of his writings: for they amount to about three hundred rolls, and contain not a single citation from other authors; it is Epicurus himself who speaks throughout."  (X.26)

> "Of Nature, thirty-seven books."  (X.27, first item in Diogenes' list of "the best" of the writings)

## Letter to Menoeceus

> "Let no one be slow to seek wisdom when he is young nor weary in the search thereof when he is grown old. For no age is too early or too late for the health of the soul."  (X.122)

> "Not the man who denies the gods worshipped by the multitude, but he who affirms of the gods what the multitude believes about them is truly impious."  (X.123)

> "Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an illimitable time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality."  (X.124)

> "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not. It is nothing, then, either to the living or to the dead, for with the living it is not and the dead exist no longer."  (X.125)

> "We must also reflect that of desires some are natural, others are groundless; and that of the natural some are necessary as well as natural, and some natural only."  (X.127)

> "For the end of all our actions is to be free from pain and fear, and, when once we have attained all this, the tempest of the soul is laid"  (X.128)

> "Wherefore we call pleasure the alpha and omega of a blessed life."  (X.128)

> "While therefore all pleasure because it is naturally akin to us is good, not all pleasure is choiceworthy, just as all pain is an evil and yet not all pain is to be shunned."  (X.129)

> "When we say, then, that pleasure is the end and aim, we do not mean the pleasures of the prodigal or the pleasures of sensuality, as we are understood to do by some through ignorance, prejudice, or wilful misrepresentation. By pleasure we mean the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul."  (X.131)

> "Of all this the beginning and the greatest good is prudence."  (X.132)

> "For he sees that necessity destroys responsibility and that chance or fortune is inconstant; whereas our own actions are free, and it is to them that praise and blame naturally attach."  (X.133)

## Diogenes on the Cyrenaic contrast

> "He differs from the Cyrenaics with regard to pleasure. They do not include under the term the pleasure which is a state of rest, but only that which consists in motion. Epicurus admits both; also pleasure of mind as well as of body"  (X.136)

> "Peace of mind and freedom from pain are pleasures which imply a state of rest; joy and delight are seen to consist in motion and activity."  (X.136, quoting Epicurus, On Choice)

> "And we choose the virtues too on account of pleasure and not for their own sake, as we take medicine for the sake of health."  (X.138)

## Principal Doctrines (KD)

> "A blessed and eternal being has no trouble himself and brings no trouble upon any other being; hence he is exempt from movements of anger and partiality, for every such movement implies weakness"  (KD 1, X.139)

> "Death is nothing to us; for the body, when it has been resolved into its elements, has no feeling, and that which has no feeling is nothing to us."  (KD 2)

> "The magnitude of pleasure reaches its limit in the removal of all pain."  (KD 3)

> "It is impossible to live a pleasant life without living wisely and well and justly, and it is impossible to live wisely and well and justly without living pleasantly."  (KD 5)

> "Unlimited time and limited time afford an equal amount of pleasure, if we measure the limits of that pleasure by reason."  (KD 19, X.145)

> "Natural justice is a symbol or expression of expediency, to prevent one man from harming or being harmed by another."  (KD 31, X.150)

> "There never was an absolute justice, but only an agreement made in reciprocal intercourse in whatever localities now and again from time to time, providing against the infliction or suffering of harm."  (KD 33)

> "ἡ μὲν γὰρ ἀταραξία καὶ ἀπονία καταστηματικαί εἰσιν ἡδοναί· ἡ δὲ χαρὰ καὶ ἡ εὐφροσύνη κατὰ κίνησιν ἐνεργείᾳ βλέπονται."  (X.136)

> "νοουμένης δὲ ἡδονῆς τῆς τε κατὰ κίνησιν καὶ τῆς καταστηματικῆς."  (X.136, Metrodorus as quoted by Diogenes)

Hicks renders the first sentence "Peace of mind and freedom from pain are
pleasures which imply a state of rest; joy and delight are seen to consist
in motion and activity." — so ἀταραξία (ataraxia) is his "peace of mind",
ἀπονία (aponia) his "freedom from pain", καταστηματικαί (katastematic) his
"which imply a state of rest", κατὰ κίνησιν (kinetic) his "in motion".

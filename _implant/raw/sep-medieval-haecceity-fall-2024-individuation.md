# SEP Fall 2024 — Cross, "Medieval Theories of Haecceity": the scholastic problem of individuation

Source: Richard Cross, "Medieval Theories of Haecceity", *Stanford
  Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (Fall 2024 Edition), ed. Edward N. Zalta &
  Uri Nodelman; first published 31 Jul 2003, substantive revision
  18 Jan 2022. Preamble, §1, §4, §5.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/medieval-haecceity/
  (copyrighted, © 2022 Richard Cross; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (archive HTML fetched; quotations copied from the
  tag-stripped text)

> "First proposed by John Duns Scotus (1266–1308), a haecceity is a non-qualitative property responsible for a substance’s individuation and identity."  (preamble)

> "it is a “thisness” (a haecceitas, from the Latin haec, meaning “this”) as opposed to a “whatness” (a quidditas, from the Latin quid, meaning “what”)"  (preamble)

> "The condemned view was held by Christian Aristotelians in the thirteenth century – pre-eminently, Thomas Aquinas – and originated in the claim that intra-specific individuation is in some sense through different lumps of matter."  (preamble, on the 1277 Paris condemnation concerning angels)

> "angels are good cases for testing intuitions related to the identity of indiscernibles, and thus the question of individuation."  (preamble)

> "Scotus rejects the view that matter could be responsible for individuation, on the grounds that we require too an explanation for the individuation of (chunks of) matter"  (§1)

> "According to Fonseca, the principle of individuation is a certain positive difference, primarily incommunicable, which, when added to the species, in itself constitutes the individual … which difference others call haecceities."  (§4, Cross quoting Fonseca, In Met. V, c. 6, q. 5, sect. 1)

> "Maintaining that an object is singular or individual because it results from or is endowed with a proper principle of individuation which is itself singular, amounts to nothing more than duplicating what should be explained."  (§5, Cross quoting Nielsen 2003, 498, reporting Peter Auriol)

> "Ockham claims that a substance is singular through itself"  (§5)

Relied on for: the scholastic sense of individuation (what makes a
  substance this individual, distinct from others of its species); the
  rival answers — matter (Aquinas, as Cross reports the condemned view),
  haecceity (Scotus, Fonseca), and primitive singularity (Auriol, Ockham);
  the link to the identity of indiscernibles.
Context: §1 introduces haecceity through Max Black's two-spheres
  counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles. Cross notes that "In
  much modern literature, an item’s haecceity is simply the primitive
  property of being that item", which he contrasts with Scotus's
  explanatory use (§3).

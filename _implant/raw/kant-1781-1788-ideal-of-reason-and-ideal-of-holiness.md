# Kant 1781/1787 and 1788 — "Of the Ideal in General" (Critique of Pure Reason) and the "ideal of holiness" (Critique of Practical Reason)

> "But still further removed than the idea from objective reality is the Ideal, by which term I understand the idea, not in concreto, but in individuo—as an individual thing, determinable or determined by the idea alone."  (AA III 383, ll. 21–24: "was ich das Ideal nenne")

> "Without rising to these speculative heights, we are bound to confess that human reason contains not only ideas, but ideals, which possess, not, like those of Plato, creative, but certainly practical power—as regulative principles, and form the basis of the perfectibility of certain actions."  (AA III 384, ll. 3–6: "nicht allein Ideen, sondern auch Ideale enthalte")

> "But the wise man of the Stoics is an ideal, that is to say, a human being existing only in thought and in complete conformity with the idea of wisdom."  (AA III 384, ll. 13–15: "Aber der Weise (des Stoikers) ist ein Ideal")

> "As the idea provides a rule, so the ideal serves as an archetype for the perfect and complete determination of the copy."  (AA III 384, ll. 15–17: "so dient das Ideal in solchem Falle zum Urbilde")

> "Although we cannot concede objective reality to these ideals, they are not to be considered as chimeras; on the contrary, they provide reason with a standard, which enables it to estimate, by comparison, the degree of incompleteness in the objects presented to it."  (AA III 384, ll. 20–23: "Diese Ideale, ob man ihnen gleich")

> "This holiness of will is, however, a practical idea, which must necessarily serve as a type to which finite rational beings can only approximate indefinitely, and which the pure moral law, which is itself on this account called holy, constantly and rightly holds before their eyes."  (AA V 32, l. 35ff.: "Diese Heiligkeit des Willens ist gleichwohl")

> "That law of all laws, therefore, like all the moral precepts of the Gospel, exhibits the moral disposition in all its perfection, in which, viewed as an ideal of holiness, it is not attainable by any creature, but yet is the pattern which we should strive to approach, and in an uninterrupted but infinite progress become like to."  (AA V 83, ll. 23–26: "als ein Ideal der Heiligkeit von keinem Geschöpfe erreichbar")

Relied on for: Kant's technical definition of "Ideal" (idea in individuo)
and its practical, regulative role (the Stoic sage); holiness of will as a
type finite beings approximate; the "ideal of holiness" (the love
commandment) in the second Critique.
Context: the first-Critique section opens the chapter on the theological
ideal (the "ens realissimum"); the Stoic-sage passage continues that to
realise the ideal in a romance "is impracticable". The AA V 32 sentence
follows the remark that the holy will is "above obligation and duty"; the
AA V 83 passage interprets the command to love God and neighbour (ch. III,
"Of the Motives of Pure Practical Reason").

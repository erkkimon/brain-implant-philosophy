# Dershowitz 2002 — "Want to torture? Get a warrant" (torture warrants)

Source: Alan M. Dershowitz, "Want to torture? Get a warrant", San Francisco
  Chronicle, Open Forum, 22 January 2002.
Original: https://www.sfgate.com/opinion/openforum/article/want-to-torture-get-a-warrant-2880547.php
  (no Wayback snapshot found by the availability API on 2026-09-27)
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (HTML of the SFGate page read)

> "IF AMERICAN law enforcement officers were ever to confront the law school hypothetical case of the captured terrorist who knew about an imminent attack but refused to provide the information necessary to prevent it, I have absolutely no doubt that they would try to torture the terrorists into providing the information."

> "The real question is not whether torture would be used -- it would -- but whether it would be used outside of the law or within the law."

> "Under my proposal, no torture would be permitted without a "torture warrant" being issued by a judge."

> "An application for a torture warrant would have to be based on the absolute need to obtain immediate information in order to save lives coupled with probable cause that the suspect had such information and is unwilling to reveal it."

> "The warrant would limit the torture to nonlethal means, such as sterile needles, being inserted beneath the nails to cause excruciating pain without endangering life."

> "But consider the alternatives: Either police would torture below the radar screen of accountability, or the judge who issued the warrant would be accountable. Which would be more consistent with democratic values?"

> "Those opposed to the idea of a torture warrant argue -- quite reasonably -- that establishing such a precedent would legitimize torture and make it easier to extend its permissible use beyond the ticking bomb case."

> "Those who favor the torture warrant argue that the opposite would be true: By expressly limiting the use of torture only to the ticking bomb case and by requiring a highly visible judge to approve, limit and monitor the torture, it will be far more difficult to justify its extension to other institutions."

> "The goal of the warrant would be to reduce and limit the amount of torture that would, in fact, be used in an emergency."

Dershowitz as quoted in an interview by Dan Gardner, "Is Torture Always
Wrong?" (undated; read at
https://web.archive.org/web/20260606161211/https://www.dangardner.ca/article/is-torture-always-wrong ;
see also `posner-2004-torture-terrorism-interrogation-as-quoted.md`):

> "Mr. Dershowitz says he's not trying to resolve whether torture is morally right in some circumstances. "My position is that torture will, in fact, occur," particularly in a major crisis like a ticking-bomb scenario."

Relied on for: the torture-warrant proposal in Dershowitz's own words — the
predictive premise, the conditions for a warrant, the accountability argument,
and his statement of both sides of the precedent objection; his description,
in Gardner's interview, of his position as predictive rather than a moral
verdict (contrast SEP "Torture", preamble: "Thus Alan Dershowitz claims that
torture is morally justified in some extreme emergencies").
Context: the op-ed describes Israel's former "moderate physical pressure"
practice and the Israeli Supreme Court's 1999 ruling leaving open a necessity
defence in a "ticking bomb" case; it presents the proposal as from his book
Shouting Fire (Little, Brown, 2002). His book Why Terrorism Works (2002; SEP
cites the 2003 Scribe edition, ch. 4) was not read.

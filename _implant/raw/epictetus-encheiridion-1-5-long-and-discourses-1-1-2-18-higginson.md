> "Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion ([Greek: hupolaepsis]), movement towards a thing ([Greek: hormae]), desire, aversion ([Greek: echchlisis]), turning from a thing; and in a word, whatever are our acts. Not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts."  (Ench. 1, Long)

> "And the things in our power are by nature free, not subject to restraint or hindrance; but the things not in our power are weak, slavish, subject to restraint, in the power of others."  (Ench. 1, Long)

> "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things; for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death that it is terrible, is the terrible thing."  (Ench. 5, Long)

> "As it was fit, then, this most excellent and superior faculty alone, a right use of the appearances of things, the gods have placed in our own power; but all other matters they have not placed in our power."  (Disc. 1.1, Higginson)

> "O Epictetus, if it had been possible, I had made this little body and property of thine free, and not liable to hindrance. But now do not mistake; it is not thy own, but only a finer mixture of clay."  (Disc. 1.1, Higginson; Zeus speaking)

> "You will fetter my leg, but not Zeus himself can get the better of my free will."  (Disc. 1.1, Higginson)

> "But in the first place, be not hurried away by excitement; but say, Semblance, wait for me a little. Let me see what you are, and what you represent. Let me try you."  (Disc. 2.18, Higginson)

> "What is the first business of one who studies philosophy? To part with self-conceit. For it is impossible for any one to begin to learn what he thinks that he already knows."  (Disc. 2.17, Higginson)

Relied on for: the dichotomy of what is and is not in our power
(Ench. 1); "not by things" (Ench. 5); Zeus unable to make the body free
"if it had been possible" and unable to overpower the will (Disc. 1.1);
testing impressions (Disc. 2.18); self-conceit as a bar to learning (Disc. 2.17).
Context: Ench. 1 goes on to say that whoever takes only what is his own
for his own "will never blame any man". Disc. 1.1 opens on the rational
faculty as the only one that "contemplates both itself and all other
things" (Long's wording) and closes with the anecdote of Agrippinus.
In 2.18 the passage follows advice to "conquer any alluring semblance".

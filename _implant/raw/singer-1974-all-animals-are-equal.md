# Singer 1974 — "All Animals Are Equal": equal consideration of interests, sentience as the boundary, speciesism, the orphaned-infant comparison

> "The extension of the basic principle of equality from one group to another does not imply that we must treat both groups in exactly the same way, or grant exactly the same rights to both groups."  (p. 104)

> "It is an implication of this principle of equality that our concern for others 106 Published by Digital Commons @Brockport, 1974 5 Philosophic Exchange, Vol. 5 [1974], No. 1, Art. 6 PETER SINGER ought not lo depend on what they are like, or what abilities they possess-although precisely what this concern requires us to do may vary according to the characteristics of those affected by what we do. It is on this basis that the case against racism and the case against sexism must both ultimately rest; and it is in accordance with this principle that speciesism is also to be condemned. If possessing a higher degree of intelligence does not entitle one human to use another for his own ends, how can it entitle humans to exploit non-humans?"  (pp. 106–107; the page-break furniture of the PDF is kept as extracted)

> "If a being suffers, there can be no moral justification for refusing to take that suffering into consideration."  (p. 107)

> "The capacity for suffering and enjoying things is a pre-requisite for having interests at all, a condition that must be satisfied before we can speak of interests in any meaningful way."  (p. 107)

> "This is why the limit of sentience (using the term as a convenient, if not strictly accurate, shorthand for the capacity to suffer or experience enjoyment or happiness) is the only defensible boundary of concern for the interests of others. To mark this boundary by some characteristic like intemgence or rationality would be to mark it in an arbitrary way."  (p. 108)

> "If the experimenter is not prepared to use an orphaned human infant, then his readiness to use nonhumans is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing and!, so far as we can tell, at least as sensitive to pain, as any human infant."  (p. 110)

> "I am thinking of humans with severe and irreparable brain damage, and also of infant humans. To avoid the complication of the relevance of a being's potential, however, I shall henceforth concentrate on permanently retarded humans."  (p. 113)

Relied on for: Singer's principle of equal consideration of interests
extended beyond the species; sentience as the boundary; his use of human
infants and cognitively disabled humans in the comparison (the argument
later called "from marginal cases").
Context: the paper was reprinted in revised form as ch. 1 of Animal
Liberation (1975), which was not read. Pp. 106–107 quote the same
Bentham note (raw/bentham-1789-principles-ch17-can-they-suffer.md). The
wording "permanently retarded humans" is Singer's 1974 wording. On
p. 110 Singer adds a parenthesis on the potential of infants and its
consequences for abortion. Gruen & Monsó (SEP 2024 §1.1) quote p. 108 on
the racist/speciesist parallel.

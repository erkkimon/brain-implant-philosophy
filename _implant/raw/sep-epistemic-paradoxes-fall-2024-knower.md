> "David Kaplan and Richard Montague (1960) think the announcement by the teacher in our surprise exam example is equivalent to the self-referential"  (§5.1; followed by (K-3))

> "Kaplan and Montague note that the number of alternative test dates can be increased indefinitely. Shockingly, they claim the number of alternatives can be reduced to zero! The announcement is then equivalent to"  (§5.1)

> "(K-0) This sentence is known to be false."  (§5.1)

> "If (K-0) is true then it known to be false. Whatever is known to be false, is false. Since no proposition can be both true and false, we have proven that (K-0) is false. Given that proof produces knowledge, (K-0) is known to be false. But wait! That is exactly what (K-0) says – so (K-0) must be true."  (§5.1; "it known" sic)

> "The (K-0) argument bears a suspicious resemblance to the liar paradox."  (§5.1)

> "Subsequent commentators sloppily switch the negation sign in the formal presentations of the reasoning"  (§5.1; from K~p to ~Kp)

> "Ironically, this garbled transmission results in a cleaner variation of the knower:"  (§5.1)

> "(K) No one knows this very sentence."  (§5.1)

> "Is (K) true? On the one hand, if (K) is true, then what it says is true, so no one knows it. On the other hand, that very reasoning seems to be a proof of (K). Believing a proposition by seeing it to be proved is a sufficient for knowledge of it, so someone must know (K). But then (K) is false! Since no one can know a proposition that is false, (K) is not known."  (§5.1)

> "The skeptic could hope to solve (K-0) by denying that anything is known. This remedy does not cure (K). If nothing is known then (K) is true."  (§5.1)

> "Can the skeptic instead challenge the premise that proven is a sufficient for knowing it?"  (§5.1)

> "But the skeptic should not lose his nerve. Proof does not always yield knowledge."  (§5.1)

> "Consider a student who correctly guesses that a step in his proof is valid. The student does not know the conclusion but did prove the theorem."  (§5.1)

> "An excessively high standard of proof can prevent knowledge by proof."  (§5.1)

## Gellius V.10

> "On the arguments which by the Greeks are called ἀντιστρέφοντα, and in Latin may be termed reciproca."  (chapter title)

> "Among fallacious arguments the one which the Greeks call ἀντιστρέφων seems to be by far the most fallacious."  (§1)

> "The fallacy arises from the fact that the argument that is presented may be turned in the opposite direction and used against the one who has offered it, and is equally strong for both sides of the question. An example is the well-known argument which Protagoras, the keenest of all sophists, is said to have used against his pupil Euathlus."  (§3)

> "He paid half of the amount at once, before beginning his lessons, and agreed to pay the remaining half on the day when he first pleaded before jurors and won his case."  (§6)

> "And when the time was already getting long, and he seemed to be acting thus in order not to pay the rest of the fee,"  (§7)

> "Protagoras formed what seemed to him at the time a wily scheme; he determined to demand his pay according to the contract, and brought suit against Euathlus."  (§8)

> "Let me tell you, most foolish of youths, that in either event you will have to pay what I am demanding, whether judgment be pronounced for or against you."  (§9, Protagoras)

> "For if the case goes against you, the money will be due me in accordance with the verdict, because I have won; but if the decision be in your favour, the money will be due me according to our contract, since you will have won a case."  (§10, Protagoras)

> "I might have met this sophism of yours, tricky as it is, by not pleading my own cause but employing another as my advocate."  (§11, Euathlus)

> "So let me tell you in turn, wisest of masters, that in either event I shall not have to pay what you demand, whether judgment be pronounced for or against me."  (§13, Euathlus)

> "For if the jurors decide in my favour, according to their verdict nothing will be due you, because I have won; but if they give judgment against me, by the terms of our contract I shall owe you nothing, because I have not won a case."  (§14, Euathlus)

> "Then the jurors, thinking that the plea on both sides was uncertain and insoluble, for fear that their decision, for whichever side it was rendered, might annul itself, left the matter undecided and postponed the case to a distant day."  (§15)

> "Thus a celebrated master of oratory was refuted by his youthful pupil with his own argument, and his cleverly devised sophism failed."  (§16)

## Gellius V.11.1

> "Some think that the famous answer of the wise and noble Bias, like that of Protagoras of which I have just spoken, was ἀντιστρέφων."  (V.11.1; the chapter title argues "The impossibility of regarding Bias's syllogism on marriage as an example of ἀντιστρέφων.")

## Diogenes Laertius IX.56 (Hicks tr.)

> "The story is told that once, when he asked Euathlus his disciple for his fee, the latter replied, “But I have not won a case yet.” “Nay,” said Protagoras, “if I win this case against you I must have the fee, for winning it; if you win, I must have it, because you win it.”"  (IX.56)

> "His accuser was Pythodorus, son of Polyzelus, one of the four hundred; Aristotle, however, says it was Euathlus."  (IX.54, on the reading of *On the Gods*)

Relied on for: the story, the two dilemmas, the jurors' postponement and
Gellius's classification of the argument as ἀντιστρέφων / reciproca
("convertible"); Diogenes's shorter version, in which only Protagoras's
dilemma is given.
Context: Gellius V.10 is a chapter on convertible arguments; V.11 goes on to
deny that Bias's argument on marriage is one. In Diogenes, IX.56 is an
anecdote at the end of the life of Protagoras, after the reports of his death.

> "let’s explore the apportionment paradox , a situation that occurs when an apportionment method produces results that seem to contradict reasonable expectations of fairness."

> "The chief clerk of the Census Bureau calculated the apportionments for house sizes between 275 and 350. There was a surprising result that became known as the Alabama paradox , which is said to occur when an increase in house size reduces a state’s quota. Alabama would receive eight seats with a house size of 299, but only receive seven seats if the house size increased to 300."  (cites Michael J. Caulfield, "Apportioning Representatives in the United States Congress - Paradoxes of Apportionment", Convergence, November 2010, DOI 10.4169/loci003163)

> "The 1880 census recorded the population of Alabama as 1,513,401 and that of the U.S. as 62,979,766."  (Example 1)

> "The standard quota for Alabama is 1,513,401 ÷ 210,634.7 = 7.1850"  (Example 1, house size 299; the page renders each formula twice)

> "The standard quota for Alabama is 1,513,401 ÷ 209,932.6 = 7.2090"  (Example 1, house size 300)

> "It must have been the case that either the fractional part 0.2090 ranked lower amongst the other fractional parts of the state quotas than the fractional part 0.1850 did, or there were fewer remaining seats, or both."  (Example 1, answer)

> "It was determined that Colorado would receive three seats with a house size of 356, but only two seats with a house size of 357."  (after the 1900 census)

> "The House size was increased from 386 to 391 to accommodate Oklahoma’s quota of five seats. When the seats were reapportioned using Hamilton’s method, New York lost a seat to Maine despite the fact that their populations had not changed."  ("The New-States Paradox")

> "It turns out that, although all three of these divisor methods violate the quota rule, none of them ever causes the population paradox, new-states paradox, or even the Alabama paradox. On the other hand, the Hamilton method satisfies the quota rule, but will cause the population paradox, the new-states paradox, and the Alabama paradox in some scenarios."  (the three are Jefferson, Adams, Webster)

> "In 1983, mathematicians Michel Balinski and Peyton Young proved that no method of apportionment can simultaneously satisfy all four fairness criteria."  (the four: satisfy the quota rule; avoid the Alabama, population and new-states paradoxes)

> "However, no method may always follow the quota rule and simultaneously be free of the population paradox."  (cites Balinski and Young 1980, IIASA WP-80-131)

> "do not look for a perfect apportionment method. Instead, look for an apportionment method that best meets the needs and concerns of Imaginarians."

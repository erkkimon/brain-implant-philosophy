> "(1) I went to the pictures last Tuesday but I don’t believe that I did. (1942: 543) (2) I believe that he has gone out, but he has not. (1944: 204)"  (§2, ms. p. 6)

> "(3) p, but I don’t believe that p. (The omissive form.) (4) I believe that p, but it’s not the case that p. (The commissive form.)"  (§2, ms. p. 6)

> "10 The paradox was named by Wittgenstein 1953: 190. Moore mentions the paradox in his 1942: 540-3 and 1944: 204, but his most detailed discussion is in Moore 1993. See Green and Williams 2007 for a historical introduction to Moore’s paradox."  (n. 10)

> "There is a paradox here because asserting Moorean sentences seems absurd or self-defeating in much the same way as asserting a contradiction, and yet Moorean assertions are not contradictions; after all, they can be true."  (§2, ms. p. 7)

> "As Moore says, “It is a paradox that it should be perfectly absurd to utter assertively words of which the meaning is something which may well be true – is not a contradiction” (1993: 209)."  (§2, ms. p. 7; Moore 1993 itself not read)

> "If Moore’s paradox is not a purely linguistic phenomenon, then it cannot be given a purely linguistic solution. This undermines many of the earliest solutions to Moore’s paradox, including those originally proposed by Moore and Wittgenstein."  (§2.1)

> "In contrast, Wittgenstein claims that in asserting that I believe that p, I thereby assert that p. On this view, one asserts a contradiction by asserting a conjunction of the commissive form. Inspired by Wittgenstein, Jane Heal (1994) claims that asserting that I believe that p functions to express and not merely to report the belief that p."  (§2.1; footnote marker 12 after "that p." omitted; n. 12 "See Wittgenstein 1953: 190-2 and 1980: 90-6.")

> "On the one hand, the Moorean solution cannot easily be extended from the omissive form to the commissive form. Does asserting that p express not only that I believe that p, but also that I don’t believe its negation? On the other hand, the Wittgensteinian solution cannot easily be extended from the commissive form to the omissive form."  (§2.1)

> "Wittgenstein gives the example of a railway employee who concludes his announcement of the schedule with the skeptical disclaimer, “Personally, I don’t believe it” (1980: 84)."  (§2.1)

> "As Sydney Shoemaker writes, “An explanation of why one cannot (coherently) assert a Moore-paradoxical sentence will come along for free, via the principle that what can be (coherently) believed constrains what can be (coherently) asserted” (1996: 76)."  (§2.1)

> "We can draw a distinction between psychological solutions, which claim that believing Moorean conjunctions is psychologically impossible, and epistemological solutions, which claim that believing Moorean conjunctions is epistemically irrational."  (§2.2)

> "13 See Hintikka 1962: 67 and Shoemaker 1996: 85-6 for the claim that it’s impossible to believe an omissive Moorean conjunction."  (n. 13)

> "Finally, Roy Sorensen notes that there are Moorean sentences that have neither omissive nor commissive forms, although they entail sentences with omissive or commissive forms."  (§2.2; examples "(5) God knows that we are atheists. (1988: 17)" and "(7) God exists, but I don’t believe that God exists.")

> "John Williams (1994: 165) argues that it’s always irrational to believe an omissive Moorean conjunction, p and I don’t believe that p, because it is self-falsifying in the sense that believing the conjunction makes it false."  (§2.3)

> "Claudio de Almeida (2001: 41) rejects Williams’ proposal on the grounds that one can rationally believe necessary falsehoods."  (§2.3)

> "Timothy Williamson argues that believing omissive Moorean conjunctions is irrational because they cannot be known (2000: 253-4). Knowing that p requires truly believing that p, but you can’t truly believe an omissive Moorean conjunction, since believing the conjunction makes it false."  (§2.4)

> "The knowledge rule: one should believe p only if one knows p. (2000: 255-6)"  (§2.4)

> "Like many others, I reject the knowledge rule on the grounds that one can rationally believe that p without knowing that p in deception cases in which it’s false that p or Gettier cases in which it’s accidentally true that p."  (§2.4)

> "Sydney Shoemaker (1996: 76) notes that one cannot self-consciously believe an omissive Moorean conjunction without thereby having contradictory beliefs."  (§2.5)

> "As Shoemaker puts the point, “if one believes something, and considers whether one does, one must, on pain of irrationality, believe that one believes it” (1996: 77)."  (§2.5)

> "It is sometimes assumed that a solution to Moore’s paradox should give a unified treatment of omissive and commissive forms."  (§2.7; n. 28 "See, for example, Green and Williams 2007.")

> "I’ve argued that believing omissive Moorean conjunctions is always irrational. In contrast, I’ll argue that believing commissive Moorean conjunctions is sometimes rational, but only when one has contradictory beliefs."  (§2.7)

> "The rational biconditional thesis: necessarily, if one is rational, and one has some doxastic attitude towards the proposition that one believes that p, then one believes that p if and only if one believes that one believes that p."  (§2.8)

Context: Smithies' paper argues that "the simple theory of introspection"
  explains why Moorean belief is always associated with irrationality; §2
  is his survey of solutions before his own. His own verdicts ("Like many
  others, I reject …") are his.

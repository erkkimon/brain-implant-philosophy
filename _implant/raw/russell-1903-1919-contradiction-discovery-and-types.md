# Russell 1903 and 1919 — "The Contradiction", the doctrine of types, the note on Frege's vol. II, and the 1901 discovery account

Source: Bertrand Russell, The Principles of Mathematics, vol. I,

  (public domain in the US; excerpts only)

  checked against the page images; page numbers read from the running

> "In the case of classes, I must confess, I have failed to perceive any concept fulfilling the conditions requisite for the notion of class. And the contradiction discussed in Chapter x. proves that something is amiss, but what this is I have hitherto failed to discover."  (Preface, pp. v–vi)

> "Before taking leave of fundamental questions, it is necessary to examine more in detail the singular contradiction, already mentioned, with regard to predicates not predicable of themselves."  (§100, p. 101)

> "I may mention that I was led to it in the endeavour to reconcile Cantor's proof that there can be no greatest cardinal number with the very plausible supposition that the class of all terms (which we have seen to be essential to all formal propositions) has necessarily the greatest possible number of members"  (§100, p. 101)

> "Note. The second volume of Gg., which appeared too late to be noticed in the Appendix, contains an interesting discussion of the contradiction (pp. 253—265), suggesting that the solution is to be found by denying that two propositional functions which determine equal classes must be equivalent. As it seems very likely that this is the true solution, the reader is strongly recommended to examine Frege's argument on the point."  (Appendix A, end, p. 522)

> "The doctrine of types is here put forward tentatively, as affording a possible solution of the contradiction"  (Appendix B, §497, p. 523)

> "To sum up: it appears that the special contradiction of Chapter x is solved by the doctrine of types, but that there is at least one closely analogous contradiction which is probably not soluble by this doctrine."  (Appendix B, §500, p. 528)

## B. Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy (1919)

Source: Bertrand Russell, Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy, London:
  George Allen & Unwin, 1919 (Muirhead Library of Philosophy), ch. XIII
  "The Axiom of Infinity and Logical Types", p. 136.
Original: https://archive.org/details/introductiontoma00russ (scan of a
  later impression of the Allen & Unwin edition, archive.org dates it 1919)
  (copyright status varies by jurisdiction; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (read in the archive.org OCR text
  introductiontoma00russ_djvu.txt; page number from the running head)

> "When I first came upon this contradiction, in the year 1901, I attempted to discover some flaw in Cantor's proof that there is no greatest cardinal, which we gave in Chapter VIII."  (ch. XIII, p. 136)

> "Form now the assemblage of all classes which are not members of themselves. This is a class: is it a member of itself or not? If it is, it is one of those classes that are not members of themselves, i.e. it is not a member of itself. If it is not, it is not one of those classes that are not members of themselves, i.e. it is a member of itself. Thus of the two hypotheses—that it is, and that it is not, a member of itself—each implies its contradictory. This is a contradiction."  (ch. XIII, p. 136)

> "The following theory of symbolic logic recommended itself to me in the first instance by its ability to solve certain contradictions, of which the one best known to mathematicians is Burali-Forti's concerning the greatest ordinal."  (p. 222)

> "(2) Let w be the class of all those classes which are not members of themselves. Then, whatever class x may be, "x is a w" is equivalent to "x is not an x." Hence, giving to x the value w, "w is a w" is equivalent to "w is not a w.""  (§I, p. 222)

> "In all the above contradictions (which are merely selections from an indefinite number) there is a common characteristic, which we may describe as self-reference or reflexiveness."  (§I, p. 224)

> "This leads us to the rule: "Whatever involves all of a collection must not be one of the collection;""  (§I, p. 225)

> "These fallacies, as we saw, are to be avoided by what may be called the "vicious-circle principle;" i.e., "no totality can contain members defined in terms of itself.""  (p. 237)

Relied on for: Russell's own statements of the paradox (1903, 1908, 1919)
and of how he came to it (Cantor's no-greatest-cardinal proof, 1901);
the tentative 1903 doctrine of types and its self-declared limit; the 1908
vicious-circle principle; Russell's 1903 note endorsing Frege's vol. II
suggestion.
Context: the 1903 contradiction is stated first for class-concepts and
predicates, the class version following; the 1903 Appendix B closes with a
propositions paradox Russell thought types probably did not solve (the SEP
says it led to the ramified theory of 1908). The OCR quotations were
normalised only for line-break hyphenation and doubled spaces. Page 223 of
the 1908 paper begins mid-quotation (2); the list opens on p. 222.

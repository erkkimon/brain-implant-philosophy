> "EVERSINCE mathematicians first began to study the measurement of risk there has been general agreement on the following proposition: Expected values are computed by multiplying each possible gain by the number of ways in which it can occur, and then dividing the sum of these products by the total number of possible cases"  (§1, p. 23; "EVERSINCE" as extracted)

> "To do this the determination of the value of an item must not be based on its price, but rather on the utility it yields. The price of the item is dependent only on the thing itself and is equal for everyone; the utility, however, is dependent on the particular circumstances of the person making the estimate."  (§3, p. 24)

> "Thus there is no doubt that a gain of one thousand ducats is more significant to a pauper than to a rich man though both gain the same amount."  (§3, p. 24)

> "Now it is highly probable that any increase in wealth, no matter how insignificant, will always result in an increase in utility which is inversely proportionate to the quantity of goods already possessed."  (§5, p. 25)

> "The curve sBS is therefore a logarithmic curve"  (§10, p. 28; from dy = b dx/x, y = b log x/a)

> "Peter tosses a coin and continues to do so until it should land "heads" when it comes to the ground. He agrees to give Paul one ducat if he gets "heads" on the very first throw, two ducats if he gets it on the second, four if on the third, eight if on the fourth, and so on, so that with each additional throw the number of ducats he must pay is doubled."  (§17, p. 31)

> "Although the standard calculation shows that the value of Paul's expectation is infinitely great, it has, he said, to be admitted that any fairly reasonable man would sell his chance, with great pleasure, for twenty ducats."  (§17, p. 31; "he" is Nicolas Bernoulli)

> "If Paul owned nothing at all the value of his expectation would be [formula] which amounts to two ducats, precisely. If he owned ten ducats his opportunity would be worth approximately three ducats; it would be worth approximately four if his wealth were one hundred, and six if he possessed one thousand."  (§19, pp. 32; the displayed formula did not survive text extraction)

> "Paul's total expectation is therefore [1/2 + 1/2 + …], and that is infinite -Karl Menger."  (footnote 9, p. 31, Menger; the bracketed series is garbled in the extraction and is reconstructed from the same footnote's preceding sentence, that heads on the n-th throw "contributes" 1/2 ducat to the expectation)

> "In a letter to me written in 1732 he declared that he was in no way dissatisfied with my proposition on the evaluation of risky propositions when applied to the case of a man who is to evaluate his own prospects. However, he thinks that the case is different if a third person, somewhat in the position of a judge, is to evaluate the prospects of any participant in a game in accord with equity and justice."  (p. 33)

> "Then this distinguished scholar informed me that the celebrated mathematician, Cramer, had developed a theory on the same subject several years before I produced my paper. Indeed I have found his theory so similar to mine that it seems miraculous that we independently reached such close agreement on this sort of subject."  (p. 33)

> "Thus far the exposition is somewhat vague and subject to counter argument."  (p. 34, Bernoulli on Cramer's cap at 2^24; OCR "T h u s furl4")

> "I refer to the proposition that reasonable men should evaluate money in accord with the utility they derive therefrom."  (p. 34)

> "Since the number of cases is infinite, it is impossible to speak about one half of the cases, one quarter of the cases, etc., and the letter N in Bernoulli's argument is meaningless."  (footnote 10, p. 32, Menger)

> "The rule established in §1 must, therefore, be discarded."  (§3, p. 24; "§" is "$" in the extraction)

> "D = (a + 1)^(1/2)(a + 2)^(1/4) ....(a + 2^(n-1))^(1/2^n).... - a."  (footnote 10, p. 32, Menger: the addition D to Paul's fortune a equal in value to the game under Bernoulli's hypothesis; exponents re-typed from the garbled extraction "( a 1)'12(a + 2)'/4 ....(a + 2"-l)llzn.... - a")

> "Cramer, Gabriel, famous mathematician, born in Geneva, Switzerland (1704-1752)."  (footnote 12, p. 33, translator)

> "The work referred to here is the then famous "Essai d'analyse sur les jeux de hazard," Paris, 1708. Appended to the second edition, published in 1713, is Montmort's correspondence with Jean and Nicolas Bernoulli"  (footnote 8, p. 31, translator)

Relied on for: Bernoulli's utility-not-price rule; the hypothesis that utility increments are inversely proportional to wealth, giving a logarithmic curve; his statement of the game (1 ducat for heads on the first throw, doubling) and attribution to Nicolas Bernoulli via Montmort p. 402; the values 2, ≈3, ≈4, ≈6 ducats for fortunes 0, 10, 100, 1000; Nicolas's 1732 equity objection as Daniel reports it; the acknowledgement of Cramer; Menger's footnotes.
Context: §§1–2 state the received rule of expected value; §§3–16 develop the utility theory with insurance and diversification examples (Caius the Petersburg merchant, §15); §17 introduces the problem "which inspired these thoughts"; §§18–19 solve it; the paper closes by quoting Cramer's 1728 letter (in French in the original, per translator's note 13). Note: Bernoulli's payoff schedule starts at 1 ducat (2^(n-1)), the SEP modern schedule at $2 (2^n).

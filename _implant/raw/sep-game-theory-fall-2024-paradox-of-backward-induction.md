# SEP "Game Theory" (Ross) — backward induction and the "paradox of backward induction"

Source: Don Ross, "Game Theory", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published 1997-01-25, substantive revision 2023-09-03. §§2.3, 2.8.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/game-theory/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (archived page fetched with curl, HTML stripped,
  read; quotations checked by script against the stripped text.)

> "This process is called backward induction (because the reasoning works backwards from eventual outcomes to present choice problems)."  (§2.3)

> "Both players use backward induction to solve the game; backward induction requires that Player I know that Player II knows that Player I is economically rational; but Player II can solve the game only by using a backward induction argument that takes as a premise the failure of Player I to behave in accordance with economic rationality. This is the paradox of backward induction."  (§2.8)

> "A standard way around this paradox in the literature is to invoke the so-called ‘trembling hand’ due to Selten (1975)."  (§2.8)

> "Gintis (2009a) points out that the apparent paradox does not arise merely from our supposing that both players are economically rational."  (§2.8)

> "The paradox of backward induction, like the puzzles raised by equilibrium refinement, is mainly a problem for those who view game theory as contributing to a normative theory of rationality (specifically, as contributing to that larger theory the theory of strategic rationality)."  (§2.8)

> "The paradox of backward induction is one of a family of paradoxes that arise if one builds possession and use of literally complete information into a concept of rationality."  (§2.8)

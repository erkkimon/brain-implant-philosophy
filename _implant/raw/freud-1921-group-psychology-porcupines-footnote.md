# Freud 1921 — the porcupine simile in Massenpsychologie und Ich-Analyse, ch. VI

Source: Sigmund Freud, *Massenpsychologie und Ich-Analyse*, Leipzig/Wien/Zürich:
Internationaler Psychoanalytischer Verlag, 1921, ch. VI "Weitere Aufgaben und
Arbeitsrichtungen" (the parable is in the main text in German); English:
*Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego*, tr. James Strachey, London/Vienna:
International Psycho-Analytical Press, 1922, ch. VI "Further Problems and Lines
of Work", text and footnote 34 (the translator's note says passages were moved
from text to footnotes "at the author's express desire").

German, ch. VI:

> "Wir halten uns vor, wie sich die Menschen im allgemeinen affektiv zueinander verhalten. Nach dem berühmten _Schopenhauer_'schen Gleichnis von den frierenden Stachelschweinen verträgt keiner eine allzu intime Annäherung des anderen."

(The German text then quotes the first sentences of §396, ending "bis sie eine mäßige Entfernung herausgefunden hatten, in der sie es am besten aushalten konnten.« (Parerga und Paralipomena, II. Teil, XXXI., Gleichnisse und Parabeln.)")

Strachey 1922, Translator's Note:

> "A comparison of the following pages with the German original (_Massenpsychologie und Ich-Analyse_, Internationaler Psychoanalytischer Verlag, Vienna, 1921) will show that certain passages have been transferred in the English version from the text to the footnotes. This alteration has been carried out at the author's express desire."

Strachey 1922, ch. VI:

> "According to Schopenhauer's famous simile of the freezing porcupines no one can tolerate a too intimate approach to his neighbour."

> "The evidence of psycho-analysis shows that almost every intimate emotional relation between two people which lasts for some time--marriage, friendship, the relations between parents and children[35]--leaves a sediment of feelings of aversion and hostility, which have first to be eliminated by repression."

Strachey 1922, footnote 34 (start and end):

> "'A company of porcupines crowded themselves very close together one cold winter's day so as to profit by one another's warmth and so save themselves from being frozen to death."

> "So that they were driven backwards and forwards from one trouble to the other, until they had discovered a mean distance at which they could most tolerably exist.'"

Strachey 1922, ch. VI, a few paragraphs on:

> "When this hostility is directed against people who are otherwise loved we describe it as ambivalence of feeling"

> "But the whole of this intolerance vanishes, temporarily or permanently, as the result of the formation of a group, and in a group."

Relied on for: Freud's use of the simile, its locator, the claim he
illustrates with it (aversion and hostility in lasting intimate relations),
and his proposed counterweight (group formation, libidinal ties).
Context: chapter VI turns from the two "artificial groups" (church, army)
to the emotional relations between people in general; after the porcupine
passage Freud discusses narcissism and states that only a libidinal tie
limits it.

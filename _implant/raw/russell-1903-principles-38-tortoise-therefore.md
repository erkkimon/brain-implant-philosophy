# Russell 1903 — Principles of Mathematics §38: Carroll's Tortoise and the notion of "therefore"

Source: Bertrand Russell, *The Principles of Mathematics*, Cambridge
  University Press, 1903, ch. III "Implication and Formal Implication", § 38
  (n. 35 refers to "Mind, N. S. Vol. IV, p. 278").

> "The independence of this principle is brought out by a consideration of Lewis Carroll's puzzle, What the Tortoise said to Achilles"  (§ 38 ¶ 2)

> "At first sight, it might be thought that this would enable us to assert q provided p is true and implies q. But the puzzle in question shows that this is not the case, and that, until we have some new principle, we shall only be led into an endless regress of more and more complicated implications, without ever arriving at the assertion of q."  (§ 38 ¶ 2)

> "We need, in fact, the notion of therefore, which is quite different from the notion of implies, and holds between different entities."  (§ 38 ¶ 2)

> "This principle, it was observed, eludes formal statement, and points to a certain failure of formalism in general."  (§ 38 ¶ 1; of the principle "that if the hypothesis of an implication is true, it may be dropped, and the consequent asserted")

Relied on for: Russell's reading of the puzzle (an independent principle of
inference; "therefore" distinct from "implies"; asserted vs. considered
propositions).
Context: § 38 opens on "the distinction between a proposition actually
asserted, and a proposition considered merely as a complex concept"; Russell
says that "there are grave difficulties in forming a consistent theory on
this point".

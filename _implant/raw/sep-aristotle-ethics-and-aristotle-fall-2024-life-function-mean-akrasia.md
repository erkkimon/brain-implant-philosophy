## A. Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics"

Source: Richard Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", The Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.);
  "First published Tue May 1, 2001; substantive revision Sat Jul 2, 2022";

  Bekker numbers in parentheses are Kraut's.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/aristotle-ethics/

> "Aristotle wrote two ethical treatises: the Nicomachean Ethics and the Eudemian Ethics."  (§1)

> "The words “Eudemian” and “Nicomachean” were added later, perhaps because the former was edited by his friend, Eudemus, and the latter by his son, Nicomachus."  (§1)

> "it is widely assumed that the Nicomachean Ethics is a later and improved version of the Eudemian Ethics."  (§1)

> "(Not all of the Eudemian Ethics was revised: its Books IV, V, and VI re-appear as V, VI, VII of the Nicomachean Ethics.)"  (§1)

> "No one had written ethical treatises before Aristotle."  (§1)

> "Aristotle’s search for the good is a search for the highest good, and he assumes that the highest good, whatever it turns out to be, has three characteristics: it is desirable for itself, it is not desirable for the sake of some other good, and all other goods are desirable for its sake."  (§2)

> "To resolve this issue, Aristotle asks what the ergon (“function”, “task”, “work”) of a human being is, and argues that it consists in activity of the rational part of the soul in accordance with virtue (1097b22–1098a20)."  (§2)

> "He says, not that happiness is virtue, but that it is virtuous activity."  (§2)

> "These terms play an evaluative role, and are not simply descriptions of someone’s state of mind."  (§2, of “eudaimonia” and “eu zên”)

> "Aristotle’s reply is that one’s virtuous activity will be to some extent diminished or defective, if one lacks an adequate supply of other goods (1153b17–19)."  (§2)

> "A common complaint about Aristotle’s attempt to defend his conception of happiness is that his argument is too general to show that it is in one’s interest to possess any of the particular virtues as they are traditionally conceived."  (§3.1)

> "He rejects the existence of Plato’s forms in general and the form of the good in particular;"  (§3.2)

> "Ethical virtue is fully developed only when it is combined with practical wisdom (1144b14–17)."  (§4)

> "Aristotle describes ethical virtue as a “hexis” (“state” “condition” “disposition”)—a tendency or disposition, induced by our habits, to have appropriate feelings (1105b25–6)."  (§5.1)

> "The significance of Aristotle’s characterization of these states as hexeis is his decisive rejection of the thesis, found throughout Plato’s early dialogues, that virtue is nothing but a kind of knowledge and vice nothing but a lack of knowledge."  (§5.1)

> "Furthermore, every ethical virtue is a condition intermediate (a “golden mean” as it is popularly known) between two other states, one involving excess, and the other deficiency (1106a26–b28)."  (§5.1)

> "The objection, then, is that Aristotle’s doctrine of the mean, taken as a doctrine about what the ethical agent does when he deliberates, is in many cases inapplicable or unilluminating."  (§5.1)

> "These are precisely the questions that were asked in antiquity by the Stoics, and they came to the conclusion that such common emotions as anger and fear are always inappropriate."  (§5.1)

> "So far from offering a decision procedure, Aristotle insists that this is something that no ethical theory can do."  (§5.2)

> "So it is clear that exercising theoretical wisdom is a more important component of our ultimate goal than practical wisdom."  (§6)

> "Aristotle distinguishes two kinds of akrasia: impetuosity (propeteia) and weakness (astheneia)."  (§7)

> "It should be noticed that Aristotle’s treatment of akrasia is heavily influenced by Plato’s tripartite division of the soul in the Republic."  (§7)

> "What is most remarkable about Aristotle’s discussion of akrasia is that he defends a position close to that of Socrates."  (§7)

> "in a way Socrates was right after all (1147b13–17). For, he says, the person who acts against reason does not have what is thought to be unqualified knowledge; in a way he has knowledge, but in a way does not."  (§7)

> "But Aristotle’s agreement with Socrates is only partial, because he insists on the power of the emotions to rival, weaken or bypass reason."  (§7)

> "In fact, some scholars have held that X.7–8 are deeply at odds with the rest of the Ethics; they take Aristotle to be saying that we should be prepared to act unethically, if need be, in order to devote ourselves as much as possible to contemplation."  (§10)

> "The best way to understand him is to take him to be assuming that one will need the ethical virtues in order to live the life of a philosopher, even though exercising those virtues is not the philosopher’s ultimate end."  (§10)

> "Perfect happiness, he says, consists in contemplation; but he indicates that the life devoted to practical thought and ethical virtue is happy in a secondary way."  (§10)

Glossary (Kraut's, verbatim lines): "function: ergon"; "happiness:
eudaimonia"; "incontinence (literally: lack of mastery): akrasia";
"practical wisdom: phronêsis"; "disposition: hexis".

Relied on for: the two Ethics and their names; the highest good; the

complaint; hexis, the mean, its objection and the Stoic question; no

> "Aristotle (384–322 B.C.E.) numbers among the greatest philosophers of all time. Judged solely in terms of his philosophical influence, only Plato is his peer:"  (preamble)

> "Born in 384 B.C.E. in the Macedonian region of northeastern Greece in the small city of Stagira (whence the moniker ‘the Stagirite’, which one still occasionally encounters in Aristotelian scholarship), Aristotle was sent to Athens at about the age of seventeen to study in Plato’s Academy, then a pre-eminent place of learning in the Greek world."  (§1)

> "Once in Athens, Aristotle remained associated with the Academy until Plato’s death in 347,"  (§1)

> "In 343, upon the request of Philip, the king of Macedon, Aristotle left Lesbos for Pella, the Macedonian capital, in order to tutor the king’s thirteen-year-old son, Alexander—the boy who was eventually to become Alexander the Great."  (§1)

> "Although speculation concerning Aristotle’s influence upon the developing Alexander has proven irresistible to historians, in fact little concrete is known about their interaction."  (§1)

> "He evidently remained a further five years in Stagira or Macedon before returning to Athens for the second and final time, in 335. In Athens, Aristotle set up his own school in a public exercise area dedicated to the god Apollo Lykeios, whence its name, the Lyceum."  (§1)

> "In any event, they had children together, including a son, Nicomachus, named for Aristotle’s father and after whom his Nicomachean Ethics is presumably named."  (§1)

> "After thirteen years in Athens, Aristotle once again found cause to retire from the city, in 323."  (§1)

> "He withdrew directly to Chalcis, on Euboea, an island off the Attic coast, and died there of natural causes the following year, in 322."  (§1)

> "in Arabic Philosophy, where Aristotle was so prominent that be became known simply as The First Teacher"  (§14; "be" sic)

> "Among Aristotle’s greatest exponents during the early period of his reintroduction to the West, Albertus Magnus, and above all his student Thomas Aquinas, sought to reconcile Aristotle’s philosophy with Christian thought."  (§14)

> "Some Aristotelians disdain Aquinas as bastardizing Aristotle, while some Christians disown Aquinas as pandering to pagan philosophy. Many others in both camps take a much more positive view, seeing Thomism as a brilliant synthesis of two towering traditions;"  (§14)

> "though perhaps Aristotle’s influence is seen most overtly and avowedly in the resurgence of virtue ethics which began in the last half of the twentieth century."  (§14)

Relied on for: dates and places of life; Academy, Alexander, Lyceum,
Nicomachus; Shields's standing assessment; Arabic, Latin and Thomist
reception with both camps; virtue-ethics revival (aristotle.md).
Context: the "they" of the Nicomachus sentence is Aristotle and Herpyllis,
whose relation to him Shields says is disputed. The 323 departure is
linked by Shields to anti-Macedonian sentiment after Alexander's death.

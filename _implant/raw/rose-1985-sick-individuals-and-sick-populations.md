# Rose 1985 (reprinted 2001) — "Sick individuals and sick populations": causes of cases vs causes of incidence; high-risk and population strategies

Source: Geoffrey Rose, "Sick individuals and sick populations", International
  Journal of Epidemiology 14(1) (1985), 32–38; reprinted ("Reiterations") in
  International Journal of Epidemiology 30(3) (2001), 427–432.
Original: https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/14.1.32 ; reprint https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/30.3.427

> "The corresponding strategies in control are the ‘high-risk’ approach, which seeks to protect susceptible individuals, and the population approach, which seeks to control the causes of incidence."  (abstract)

> "The two approaches are not usually in competition, but the prior concern should always be to discover and control the causes of incidence."  (abstract)

> "The first seeks the causes of cases, and the second seeks the causes of incidence."  ("The Determinants of Population Incidence Rate")

> "If everyone smoked 20 cigarettes a day, then clinical, case-control and cohort studies alike would lead us to conclude that lung cancer was a genetic disease"  ("The Determinants of Individual Cases")

> "Mothers under 30 years are individually at minimal risk; but because they are so numerous, they generate half the cases. High-risk individuals aged 40 and above generate only 13% of the cases."  ("The ‘High-Risk’ Strategy"; data from Alberman, Table 4)

> "This situation seems to be common, and it limits the utility of the ‘high-risk’ approach to prevention."  ("The ‘High-Risk’ Strategy")

> "A further disadvantage of the ‘high-risk’ strategy is that it is behaviourally inappropriate."  ("The ‘High-Risk’ Strategy")

> "Everyone, in fact, is a high-risk individual for this uniquely mass disease."  ("The ‘High-Risk’ Strategy"; coronary heart disease)

> "This is the attempt to control the determinants of incidence, to lower the mean level of risk factors, to shift the whole distribution of exposure in a favourable direction."  ("The Population Strategy")

> "Unfortunately the population strategy of prevention has also some weighty drawbacks (Table 6)."  ("The Population Strategy")

> "This leads to the Prevention Paradox: ‘A preventive measure which brings much benefit to the population offers little to each participating individual’."  ("The Population Strategy"; footnote 8 = Rose 1981; the HTML has a space before the colon and the footnote number, removed here)

> "This has been the history of public health—of immunization, the wearing of seat belts and now the attempt to change various life-style characteristics."  ("The Population Strategy")

> "Their health next year is not likely to be much better if they accept our advice or if they reject it."  ("The Population Strategy")

> "Grateful patients are few in preventive medicine, where success is marked by a non-event."  ("The Population Strategy")

> "In mass prevention each individual has usually only a small expectation of benefit, and this small benefit can easily be outweighed by a small risk."  ("The Population Strategy")

> "The ‘high-risk’ strategy of prevention is an interim expedient, needed in order to protect susceptible individuals, but only for so long as the underlying causes of incidence remain unknown or uncontrollable; if causes can be removed, susceptibility ceases to matter."  ("Conclusions")

> "Realistically, many diseases will long continue to call for both approaches, and fortunately competition between them is usually unnecessary."  ("Conclusions")

Relied on for: the two aetiological questions; the two strategies with the
  advantages and drawbacks Rose lists; the 1985 wording of the prevention
  paradox; the Down's syndrome figures as Rose reports Alberman's data;
  Rose's conclusion that the approaches are usually not in competition.
Context: Based on a lecture to the Xth Scientific Meeting of the International
  Epidemiological Association, Vancouver, 27 August 1984 (reprint note).
  Advantages of the high-risk strategy Rose lists: intervention appropriate
  to the individual, subject and physician motivation, cost-effective use of
  resources, favourable benefit/risk ratio. Disadvantages: screening costs,
  palliative and temporary, weak prediction, behaviourally inappropriate.
  Rose's examples include Kenyan nomads vs London civil servants (blood
  pressure) and East Finland vs Japan (cholesterol).

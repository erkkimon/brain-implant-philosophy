# Kohlberg 1972 — stages are structures of reasoning, not the content chosen

Source: Lawrence Kohlberg, "A.P.A. (American Psychological Association)
  Values Symposium Paper 1972", paper presented at the 80th Annual
  Convention of the APA, Honolulu, 2–8 September 1972. ERIC document
  ED 069 413 (PS 006 142), 14 pp. Locators are pages of the ERIC scan's PDF.

> "Each, we thought, involved a choice between following fixed social rules and authority and serving situational human needs and welfare."  (PDF p. 6, on "the nine moral decision situations we have used in our research on moral Stages")

> "Those who decided, for instance, that one should steal a drug to save ones dying wife, would also tend to decide to keep quiet about a brother's excuseable lie to his father, because both alternatives would appeal to someone highly valuing serving human needs,"  (PDF p. 7, stating the assumption of the "attitude-strength" approach that Kohlberg contrasts with his own)

> "Our phenomonological postulate is exemplified in the fact that instead of asking subjects only for a predefined action choice or for a rating of preferance, we asked him to structure the dilemma himself and to explain why and how he would make a choice in the situation."  (PDF p. 8)

> "The types of orientation we call stages are not values-or value- orientations in the sense of classes of preferred content, of WHAT is chosen. We distinguish between the content of value judgment and the structure of value judgment. The structure of value judgement is the way in which the individual judges and chooses and reasons, the reasons behind his choice. The distinction between content and structure is basic but not absolute."  (PDF p. 9)

Relied on for: the claim, in Kohlberg's words, that a stage is assigned
from the structure of the reasoning and not from the action chosen; that
the dilemmas were built as "rules vs. needs" choices.
Context: The paper contrasts an "attitude-strength" approach to values
with the "structural approach"; the example that follows (ten-year-old
Danny, a brother-and-father dilemma) is scored Stage 1, "the punishment
and obedience orientation" (PDF p. 9).

# Nozick 1974 — the experience machine (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, pp. 42–45)

Source: Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (New York: Basic Books,
  1974), ch. 3 "Moral Constraints and the State", section "The Experience
  Machine", pp. 42–45.
Original: ISBN 0-465-09720-0 (Basic Books 1974), pp. 42–45 (copyrighted;
  excerpts only). Read in a two-page scan of pp. 42–45 posted by the
  University of Kentucky philosophy department:

> "There are also substantial puzzles when we ask what matters other than how people's experiences feel "from the inside." Suppose there were an experience machine that would give you any experience you desired."  (p. 42)

> "Superduper neuropsychologists could stimulate your brain so that you would think and feel you were writing a great novel, or making a friend, or reading an interesting book. All the time you would be floating in a tank, with electrodes attached to your brain."  (p. 42)

> "selecting your life's experiences for, say, the next two years. After two years have passed, you will have ten minutes or ten hours out of the tank, to select the experiences of your next two years."  (pp. 42–43)

> "Of course, while in the tank you won't know that you're there; you'll think it's all actually happening. Others can also plug in to have the experiences they want, so there's no need to stay unplugged to serve them."  (p. 43)

> "Would you plug in? What else can matter to us, other than how our lives feel from the inside?"  (p. 43)

> "What does matter to us in addition to our experiences? First, we want to do certain things, and not just have the experience of doing them."  (p. 43)

> "A second reason for not plugging in is that we want to be a certain way, to be a certain sort of person."  (p. 43)

> "Plugging into the machine is a kind of suicide."  (p. 43)

> "Thirdly, plugging into an experience machine limits us to a man-made reality, to a world no deeper or more important than that which people can construct."  (pp. 43–44)

> "We learn that something matters to us in addition to experience by imagining an experience machine and then realizing that we would not use it."  (p. 44)

> "imagine a transformation machine which transforms us into whatever sort of person we'd like to be (compatible with our staying us)."  (p. 44)

> "Consider then the result machine, which produces in the world any result you would produce and injects your vector input into any joint activity."  (p. 44)

> "What is most disturbing about them is their living of our lives for us."  (p. 44)

> "Perhaps what we desire is to live (an active verb) ourselves, in contact with reality."  (p. 45)

> "Until one finds a satisfactory answer, and determines that this answer does not also apply to animals, one cannot reasonably claim that only the felt experiences of animals limit what we may do to them."  (p. 45)

> "But isn't utilitarianism at least adequate for animals? I think not."  (p. 42)

Relied on for: the 1974 statement of the case (two-year runs, no knowledge
  of being in the tank, others can plug in), Nozick's three reasons, his
  conclusion, the transformation and result machines, "in contact with
  reality", and the context (animals and utilitarianism).
Context: the section sits in a discussion of whether utilitarianism is
  adequate for animals (p. 42); it ends (p. 45) by returning to animals.
  Nozick's footnote on religious views and psychoactive drugs (p. 44) is
  not excerpted.

> "It is easy to persuade ourselves of this equivalence, by remembering that a false proposition implies every other;"  (§19, of the definition of disjunction)

> "not-p is equivalent to the assertion that p implies all propositions"  (§19; plain text of the transcription)

> "The principle that false propositions imply all propositions solves Lewis Carroll’s logical paradox in Mind, N. S. No. 11 (1894)."  (§19 n. 1)

> "The assertion made in that paradox is that, if p, q, r be propositions, and q implies r, while p implies that q implies not-r, then p must be false, on the supposed ground that q implies r and q implies not-r are incompatible."  (§19 n. 1)

> "But in virtue of our definition of negation, if q be false both these implications will hold: the two together, in fact, whatever proposition r may be, are equivalent to not-q."  (§19 n. 1)

> "Thus the only inference warranted by Lewis Carroll’s premisses is that if p be true, q must be false, i.e. that p implies not-q; and this is the conclusion, oddly enough, which common sense would have drawn in the particular case which he discusses."  (§19 n. 1)

Relied on for: Russell's response (material implication; "if p be true, q
must be false"), the propositional checks on the page.
Context: §19 sets out Russell's propositional calculus, defining
disjunction and negation through implication; the note is attached to the
remark "a false proposition implies every other".

# Mercier & Sperber 2011 — confirmation bias as a feature of argument production

Source: Hugo Mercier & Dan Sperber (2011), "Why do humans reason? Arguments
  for an argumentative theory", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 34(2), 57–111
  (target article with commentaries); abstract p. 57, §3 pp. 63–65.
Original: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X10000968
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (DOI verified by content negotiation; text read in
  the authors' copy at dan.sperber.fr/wp-content/uploads/2009/10/
  MercierSperberWhydohumansreason.pdf)

> "Our hypothesis is that the function of reasoning is argumentative. It
>  is to devise and evaluate arguments intended to persuade."  (p. 57)

> "For standard theories of reasoning, the confirmation bias is no more
>  than a flaw of reasoning. For the argumentative theory, however, it is a
>  consequence of the function of reasoning and hence a feature of
>  reasoning when used for the production of arguments."  (p. 63)

> "When one is alone or with people who hold similar views, one's arguments
>  will not be critically evaluated. This is when the confirmation bias is
>  most likely to lead to poor outcomes. However, when reasoning is used in
>  a more felicitous context – that is, in arguments among people who
>  disagree but have a common interest in the truth – the confirmation
>  bias contributes to an efficient form of division of cognitive labor."
>  (p. 65)

Relied on for: the interpretive claim that the bias is a feature of
reasoning's function rather than a flaw; the boundary condition (solitary
or like-minded reasoning vs. disagreement with common interest).
Context: §3 is titled "The confirmation bias: A flaw of reasoning or a
feature of argument production?" and opens by adopting Nickerson's
definition. The authors also distinguish two cases both labelled
confirmation bias (arguing to convince others; reasoning proactively for
an anticipated debate). The commentaries (De Neys, Poletiek, Stupple &
Ball, Wolfe) express "reservations about the prevalence and robustness of
this bias" per the authors' response (in the same issue; not excerpted further).

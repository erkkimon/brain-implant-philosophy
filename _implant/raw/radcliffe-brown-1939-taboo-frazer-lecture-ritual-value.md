# Radcliffe-Brown 1939 — "Taboo" (Frazer Lecture): ritual avoidance, ritual status, ritual value

Source: A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, "Taboo" (The Frazer Lecture, 1939), reprinted
  as ch. VII in *Structure and Function in Primitive Society: Essays and
  Addresses* (London: Cohen & West, 1952), from p. 133 (book pagination;
  the essay begins on scan page 147; quoted pages read from the running
  heads).
Original: https://archive.org/details/structurefunctio00radc (Internet
  Archive scan, UCLA copy; copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-15 (Internet Archive full-text search of the OCR, read
  in context; OCR errors such as stray marginal characters silently
  corrected only where the word is certain)

> "The English word 'taboo' is derived from the Polynesian word 'tabu' (with the accent on the first syllable). In the languages of Polynesia the word means simply 'to forbid', 'forbidden', and can be applied to any sort of prohibition."  (p. 133)

> "The use of the word taboo in anthropology for customs all over the world which resemble in essentials the example given from Polynesia seems to me undesirable and inconvenient."  (p. 134)

> "I propose to refer to the customs we are considering as 'ritual avoidances' or 'ritual prohibitions' and to define them by reference to two fundamental concepts for which I have been in the habit of using the terms 'ritual status' and 'ritual value'."  (p. 134)

> "A member of the Roman Catholic Church, unless granted a dispensation, is required by his religion to abstain from eating meat on Fridays and during Lent. If he fails to observe the rule he sins, and must proceed, as in any other sin, to confess and obtain absolution."  (p. 135)

> "Here the change in ritual status through touching an unclean carcase is described by the terms 'sin', 'unclean' and 'guilty'."  (p. 136)

> "There is the fact already mentioned that in the Polynesian language the word tabu has a much wider meaning, equivalent to our own word 'forbidden'."  (p. 134)

> "Different as this is in important ways from the rule about spilling salt, it can and must for scientific purposes be regarded as belonging to the same general class."  (p. 135)

> "This would seem to make the Polynesian taboo a matter of religion, not of magic."  (p. 137, on Frazer's Maori evidence)

> "Anything — a person, a material thing, a place, a word or name, an occasion or event, a day of the week or a period of the year — which is the object of a ritual avoidance or taboo can be said to have ritual value."  (p. 139)

> "The primary basis of ritual, so the formulation would run, is the attribution of ritual value to objects and occasions which are either themselves objects of important common interests linking together the persons of a community or are symbolically representative of such objects."  (p. 151)

Relied on for: Radcliffe-Brown's etymology and his objection to the
  anthropological extension of "taboo"; his replacement terms; his
  hypothesis on the basis of ritual.
Context: the lecture opens by crediting Frazer's EB9 article as the first
  systematic study; examples range over Polynesia, salt-spilling in England,
  Roman Catholic Friday abstinence, Leviticus, the Kikuyu *thahu* and
  Andamanese childbirth and mourning taboos, all classed by him in one
  "general class". He sets his hypothesis against the anxiety hypothesis
  (a father "reassures himself by observing some taboo") and says it "is
  not concerned with the historical origin of ritual".
  On magic vs religion (pp. 137–138) he reviews Frazer's, Durkheim's and
  Malinowski's criteria and, citing "this absence of agreement as to the
  definitions of magic and religion", recommends avoiding both terms "until
  there is some general agreement about their" use. He keeps "taboo" (English
  spelling) for the anthropologists' sense and "tabu" for the Polynesian
  one (p. 134).

Source: James Robert Brown & Yiftach Fehige, "Thought Experiments", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta &

  2023-11-28. §2 "Taxonomies of Thought Experiments".
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/thought-experiment/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive page fetched with curl, HTML converted to text)

> "Judith Thomson provided one of the most striking and effective thought experiments in the moral realm (see Thomson 1971)."  (§2)

> "Thomson’s thought experiment is effective in distinguishing two concepts that had previously been run together: “right to life” and “right to what is needed to sustain life.”"  (§2)

> "Those opposed to Thomson’s view have two options. They can either dismiss her thought experiment as a useless fiction. In fact, thought experiments as a method in ethics have their critics (see, e.g., Dancy 1985)."  (§2)

## E. Doing vs. Allowing Harm (supplement to sep-double-effect-and-doing-allowing-fall-2024-trolley.md)

> "This undermines Foot’s claim that the difference between doing and allowing explains our intuitions about these cases."  (§3, of Thomson's bystander version)

> "Fitzpatrick (2009) has responded to Thomson by casting doubt on the analogy between stealing money to give to charity and turning the trolley on a stranger rather than oneself."  (§3)

Fitzpatrick 2009 checked by DOI content negotiation: William J. Fitzpatrick,
"Thomson's turnabout on the trolley", Analysis 69(4): 636–643,
doi 10.1093/analys/anp091.

Relied on for: Brown & Fehige's assessment of the violinist and the two
  options they give its opponents; Roessler & DeCew's report of Thomson's
  privacy reductionism and of Scanlon's and Geuss's responses; Frowe &
  Parry's association of the causal account of liability with Thomson and
  the objection and reply they report; Hacker-Wright's report of Foot's
  reply to the violinist; Woollard's report of what the bystander case does
  to Foot's thesis and of Fitzpatrick's reply to Thomson 2008.
Context: A: the passage continues that opponents may instead "provide a
  different version of the same scenario to challenge the conclusion";
  Brown & Fehige use the violinist again in §2 as an example
  of a "negative" thought experiment. B: Scanlon's reply grounds privacy in
  "special interests"; Geuss's quotation calls her argument "very
  persuasive" (his words). C: the section goes on to the responsibility
  account (McMahan, Otsuka) and reports Thomson's own rejection of harming
  innocent obstructors (Thomson 1991: 290). D: Foot's reply turns on the
  difference between withdrawing aid and initiating a fatal sequence
  (Hacker-Wright's summary). E: Woollard reports Fitzpatrick's point that
  stealing is wrong except in exceptional cases.

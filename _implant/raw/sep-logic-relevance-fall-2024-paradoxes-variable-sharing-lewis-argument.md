> "These so-called paradoxes are valid conclusions that follow from the definitions of material and strict implication but are seen, by some, as problematic."  (preamble)

> "The first asserts that every proposition implies a true one; the second that a false proposition implies every proposition, and the third that for any three propositions, either the first implies the second or the second implies the third."  (preamble; of p → (q → p), ¬p → (p → q), (p → q) ∨ (q → r))

> "The first asserts that a contradiction strictly implies every proposition; the second and third imply that every proposition strictly implies a tautology."  (preamble; of (p & ¬p) → q, p → (q → q), p → (q ∨ ¬q))

> "Many philosophers, beginning with Hugh MacColl (1908), have claimed that these theses are counterintuitive."  (preamble; MacColl, "'If' and 'imply'", Mind 17: 151–152, 453–455)

> "Relevance logicians claim that what is unsettling about these so-called paradoxes is that in each of them the antecedent seems irrelevant to the consequent."  (preamble)

> "The moon is made of green cheese. Therefore, either it is raining in Ecuador now or it is not."  (preamble, an inference classical logic makes valid)

> "The variable sharing principle says that no formula of the form \(A \rightarrow B\) can be proven in a relevance logic if \(A\) and \(B\) do not have at least one propositional variable (sometimes called a proposition letter) in common and that no inference can be shown valid if the premises and conclusion do not share at least one propositional variable."  (preamble)

> "Moreover, this principle does not give us a criterion that eliminates all of the paradoxes and fallacies."  (preamble)

> "Unfortunately, from a relevant point of view, the theory of strict implication is still irrelevant."  (§1, on C. I. Lewis's strict implication)

> "One of the “fallacies” that relevance logic was created to avoid is ex falso quodlibet, or explosion – the inference from a contradiction to any proposition whatsoever."  (§6)

> "C.I. Lewis justified explosion by means of a little argument."  (§6)

> "Mainstream relevance logicians block this argument by rejecting disjunctive syllogism."  (§6)

> "The rejection of disjunctive syllogism, however, has become one of the most controversial aspects of relevance logic."  (§6)

> "Tennant’s core logic, however, accepts disjunctive syllogism."  (§6)

> "What is different is its treatment of one of the structural rules of proof -- it rejects the transitivity of logical consequence in its most general form."  (§6)

Relied on for: the two lists of paradoxes (material, strict); MacColl as
the first objector named; the relevance diagnosis and the variable-sharing
principle with its stated limit; strict implication still "irrelevant";
Lewis's argument as Mares reconstructs it; the two responses (reject
disjunctive syllogism; Tennant's Core Logic rejects general transitivity);
Parry's analytic implication (restricted disjunction introduction).
Context: §6 reconstructs Lewis's argument: from p & ¬p, conjunction
elimination gives p, disjunction introduction p ∨ q, conjunction
elimination ¬p, disjunctive syllogism q. The same section presents
Parry's analytic implication, which restricts disjunction introduction to
satisfy a strong variable-sharing principle. The bibliography lists
Anderson & Belnap 1975, Entailment: The Logic of Relevance and Necessity,
vol. I, Princeton, as "still the standard books on the subject".

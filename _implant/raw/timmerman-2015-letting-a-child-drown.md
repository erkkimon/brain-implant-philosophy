> "Yet, the majority of philosophers (including ethicists) reject Singer’s conclusion that we are morally required to donate to aid agencies whenever we can do so without sacrificing anything nearly as important as the good that our donations could bring about."  (p. 204)

> "(2) If it is in your power to prevent something bad from happening, without sacrificing anything nearly as important, it is wrong not to do so."  (p. 204, Timmerman's reconstruction of Singer's premise 2)

> "(4) Therefore, if you do not donate to aid agencies, you are doing something wrong (Singer 1972: 231–3; Singer 2009: 15–16)."  (p. 204)

> "Those that have commonly reject premiss three (e.g. Fagelson 2009; Gomberg 2002; Schmidtz 2000: 684–9) and moral libertarians must reject premiss two (e.g. Narveson 2003; Pogge 2008)."  (p. 205)

> "there are many ways people in affluent nations do causally contribute to the suffering of those in extreme poverty (Singer 1999; Pogge 2001; Pogge 2008)."  (p. 205)

> "Although some deny this assumption (e.g. Kamm 1999, 2007), I will happily grant it for the sake of argument."  (p. 206, on proximity)

> "the implicit assumption is that Drowning Child is an anomalous event."  (p. 207)

> "has a moral obligation to sacrifice $200 worth of new clothing a single time to prevent a child from drowning."  (p. 208, sentence begun on p. 207: "It may be quite clear that one")

> "It is much less clear that one is morally obligated to spend one’s entire life making repeated $200 sacrifices to constantly prevent children from drowning."  (p. 208)

> "people like me, a commonsense consequentialist"  (p. 210)

> "The existing arguments given in favour of rejecting premiss two appeal to the highly controversial claim that we only have a negative duty to not inflict harm on others and no positive duty to help others, even when we can do so at little or no cost to ourselves."  (p. 205)

> "Lisa notices a vast space of land covered with hundreds of newly formed shallow ponds, each of which contains a small child who will drown unless someone pulls them to safety."  (p. 209)

> "For the remainder of her life, the hackers repeat their actions on a daily basis and, every day, the ponds adjacent to Lisa’s bank are filled with drowning children."  (p. 209)

> "Given the totality of the sacrifice Lisa is making, morality intuitively permits Lisa to indulge in theatre at least one time in, let’s say, the remaining 80 years of her life."  (p. 210)

> "To be sure, impartial consequentialists will not be convinced that premiss two is false and their theory-laden intuitions may conflict with the commonsense intuition in my Drowning Children case."  (p. 210)

> "Either our judgments in both cases are reliable and, consequently, we have reason to reject premiss two or neither is reliable and, consequently, Singer has provided no justification for premiss two."  (p. 211)

> "For what it’s worth, I am inclined to hold that Lisa is obligated to rescue a great many children, though significantly fewer than is required by premiss two."  (p. 210)

Relied on for: the reconstruction of Singer's argument as premises
(1)–(4); the claim that most philosophers reject Singer's conclusion
(Timmerman's report, no survey cited at that point); who rejects which
premise; the Drowning Children case and its conclusion that premise 2 is
false; the two objections he considers.
Context: Timmerman grants premises 1 and 3, grants Singer's claims about
distance and numbers for the sake of argument, and describes himself as a commonsense consequentialist (p. 210, quoted
above).

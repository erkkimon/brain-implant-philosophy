# SEP "Normativity in Metaethics" (Copp & Morton) — normative vs non-normative claims, normative formalism, Kantian and teleological accounts

> "In sum, the first is a normative claim, whereas the second is not."  (preamble; the first claim is "I shouldn’t drink another Dr. Pepper", the second "I’ve had two already")

> "Action-guiding claims include claims about what someone ought to do, or should do, or is obligated to do, or has reason to do. Some philosophers take it that all normative claims are action-guiding in this way. We will take a broader view, however, and include evaluative claims among those that we classify as “normative”."  (preamble)

> "Are any normative claims actually true, or is there properly speaking nothing I should or shouldn’t do?"  (preamble)

> "Are there normative properties, like goodness, obligation, reasons, etc. – and if so, how are they related to each other?"  (preamble)

> "By far the most prominent concern with the existence of such facts, going back at least to J.L. Mackie, is the concern that they would be intolerably queer (Mackie 1977: 33)."  (§2)

> "There is nothing to normativity beyond the fact that certain rules or norms “call for” certain actions or decisions in certain situations in the way that the rules of chess and considerations of strategy in chess call for castling early."  (§3.1, stating normative formalism)

> "Formalism has been defended by Evan Tiffany (2007; see also Hubin 2001), and it seems to be implicit in places where it is not explicitly defended."  (§3.1)

> "Yet formalism is a radical view."  (§3.1, the authors' assessment)

> "H.L.A. Hart arguably has a similar view about the normativity of law (1994: 257; see Copp 2019)."  (§3.1)

> "The controversial issue, however, is whether there is such a thing as the “robust” or “authoritative” normativity that formalism rejects."  (§3.1)

> "One approach, which is suggested by Kant, seeks to explain normativity in terms of norms that one could rationally will to serve as requirements incumbent on all agents (1785 [1981])."  (§4.5)

> "Societies do better at coping with this generic problem when their members share a moral code that calls for them to cooperate with one another, to avoid force and fraud, and, generally, and with qualifications, not to interfere with one another."  (§4.8, on the "problem of sociality" in Copp's pluralist teleology)

Relied on for: the normative / non-normative contrast; the broad
(action-guiding plus evaluative) sense of "normative"; normative formalism
and its holders; the error-theory question.
Context: §3.1 sets formalism aside "for our purposes"; §4 surveys attempts
to explain normativity (motivation, desire-based reasons, practical
identity, agency, Kantian self-legislation, categorical requirements,
natural function, Copp's pluralist teleology).

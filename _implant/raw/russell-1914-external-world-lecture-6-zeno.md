# Russell 1914 — Our Knowledge of the External World, Lecture VI: Zeno, Cantor and the mathematical answer

Source: Bertrand Russell, Our Knowledge of the External World as a Field
  for Scientific Method in Philosophy, first published 1914 by The Open
  Court Publishing Company (Lowell Lectures, March–April 1914); the
  Gutenberg text is the 1922 George Allen & Unwin reissue. Lecture VI
  "The Problem of Infinity Considered Historically" (pp. 155–184 of that
  edition, per its table of contents). Project Gutenberg eBook #37090 (no
  page numbers in the e-text).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37090 (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (read in the Gutenberg plain text; the SEP cites the
  1929 Norton edition, pp. 182–198, which was not read)

> "A long line of philosophers, from Zeno to M. Bergson, have based much of their metaphysics upon the supposed impossibility of infinite collections."  (Lecture VI; footnote marker [28] after "Zeno" omitted)

> "The definitive solution of the difficulties is due, not to Bolzano, but to Georg Cantor, whose work on this subject first appeared in 1882."  (Lecture VI)

> "Zeno's arguments would seem to be "ad hominem"; that is to say, they seem to assume premisses granted by his opponents"  (Lecture VI)

> "(1) That, in spite of MM. Milhaud and Paul Tannery, Zeno is anxious to prove that motion is really impossible, and that he desires to prove this because he follows Parmenides in denying plurality"  (Lecture VI)

> "They are not, however, on any view, mere foolish quibbles: they are serious arguments, raising difficulties which it has taken two thousand years to answer"  (Lecture VI)

> "If half the course takes half a minute, and the next quarter takes a quarter of a minute, and so on, the whole course will take a minute. The apparent force of the argument, on this interpretation, lies solely in the mistaken supposition that there cannot be anything beyond the whole of an infinite series, which can be seen to be false by observing that 1 is beyond the whole of the infinite series 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, 15/16, ..."  (Lecture VI, the race-course)

> "the view that an infinite number of instants make up an infinitely long time is not true, and therefore the conclusion that Achilles will never overtake the tortoise does not follow."  (Lecture VI, Achilles)

> "The solution lies in the theory of continuous series: we find it hard to avoid supposing that, when the arrow is in flight, there is a next position occupied at the next moment; but in fact there is no next position and no next moment"  (Lecture VI, the arrow; "next" italicised in the original)

Relied on for: Russell's own statement of the mathematical (Cantorian,
continuous-series) answer to the Dichotomy, Achilles and Arrow — the lead
the SEP says the "received view" followed; his reading of Zeno's target
against Tannery; his assessment that the arguments are serious.
Context: The Zeno discussion sits inside a lecture on the history of the
problem of infinity; Russell quotes Burnet's paraphrases of the arguments
and notes "All the interpretations in what follows are open to question"
(footnote 32). He concludes Zeno's polemic "is directed against the view
that space and time consist of points and instants".

> "Locke does agree with Hobbes, of course, in deriving obligations to obey the law from the consent of the governed."  (§1.3)

> "If the contract is meant to be a device that illustrates how people would have given their consent, on the other hand, then the difficulty is that a hypothetical contract “is no contract at all” (Dworkin, 1977, p. 151)."  (§1.3)

> "As for Locke, he seems to stretch the notion of tacit consent too far when he states, in §119 of the Second Treatise, that the “very being of anyone within the territories” expresses a person’s willing submission to the rule of its government."  (§1.3)

> "In “Of the Original Contract,” published in 1752, Hume takes particular exception to the appeal to tacit consent."  (§1.3)

> "For Hume, the obligation to obey the law derives not from consent or contract but from the straightforward utility of a system of laws that enables people to pursue their interests peacefully and conveniently."  (§1.3)

> "Most people who believe that they have an obligation to obey the law probably think that this putative obligation is grounded in their consent."  (§4.1)

> "The critics’ claim is not that consent cannot be a source of obligations, for they typically believe that it can."  (§4.1)

> "Their claim, instead, is that too few people have either expressly or tacitly given the kind of actual consent that can ground a general obligation to obey the law, and hypothetical consent cannot supply the defect, for reasons already noted."  (§4.1)

> "Nevertheless, consent theory still has its adherents among political philosophers."  (§4.1)

> "Thus John Plamenatz (1968, “Postscript”) and Peter Steinberger (2004, p. 218) have maintained that voting or otherwise participating in elections should count as consent;"  (§4.1)

> "For example, consent to be another’s slave generates no obligation even if it genuinely expresses a person’s will."  (§4.1)

Relied on for: consent of the governed as the ground of political obligation
  in Locke (and Hobbes); the authors' assessment of Locke §119; Hume's critique
  and his alternative ground (utility); the critics' (Hume, Simmons 1979)
  claim; the two lines of reply (Beran 1987 express-consent procedures;
  broader construals by Plamenatz, Steinberger, Murphy, Gilbert); the void
  consent-to-slavery example in Estlund's context.
Context: §4.1 opens by noting "the withering criticism to which Hume and more
  recent writers – notably Simmons (1979, chaps. 3 and 4) – have subjected
  consent theory" (the authors' words); it reports Beran's procedures and
  doubts (Klosko 2005) that the consent so given would be voluntary, then
  Estlund's "normative consent" (2008) with objections (Sreenivasan 2009) and
  Estlund's replies.

## The problem of identity through time (preamble, §1)

> "Irving Copi once defined the problem of identity through time by noting that the following two statements both seem true but, on the assumption that there is change, appear to be inconsistent:"  (preamble)

> "More important, we should not suppose that we have here any problem about identity. We never have. Identity is utterly simple and unproblematic."  (§1, quoting Lewis 1986, 192–193)

## The case and the transitivity conflict (§4)

> "One of the most famous examples of a fission case is provided by the ship of Theseus."  (§4)

> "Over a long period all of the planks composing a certain ship are replaced one by one. Eventually a ship indiscernible from the original, but composed of entirely different planks, results."  (§4; Gallois names this ship "Replacement")

> "As each plank is removed from the original ship it is used to construct a ship that is constituted from all and only the planks belonging to the original ship."  (§4; named "Reassembly")

> "Identity is, very plausibly, an equivalence relation"  (§4)

> "Saying that, in the case of Theseus' ship, Replacement and Reassembly are both identical with the original ship putatively conflicts with the transitivity of identity since Replacement seems to be later clearly distinct from Reassembly."  (§4)

## Constitution (§4.1)

> "Defenders of such accounts hold that constitution, unlike identity, is not an equivalence relation (Baker 2002)."  (§4.1)

> "Finally, the reassembly ship is never identical, but only constituted from exactly the same planks as, the original."  (§4.1)

> "Some think it engages in metaphysical multiple vision, seeing a multiplicity of things at a given place and time where there is plausibly only one. Others argue that constitution is identity (see Noonan 1993)."  (§4.1)

§4.1 also lists an objection from causal overdetermination, an objection
from grounding and the "too many minds" objection (Olsen 1997, Shoemaker
1999) against constitution views.

## Relative identity (§4.2)

> "Advocates of relative identity deny that there is a single identity relation (see Geach 1967, Griffin 1977)."  (§4.2)

## Strict and loose identity — Chisholm (§4.3)

> "For Chisholm things have their parts essentially in the following sense. It is impossible for a to be identical with b in the strict and philosophical sense, unless a and b have all their parts in common."  (§4.3; Gallois cites Chisholm 1969a, "The Loose and Popular and the Strict and philosophical Senses of Identity")

## Four-dimensionalism and stage theory (§4.5)

> "Armed with the unrestricted mereological composition principle a four-dimensionalist can say that what there is, in the case of Theseus' ship, is a Y branching object dividable in an indefinite number of ways into four dimensional proper parts an indefinite number of which are ships."  (§4.5)

> "Recently Sider (2002) developed an argument for Four Dimensionalism from considerations about vagueness. He argues that Four Dimensionalism is true if it cannot be vague how many things there are, and also that it cannot be vague how many things there are."  (§4.5)

> "It should be noted that Sider and Katherine Hawley defend an alternative version of Four Dimensionalism which identifies objects with what, on the first version, would qualify as their stages (Hawley 2001, Sider 2002)."  (§4.5)

> "In contrast the stage theorist identifies the table with the present stage of the table."  (§4.5)

> "Instead the stage theorist can say there is a single present person who will be identical with each of a pair of later individuals in virtue of standing in the relevant counterpart relations to each one."  (§4.5)

## Temporary identity (§4.6)

> "George Myro does so by restricting the scope of LL to exclude such properties as having had a handle (Myro 1985). Gallois (1998) does so by arguing that if the identity of Cup and Tcup is temporary, we cannot infer from Tcup having had a handle earlier that earlier Tcup has a handle."  (§4.6; note the text says "Myro 1985", the bibliography lists Myro 1986)

## Fission cases: each response applied to the ship (§4.7)

> "The best known example of a symmetrical fission case is the Ship of Theseus described at the beginning of this section."  (§4.7)

> "Constitution only delivers a candidate solution in a symmetric fission case."  (§4.7)

> "Some relative identity theorists have this to say about Theseus' Ship."  (§4.7)

> "The replacement ship is the same ship as the original, and the original is the same collection of planks as the reassembly ship. Conflict with transitivity is avoided because we need not allow that the reassembly ship is the same ship as the original."  (§4.7)

> "Reassembly is strictly identical with Original, but Replacement is only loosely identical with Original."  (§4.7, strict/loose distinction)

> "Here is what a four dimensionalist such as Lewis will say about Theseus' ship."  (§4.7)

> "In that case there are two initially indiscernible ships."  (§4.7)

> "We thus have a Y-branching four dimensionally extended object with one ship constituted by the stem together with one branch, and the other constituted by the stem together with the remaining branch."  (§4.7)

> "Four Dimensionalism, like the thesis that identities can be temporary, applies to both symmetric and asymmetric cases of fission."  (§4.7)

> "A temporary identity theorist will hold that Replacement and Reassembly are identical at t1, but distinct ships at t2."  (§4.7)

> "If that argument can be met, it is open to us to say the following about Theseus' Ship. Each of Replacement and Reassembly is indeterminately identical with Original."  (§4.7; "that argument" is the Evans–Salmon argument of §3, Evans 1978, Salmon 1981)

> "The puzzle cases are puzzle cases because they bring into putative conflict intuitions all of which we want to subscribe to."  (§4.7)

> "Thomas Sattig argues for a compatibilist view according to which we can hold on to both intuitions without giving up, or modifying, Leibniz's Law. We can do so by relativising identities to different perspectives."  (§4.7)

## Personal identity (§5)

> "We saw that making certain identifications in a fission case such as the ship of Theseus [see Section 4: Diachronic Identity Puzzles] threatens the transitivity of identity. One much discussed type of fission case, hemispheric division, is taken by many to show that the psychological continuity criterion is, after all, incompatible with the transitivity of identity (Nagel 1975)."  (§5)

> "The publication of Robert Nozick’s book, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) was the first time mainstream academic philosophy was forced to pay attention to libertarian ideas."  (§1)

> "G. A. Cohen was one of the most important of Nozick’s critics, but he nonetheless sympathized with what he took to be the core commitment of justice in Nozick’s theory – self-ownership."  (§1)

> "Cohen, however, took it that self-ownership explained the unjustly exploitative nature of capitalism."  (§1)

> "Despite Nozick only mentioning self-ownership once, and developing a large number of arguments against redistributive conceptions of economic justice which do not explicitly invoke or rely on the idea of self-ownership throughout part II of Anarchy, State, and Utopia, self-ownership became synonymous with libertarianism."  (§1)

> "and so-called full self-ownership does not appear to do that, and hence few if any libertarians support it."  (on Cohen's ascription of "full" self-ownership to Nozick)

> "Nozick interprets the proviso to require that no individual be made worse off by a particular appropriation of a natural resource. But this interpretation is problematic for at least two reasons."  (section on the Lockean proviso)

> "Robert Nozick offered a controversial and subtle argument that a state, with strictly curtailed domain of activity, could exist without anyone’s rights being violated."  (section on the state)

> "Nozick’s argument is a potentially strong one as it uses anarchist premises to argue that a state could in principle emerge through just steps."  (section on the state)

> "Anarchists argue that a de facto monopoly would not be just in using force to prohibit anyone else from forming a new protective association."  (section on the state; Chartier 2011)

> "Non-libertarian liberals argue that even if the argument is internally coherent in terms of showing that a de jure monopoly could emerge justly, it does not yield a state that wields public power, but only a form of overwhelming private power, such as the states of feudal Europe (Freeman 2001, Anderson 2017)."  (section on the state)

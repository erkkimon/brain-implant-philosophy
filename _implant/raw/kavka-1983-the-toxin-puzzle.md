> "He places before you a vial of toxin that, if you drink it, will make you painfully ill for a day, but will not threaten your life or have any lasting effects."  (p. 33)

> "The billionaire will pay you one million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon."  (p. 33)

> "He emphasizes that you need not drink the toxin to receive the money; in fact, the money will already be in your bank account hours before the time for drinking it arrives, if you succeed."  (pp. 33–34)

> "You are perfectly free to change your mind after receiving the money and not drink the toxin."  (p. 34)

> "So instead of planning to drink the toxin, you decide to intend today to drink it and then change your mind after midnight. But if that is your plan, then it is obvious that you do not intend to drink the toxin. (At most you intend to intend to drink it.)"  (p. 34)

> "Unfortunately, your daughter the lawyer, who has read the contract carefully, points out that arrangement of such external incentives is ruled out, as are such alternative gimmicks as hiring a hypnotist to implant the intention, forgetting the main relevant facts of the situation, and so forth."  (p. 34)

> "So instead of planning to drink the toxin, you decide to intend today to drink it and then change your mind after midnight."  (p. 34)

> "But, alas, your nephew, a private investigator, discovers that you are the first one to receive the offer (or that past winners drank less often than past losers)."  (p. 35)

> "Remembering Newcomb's Problem, you seek inductive evidence that this is so, hoping that previous recipients of the billionaire's offer won the million when and only when they drank the toxin."  (p. 35)

> "You are asked to form a simple intention to perform an act that is well within your power."  (p. 35)

> "Yet you cannot do so (or have extreme difficulty doing so) without resorting to exotic tricks involving hypnosis, hired killers, etc."  (p. 35)

> "But intentions are better viewed as dispositions to act which are based on reasons to act - features of the act itself or its (possible) consequences that are valued by the agent."  (p. 35)

> "Thus, we can explain your difficulty in earning a fortune: you cannot intend to act as you have no reason to act, at least when you have substantial reasons not to act."  (p. 35)

> "While you have no reasons to drink the toxin, you have every reason (or at least a million reasons) to intend to drink it."  (p. 35)

> "As a result, when we have good reasons to intend but not to act, conflicting standards of evaluation come into play and something has to give way: either rational action, rational intention, or aspects of the agent's own rationality (e.g., his correct belief that drinking the toxin is not necessary for winning the million)."  (p. 36)

> "I made some similar points in an earlier article ('Some Paradoxes of Deterrence', Journal of Philosophy, June 1978), but there I was discussing an example involving conditional intentions."  (p. 36)

> "It also reveals that intentions are only partly volitional. One cannot intend whatever one wants to intend any more than one can believe whatever one wants to believe. As our beliefs are constrained by our evidence, so our intentions are constrained by our reasons for action."  (p. 36)

> "The puzzle discussed here emerged from a conversation, some years ago, with Tyler Burge about 'Some Paradoxes of Deterrence'."  (p. 36, n. 1)

Relied on for: the statement of the case; the "intend to intend" step; the
excluded external incentives and gimmicks; the Newcomb reference; Kavka's
account of intentions as reason-based dispositions; the two points and the
"something has to give way" trilemma; the link to deterrence (Kavka 1978,
https://doi.org/10.2307/2025707, Crossref-checked: "Some Paradoxes of
Deterrence", J. Phil. 75(6), 1978, p. 285 ff.); the Burge origin note.
Context: the paper is told in the second person, as a story: the spouse
confirms the toxin's properties, a "mind-reading" brain scanner by "Doctor X"
detects the intention, the Pentagon-strategist son proposes binding devices
(a promise, a forfeit to a disliked political party, a hitman), the lawyer
daughter rules them out, a private-investigator nephew finds no inductive
evidence. Kavka says he "need not complete this tale" to show "there is a
puzzle lurking here" (p. 35). He uses "puzzle", not "paradox", throughout;
"paradoxes" appears only in the title of his 1978 paper.

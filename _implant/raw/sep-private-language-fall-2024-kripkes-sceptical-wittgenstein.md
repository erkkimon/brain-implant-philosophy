# Candlish & Wrisley (SEP 2019, Fall 2024 archive) — Kripke's sceptical Wittgenstein and the community view

Source: Stewart Candlish and George Wrisley, "Private Language",
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, substantive revision 30 July 2019,
  Fall 2024 archive edition, §§4–4.1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/private-language/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (HTML fetched with curl and converted to text)

> "The Orthodox domination of the secondary literature on private language was largely ended by Saul Kripke’s account of Wittgenstein’s treatment of rules and private language, in which Wittgenstein appears as a sceptic concerning meaning."  (§4)

> "Kripke’s Wittgenstein, real or fictional, has become a philosopher in his own right, and for many people, it is not an issue whether the historical Wittgenstein’s original ideas about private language are faithfully captured in this version."  (§4)

> "Wittgenstein himself immediately brushed this “paradox” aside in his very next paragraph: ‘That there is a misunderstanding here …’; but Kripke takes the paradox to pose a genuine and profound sceptical problem about meaning."  (§4)

> "The application of the rule is potentially infinite, and bizarre interpretations of the rule, as well as the standard use of it, are compatible with any finite set of applications of the usual sort such as 7 + 14 = 21."  (§4)

> "Wittgenstein says, in effect, “Unique meaning is unintelligible; rather, someone’s meaning the usual addition function when saying ‘plus’ consists in their being considered by a community to have passed the community’s test for employing that function.”"  (§4)

> "This requirement of community agreement for meaning (the ‘community view’) obviously rules out the possibility of private language immediately, thereby rendering the argument of Philosophical Investigations §§256–271 superfluous."  (§4)

> "Kripke’s account of the private language argument is thus vitiated by his unargued reliance on ideas which Wittgenstein argued against."  (§4)

> "Further, his reading of the argument gave new life to the debate over the community view."  (§4)

> "even the most careful, insightful and sympathetic of Wittgenstein’s commentators have divided on this matter (for example, Malcolm for the community view, and Baker and Hacker against it)."  (§4.1)

Relied on for: Kripke's reading as ending the "Orthodox" interpretation;
  "Kripke's Wittgenstein" as a figure in his own right; the entry authors'
  assessment that Wittgenstein brushed the paradox aside and that Kripke's
  account is "vitiated"; the community view and the Malcolm vs. Baker &
  Hacker division.
Context: §4 states "All that will be settled here is the interpretative
  question" and refers the philosophical questions to Boghossian [1989];
  §4.1 argues, following Canfield [1996], that "language is essentially
  social" and a lifelong Crusoe's rule-following need not conflict.

> "It seems extremely natural to require that no state's apportionment should deviate from its quota by one or more seats; in other words, no state should get less than its quota rounded down"  (§6, PDF p. 58; the sentence goes on: no state should get more than its quota rounded up, and names this property "staying within the quota")

> "The primitive desire to stay within the quota clearly motivated Hamilton's method and it was a key point in Webster's critique of Jefferson's method."  (§6, PDF p. 58)

> "the following "impossibility theorem", which says that no method can be population monotone and stay within the quota."  (§6, PDF p. 58)

Theorem 6.1 (PDF p. 59), as far as the OCR is legible: there exists no
partial method, for s at least 4 and h at least s+3, "that is population
monotone and stays within the quota" (s is the number of states, h the size
of house, §1; the inequality signs are garbled in the scan's text layer and
are read here from the proof's opening, which fixes s and h the same way).

> "Moreover these same examples suggest that staying within the quota may not be such a reasonable idea after all."  (§6, PDF p. 58)

> "It can be argued that staying within the quota is not really compatible with the idea of proportionality at all, since it allows a much greater variance in the per capita representation of smaller states than it does for larger states."  (§6, PDF p. 58)

> "Any method that is population monotone avoids the Alabama paradox."  (§4, PDF p. 38)

> "This is an elementary requirement for any scheme of fair representation, and the only methods that satisfy it are the divisor methods."  (§4, PDF p. 38; "it" is population monotonicity)

> "The decisive conclusion of the preceding section is that the only realistic candidates for methods of apportionment are the divisor methods."  (§5 opening, PDF p. 39)

> "House monotonicity is a weaker property, implied by population monotonicity but not implying it."  (§7, PDF p. 66)

> "Up to this point the only methods discussed that stay within the quota are Hamilton-like and violate both population monotonicity and house monotonicity."  (§7, PDF p. 66)

> "Unfortunately these adaptations suffer acutely from the population paradox, so are not to be recommended."  (§7, PDF p. 66; "these adaptations" are traditional methods modified so that they always stay within the quota)

> "This notion was proposed as early as 1907 by Erlang and has been studied by Hylland (1975), (1978)."  (§4, PDF p. 22; "this notion" is the definition: if one state's population increases and all others remain the same, its apportionment does not decrease)

> "A few fundamental principles suffice: consistency with changes in populations, avoiding the Alabama paradox, lack of bias, and staying within the quota."  (§4, PDF p. 20)

> "Any problem in which h objects are to be allocated in non-negative integers proportionally to some numerical criterion belongs to this class, and the theory below applies to it."  (§1, PDF p. 3)

> "While no population monotone method stays within the quota all of the time, there are population monotone methods that stay within the quota "almost" all of the time; moreover the best from this standpoint is Webster's method."  (§6, PDF p. 60)

Relied on for: Erlang's 1907 notion; the impossibility theorem in the authors' wording; the quota
property; the implications population monotonicity → no Alabama paradox and
→ divisor method; the authors' assessments of quota and of quota-keeping
adaptations; the scope of the theory beyond legislatures.
Context: the paper is the authors' survey of their axiomatic theory; §6
introduces the theorem right after examples (Table 6) where a divisor method
gives a state more than its upper quota, which the authors use to question the
quota property itself. §7 presents the "quota method" (Balinski and Young
1975), which stays within the quota and is house monotone.

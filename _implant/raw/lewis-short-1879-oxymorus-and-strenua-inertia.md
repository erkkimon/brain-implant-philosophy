# Lewis & Short 1879 — "oxymōrus" and "inertia"/"strenuus" (Horace's "strenua inertia" as oxymoron)

Source: Charlton T. Lewis and Charles Short, A Latin Dictionary (Oxford:
  Clarendon Press, 1879), entries "oxymōrus", "inertia", "strenuus".
Original: Perseus Digital Library, Perseus:text:1999.04.0059,
  https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=oxymorus
  (also entry=inertia, entry=strenuus) (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-28 (HTML fetched; entries read after tag stripping)

> "oxymōrus , a, um, adj., = ὀξύμωρος , I. acutely silly : oxymora verba,
> expressions which at first sight appear absurd , but which contain a
> concealed point; so especially of such apparently contradictory
> assertions as: cum tacent clamant, etc. ( Cic. Cat. 1, 8, 21 ), Pseudo
> Ascon. ad Cic. Div. in Caecil. 1 ."  (entry "oxymōrus")

> "— “ In an oxymoron : strenua , ” Hor. Ep. 1, 11, 28 ; cf. “ inquieta , ”
> Sen. Tranq. 12, 2 ."  (entry "inertia")

> "“ strenua nos exercet inertia , ” busy idleness , Hor. Ep. 1, 11, 28"  (entry "strenuus")

Cicero, In Catilinam 1.21 (Latin Library text,
https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/cat1.shtml, section [21], read
2026-09-28), verbatim:

> "De te autem, Catilina, cum quiescunt, probant, cum patiuntur, decernunt,
> cum tacent, clamant,"  (Cat. 1.21)

Relied on for: the Latin lexicographic definition of oxymora verba;
"strenua ... inertia" labelled an oxymoron; the Cicero example that the
dictionary gives for the apparently contradictory assertion.
Context: Cicero tells Catiline that the senators' silence is itself a
verdict. Lewis & Short read "nos" in Horace where the Latin Library text
reads "non". Spaces before punctuation are artefacts of Perseus markup.

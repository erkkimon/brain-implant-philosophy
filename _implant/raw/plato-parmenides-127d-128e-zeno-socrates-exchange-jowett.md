# Plato, Parmenides 127d–128e — Socrates questions Zeno about his book (Jowett)

> "What is your meaning, Zeno? Do you maintain that if being is many, it must be both like and unlike, and that this is impossible, for neither can the like be unlike, nor the unlike like-is that your position? Just so, said Zeno."  (127e)

> "No, said Zeno; you have correctly understood my general purpose."  (128a)

> "You affirm unity, he denies plurality. And so you deceive the world into believing that you are saying different things when really you are saying much the same."  (128a–b, Socrates to Parmenides)

> "But although you are as keen as a Spartan hound in pursuing the track, you do not fully apprehend the true motive of the composition"  (128c, Zeno)

> "Zeal for my master led me to write the book in the days of my youth, but some one stole the copy; and therefore I had no choice whether it should be published or not; the motive, however, of writing, was not the ambition of an elder man, but the pugnacity of a young one."  (128d–e, Zeno)

Relied on for: the exchange in which Socrates states, and Zeno corrects,
the purpose of the book; Zeno's account of its youthful writing and theft,
as Plato presents them.
Context: Narrated at third hand (Antiphon from Pythodorus). Immediately
after, Socrates introduces the ideas of likeness and unlikeness (128e ff.).
Palmer (SEP 2024, §3) translates the same passage with "love of victory"
(philonikia, 128d7, e2) and reads Zeno's reply as a correction of
Socrates; Huggett (SEP 2024, preamble) reads the book as "defending
Parmenides’ philosophy".

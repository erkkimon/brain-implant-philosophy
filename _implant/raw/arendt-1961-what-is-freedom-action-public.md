# Arendt 1961 — "What Is Freedom?": freedom as action in the public realm

Source: Hannah Arendt, "What Is Freedom?", in *Between Past and Future:
  Six Exercises in Political Thought*, New York: The Viking Press, 1961,
  pp. 143–171 (quotations from pp. 146, 148, 153 of that edition).
Original: ISBN-less 1961 Viking edition, scan at
  https://archive.org/details/hannah-arendt-between-past-and-future
  (copyrighted, © 1961 Hannah Arendt; short excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-15 (archive.org djvu text fetched with curl; quotations
  checked by script against that text, OCR hyphenation joined; page
  numbers read from the running heads)

> "freedom, which only seldom—in times of crisis or revolution—becomes the direct aim of political action, is actually the reason that men live together in political organization at all."  (p. 146)

> "Without it, political life as such would be meaningless."  (p. 146)

> "its field of experience is action."  (p. 146; the sentence begins "The raison d'être of politics is freedom, and")

> "man would know nothing of inner freedom if he had not first experienced a condition of being free as a worldly tangible reality."  (p. 148)

> "We first become aware of freedom or its opposite in our intercourse with others, not in the intercourse with ourselves."  (p. 148)

> "Men are free—as distinguished from their possessing the gift for freedom—as long as they act, neither before nor after; for to be free and to act are the same."  (p. 153)

Relied on for: Arendt's own statement of freedom as action in public,
  distinct from inner freedom and from freedom of the will.
Context: the essay opens on the free-will problem and argues that the
  political sense of freedom is prior to the philosophical one; p. 148
  says that before freedom "became an attribute of thought or a quality of
  the will" it "was understood to be the free man's status". The next
  paragraph after p. 153 illustrates the point with Machiavelli's virtù.

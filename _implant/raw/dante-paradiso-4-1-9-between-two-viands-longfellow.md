# Dante, Paradiso IV.1–9 — "Between two viands, equally removed"; and Britannica on Buridan's dog

(a) Dante:

> "Between two viands, equally removed / And tempting, a free man would die of hunger / Ere either he could bring unto his teeth."  (Par. IV.1–3, Longfellow)

> "So would a lamb between the ravenings / Of two fierce wolves stand fearing both alike; / And so would stand a dog between two does."  (Par. IV.4–6, Longfellow)

> "Hence, if I held my peace, myself I blame not, / Impelled in equal measure by my doubts, / Since it must be so, nor do I commend."  (Par. IV.7–9, Longfellow)

(b) Britannica:

> "The dilemma of a particular kind of moral choice, between two evidently identical items, is illustrated by the celebrated allegory of “Buridan’s ass,” though the animal mentioned in Buridan’s commentary on Aristotle’s De caelo (“On the Heavens”) is actually a dog, not an ass."  ("Jean Buridan")

> "Discerning both a symmetry of information and a symmetry of preference about the two items, he concludes that the dog must choose at random; this outcome leads to the investigation of theories of probability."  ("Jean Buridan")

Relied on for: Dante's pre-Buridan use of the equal-food image (a free
man, a lamb between wolves, a dog between two does), placing it before
Buridan's teaching (Buridan's dates from Zupko, SEP); Britannica's report
that Buridan's De caelo commentary has a dog, and its account of his
conclusion. Buridan's De caelo commentary itself was NOT read; the dog
version is reported only as Britannica reports it.
Context: (a) Dante, silent before Beatrice with two questions equally
pressing, compares himself to these cases; the image of a free man dying
between two foods is framed as a counterfactual ("would die"). No source
read for this page links Dante's lines to Buridan; they are listed here
only as an earlier text with the same image. (b) Britannica's paragraph
begins by calling Buridan "A defender of the principle of causality" who
"asserted a modified version of traditional moral determinism". Zupko
(SEP n. 57) says the ass example "is nowhere to be found in Buridan’s
writings"; Britannica's claim concerns a dog, not an ass.

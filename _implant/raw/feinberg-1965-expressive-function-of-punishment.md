> "punishment is a conventional device for the expression of attitudes of resentment and indignation, and of judgments of disapproval and reprobation, on the part either of the punishing authority himself o r of those "in whose name" the punishment is inflicted. Punishment, in short, has a symbolic significance largely missing from other kinds of penalties."  (p. 98)

> "Reprobation is itself painful, whether or not it is accompanied by further "hard treatment," and hard treatment, such as fine or imprisonment, because of its conventional symbolism, can itself be reprobatory."  (p. 98)

> "the incoherence of a familiar form of the retributive theory results from failure to appreciate the force of (2)."  (p. 98; (2) = each of the hard-treatment and reprobative aspects "raises its own kind of question about the juslification of legal punishment as a general practice" — "juslification" is the OCR's)

> "it is social disapproval and its appropriate expression that should fit the crime, and not hard treatment (pain) as such."  (p. 118)

Relied on for: Feinberg's expressive account on
  problems/justification-of-punishment.md, and his objection to the
  "pain-fitting-wickedness" form of retributivism.
Context: Feinberg opens by saying that recent articles (Flew 1954, Benn 1958,
  Hart 1959/60 "Prolegomenon") define punishment as hard treatment by an
  authority for an offence and leave out its condemnatory element; he
  distinguishes punishments from mere "penalties" (parking tickets,
  forfeitures). Feinberg's thesis is about the definition of punishment and
  what questions of justification follow; the SEP entry (§5) lists him as a
  source of expressive retributivism, while noting that consequentialists can
  also treat expression as useful.

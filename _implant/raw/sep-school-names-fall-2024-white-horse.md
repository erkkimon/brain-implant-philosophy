## §6 Gongsun Long

> "The Xunzi does not criticize him by name but does cite a version of his white horse sophism in a list of incorrect uses of names (22.3)."  (§6)

> "Gongsun Long’s disputation is perceived as plainly not fitting “reality” (shi, also the “stuff” spoken of)."  (§6)

> "He suggests that Gongsun Long probably belonged to a class of entertainers at Chinese courts who performed various skills or tricks."  (§6)

> "His sophistries may have been intended primarily as a kind of light entertainment, not as expressions of a principled philosophical position."  (§6)

> "A. C. Graham argued persuasively that three of the dialogues are not Warring States texts, but much later forgeries pieced together partly from misunderstood bits of the Mohist Dialectics."  (§6)

> "Probably only the “White Horse,” the essay “Indicating Things,” and a bit of another dialogue are genuine pre-Han texts."  (§6)

> "The Gongsun Longzi has inspired a vast exegetical literature in both Asian and European languages, with no consensus in sight as to the significance and theoretical basis of its arguments."  (§6)

> "The interpretation proposed here must be considered only one of several potentially defensible approaches (others are noted below)."  (§6)

> "the difficulty of finding an angle of approach from which the arguments will make sense.…The arguments are clear, yet the first seems an obvious non sequitur…and the rest seem to assume an elementary confusion of identity and class membership"  (§6, quoting Graham 1989: 82)

> "No one has yet proposed a reading of the dialogue as a consecutive demonstration which does not turn it into an improbable medley of gross fallacies and logical subtleties"  (§6, quoting Graham 1990: 193)

> "Building on Harbsmeier’s insight that the historical context of Gongsun Long’s disputations has been insufficiently appreciated, we may suspect Graham’s remarks signal interpretive charity gone too far."  (§6)

> "roughly a Chinese analogue to the subtle, fallacious, and deeply amusing arguments of Lewis Carroll."  (§6)

> "We can learn about the serious practice of disputation by studying what is in effect a spoof of disputation, just as an anthropologist can learn about a culture by studying its humor."  (§6)


## §6.1 "White Horse is Not Horse"

> "The “White Horse Discourse” has spawned nearly as many interpretations as there are interpreters."  (§6.1)

> "One early, influential interpretation took its theme to be denying the identity of the universals ‘horse’ and ‘white horse’ (Fung 1958, Cheng 1983)."  (§6.1)

> "There is now a fairly broad consensus, at least among European and American scholars, that the text is unlikely to concern universals, since no ancient Chinese philosopher held a realist doctrine of universals."  (§6.1)

> "Other interpretations have taken it to deal with kind and identity relations (Cikoski 1975, Harbsmeier 1998), part-whole relations (Hansen 1983, Graham 1989), how the extensions of phrases vary from those of their constituent terms (Hansen 1992), and even the use/mention distinction (Thompson 1995)."  (§6.1)

> "For examples of other recent approaches, see Indraccolo (2017), Fung (2020b), Jiang (2020), and Zhou (2020)."  (§6.1)

> "Wishing to broaden the referent of ‘person’, it’s appropriate to omit the ‘Chu’; wishing to fix the name of the color, it’s not appropriate to omit the ‘white’."  (§6.1, Kong Congzi Book 11 as translated by Fraser)

> "it suggests that the book’s ancient editors themselves took the theme to be how the scope of the extension of a noun such as ‘person’ or ‘horse’ varies when modified by an adjective such as ‘Chu’ or ‘white’."  (§6.1)

> "So we will translate the main thesis as “White horse is not horse,” variously interpretable as “a white horse is not a horse,” “white horses are not horses,” “a white horse is not an exemplar of the kind horse,” or “the kind white horse is not identical with the kind horse.”"  (§6.1)

> "Argument 1. ‘Horse’ is that by which we name the shape. ‘White’ is that by which we name the color. Naming the color is not naming the shape. So white horse is not horse."  (§6.1, argument 1)

> "Hence we should reject the third premise and insist that naming the color is naming the shape."  (§6.1, on argument 1)

> "If someone seeks a horse, then it’s admissible to deliver a brown or a black horse. If someone seeks a white horse, then it’s inadmissible to deliver a brown or a black horse."  (§6.1, argument 2)

> "The sophist plainly construes “white horse is horse” as “white horse is identical to horse.”"  (§6.1, on argument 2)

> "Notice that the sophist implicitly applies a principle roughly like Leibniz’s law of indiscernibility of identicals."  (§6.1, on argument 2)

> "White horse is horse combined with white. Is horse combined with white the same as horse?"  (§6.1, argument 3)

> "Taking brown horse to be not horse while taking white horse to be having horse, this is flying things entering a pond, inner and outer coffins in different places. These are the most contradictory sayings and confused expressions in the world."  (§6.1, argument 4)

> "The conclusion indeed follows, but only if we allow the sophist to construe “is not” as “is not identical to.”"  (§6.1, on argument 4)

> "“Horse” selects or excludes none of the colors, so brown or black horses can all answer."  (§6.1, argument 5)

> "Excluding none is not excluding some. Therefore white horse is not horse."  (§6.1, argument 5)

> "To sum up, the most natural way to read the text is as repeatedly equivocating between a statement of identity and one that predicates a more general term of the objects denoted by a less general term."  (§6.1, summary)

> "The sophist refuses to distinguish the true statement that “[the kind] white horse is not [identical to the kind] horse” from the false “white horse is not [of the kind] horse.”"  (§6.1, summary)

> "Xunzi, whose career largely overlapped with Gongsun Long’s, introduced the concept of a “common name” (gong ming), or general term, which may refer to things at different levels of generality (22.2f)."  (§6.1, on Xunzi)

> "The simplest early Chinese model of the language-world relation was “one name, one thing,” according to which all names refer at the same level of generality."  (§6.1, on Hansen)

> "The extension of ‘white horse’ is the intersection, not the sum, of white things and horses."  (§6.1, on Hansen)

> "The approach implied by “White Horse,” Hansen suggests, would address the problem by retaining the one-name-one-thing principle and reforming our language use, so that all names pick out exactly the same portion of reality in all contexts, whether used singly or compounded into phrases."  (§6.1, on Hansen)

> "white horses are neither white nor horse, but a distinct sort of thing."  (§6.1, on Hansen)

> "But moving beyond the one-name-one-thing model and explaining exactly why this is absurd was a legitimate philosophical puzzle at the time."  (§6.1, on Hansen)


## Notes

> "Graham’s conclusions, first published in 1957 and reprinted in his (1990), have been widely accepted by European and American scholars but almost completely ignored by scholars writing in Chinese."  (n. 17)

> "This argument can also be read as treating white and horse as two parts of a whole, as Graham suggests (1989)."  (n. 20)

> "Harbsmeier also points out that by using the example of seeking a horse, instead of having a horse, the sophist has created an intensional context, making it that much easier to show that “white horse” and “horse” are not intersubstitutable and thus not identical (1998: 306)."  (n. 21)

> "Noting that Gongsun Long cites Confucius in the anecdote about the King of Chu, Hansen (1992) suggests that he is proposing a language reform as a defense of the Confucian theory of “correcting names.”"  (n. 23)

> "This strikes me as far-fetched, given that Gongsun was notorious for twisting people’s words and that his ethical sympathies seem to have lain more with the Mohists than the Confucians."  (n. 23)

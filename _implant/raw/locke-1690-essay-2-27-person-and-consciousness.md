# Locke 1690 — Essay II.xxvii: the definition of a person and consciousness as what makes personal identity

Source: John Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1st ed. 1690;
  the chapter "Of Identity and Diversity" was added in the 2nd ed., 1694),
  Book II, chapter xxvii, §9 (Project Gutenberg's text numbers this
  section "11. Personal Identity.") and §10 ("12. Consciousness makes
  personal Identity.").
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10615  (Project Gutenberg
  #10615, "An Essay Concerning Humane Understanding, Volume 1"; public
  domain). Canonical locator: Essay II.xxvii.9–10 (Nidditch/Clarendon
  numbering).
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (read in the plain-text file
  https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10615/pg10615.txt, chapter XXVII)

> "This being premised, to find wherein personal identity consists, we
>  must consider what PERSON stands for;—which, I think, is a thinking
>  intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider
>  itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and
>  places; which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable
>  from thinking, and, as it seems to me, essential to it: it being
>  impossible for any one to perceive without PERCEIVING that he does
>  perceive."  (Essay II.xxvii.9; Gutenberg section "11")

> "For, since consciousness always accompanies thinking, and it is that
>  which makes every one to be what he calls self, and thereby
>  distinguishes himself from all other thinking things, in this alone
>  consists personal identity, i.e. the sameness of a rational being: and
>  as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past
>  action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person; it is
>  the same self now it was then"  (Essay II.xxvii.9; Gutenberg section "11")

> "But it is further inquired, whether it be the same identical substance.
>  This few would think they had reason to doubt of, if these perceptions,
>  with their consciousness, always remained present in the mind"
>  (Essay II.xxvii.10, opening; Gutenberg section "12. Consciousness makes
>  personal Identity.")

Relied on for: the claims that Locke defines a person as a "thinking
intelligent being" able to "consider itself as itself" across times and
places, that he treats consciousness as "inseparable from thinking" (one
cannot perceive without perceiving that one perceives), and that he locates
personal identity in continuity of consciousness rather than in sameness of
substance.
Context: §9 follows Locke's distinction of the identity of a man (same
living body) from that of a person; §10 goes on to raise forgetfulness and
sleep as interruptions of consciousness that appear to threaten the account.
Gutenberg's section numbers run two higher than the standard (Nidditch)
numbering for this chapter; the standard numbers are used above.

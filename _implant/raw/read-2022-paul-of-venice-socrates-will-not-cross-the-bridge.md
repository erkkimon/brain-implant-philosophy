# Read 2022 — Paul of Venice on bridge-type propositions as insolubles

Source: Stephen Read, "‘Everything True Will Be False’: Paul of Venice and a
  Medieval Yablo Paradox", History and Philosophy of Logic (published online

> "An insoluble proposition is a proposition having reflection on itself wholly or partially implying its own falsity or that it is not itself true."  (Paul of Venice, Logica Magna, as translated by Read)

> "Paul comments that his definition excludes many propositions counted as insolubles by others, such as ‘Socrates will not cross the bridge’ and ‘Plato will not have a penny’, for he says, they do not have reflection on themselves. But he is not consistent here, for in the fifth chapter he includes them under what he calls ‘insolubles that don’t appear at first glance to be insolubles’ (insolubilia que prima facie insolubilia non apparent)."

Relied on for: whether bridge-type cases count as insolubles was itself
disputed in the medieval literature (Paul of Venice, c. 1396–7, as Read
reports him).
Context: the passage compares Paul's narrower definition of an insoluble
with Swyneshed's broader one that includes epistemic insolubles.

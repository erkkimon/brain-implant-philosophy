# "Oxymoron" and "dilemma" — rhetorical and lexical definitions (Silva Rhetoricae, Wiktionary, Online Etymology Dictionary)

## A. Silva Rhetoricae — "oxymoron"

Source: Gideon O. Burton, Silva Rhetoricae (rhetoric.byu.edu), Brigham Young
  University, entry "oxymoron". Licensed CC BY 3.0 (per the page footer).
Original: http://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/O/oxymoron.htm
Retrieved: 2026-09-26 (HTTP 200)

> "oxymoron ox-y-mo'-ron from Gk. oxy, "sharp" and moros, "dull"
>  acutifatuum wise-folly Placing two ordinarily opposing terms adjacent to
>  one another. A compressed paradox. Examples ...Yet from those flames No
>  light, but rather darkness visible Served only to discover sights of woe.
>  —Milton, Paradise Lost 1.62-64 The Sounds of Silence Festina lente (make
>  haste slowly). Related Figures paradox irony synoeciosis"

## B. Wiktionary — "oxymoron" (English noun)

Source: Wiktionary contributors, "oxymoron", English section (CC BY-SA 4.0).
Original: https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/oxymoron (raw wikitext read via
  action=raw)
Retrieved: 2026-09-26

> "(rhetoric) A figure of speech in which two words or phrases with opposing
>  meanings are used together intentionally for effect."  (sense 1)

> "(loosely, sometimes, proscribed) A contradiction in terms."  (sense 2)

> "Historically, an oxymoron was "a paradox with a point". [...] The common
>  vernacular use of oxymoron as simply a contradiction in terms is
>  considered incorrect by some speakers and writers"  (usage notes; the
>  first phrase is referenced there to Jebb's 1900 commentary on
>  Sophocles' Antigone)

> "The compound form ὀξύμωρον is not found in the extant Ancient Greek
>  sources."  (etymology, referenced to the OED)

Examples listed on the page (verbatim): "controlled chaos", "deafening
silence", "make haste slowly", "open secret", "tough love",
"tragicomedy"; Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet: "Parting is such sweet
sorrow."

## C. Online Etymology Dictionary — "oxymoron", "dilemma"

Source: Douglas Harper, Online Etymology Dictionary, entries "oxymoron" and
  "dilemma" (copyrighted; excerpts only).
Original: https://www.etymonline.com/word/oxymoron ;
  https://www.etymonline.com/word/dilemma
Retrieved: 2026-09-26 (HTTP 200)

> "oxymoron (n.) in rhetoric, "a figure conjoining words or terms
>  apparently contradictory so as to give point to the statement or
>  expression," 1650s, from Greek oxymōron, noun use of neuter of oxymōros
>  (adj.) "pointedly foolish," [...] Now often used loosely to mean
>  "contradiction in terms.""

> "dilemma (n.) 1520s in rhetoric (see below), from Late Latin dilemma,
>  from Greek dilemma "double proposition," a technical term in rhetoric,
>  from di- "two" (see di- (1)) + lemma "premise, anything received or
>  taken," [...] A form of argument in which it is shown that whoever
>  maintains a certain proposition must accept one or other of two
>  alternative conclusions, and that each of these involves the denial of
>  the proposition in question. [Century Dictionary] Loosely, "choice
>  between two undesirable alternatives," from 1580s."

Relied on for: the rhetorical sense of "oxymoron" versus its loose sense
"contradiction in terms", and the disagreement over the loose sense; the
logical/rhetorical sense of "dilemma" versus its loose sense.
Context: dictionaries report usage; they are cited here for what the word
has been used to mean, not for what a dilemma or an oxymoron "really" is.

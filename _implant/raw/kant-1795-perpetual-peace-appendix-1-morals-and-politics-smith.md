# Kant 1795 — Perpetual Peace, Appendix I: no quarrel between morals and politics, the moral politician, "Fiat justitia, pereat mundus"

Source: Immanuel Kant, Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Essay (Zum ewigen
  Frieden, 1795), trans. M. Campbell Smith, London: Swan Sonnenschein, first
  edition 1903; Appendix I, "On the Disagreement between Morals and Politics
  with Reference to Perpetual Peace" (Ak. VIII 370–380).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/50922/pg50922.txt
  (Project Gutenberg eBook #50922; Campbell Smith translation 1903; public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain-text Gutenberg file read)

> "Hence there can be no quarrel between politics, as the practical science of right, and morals, which is also a science of right, but theoretical."  (App. I, opening paragraph)

> "Although the saying, “Honesty is the best policy,” expresses a theory which, alas, is often contradicted in practice, yet the likewise theoretical maxim, “Honesty is better than any policy,” is exalted high above every possible objection, is indeed the necessary condition of all politics."  (App. I)

> "I can thus imagine a moral politician, that is to say, one who understands the principles of statesmanship to be such as do not conflict with morals; but I cannot conceive of a political moralist who fashions for himself such a system of ethics as may serve the interest of statesmen."  (App. I)

> "_Fiat justitia, pereat mundus._ This saying has become proverbial, and although it savours a little of boastfulness, is also true."  (App. I)

Translator's note to App. I (Campbell Smith, note 147, not Kant):

> "This connection between the sciences was denied by Machiavelli, who lays down the dictum that, in the relations of sovereigns and states, the ordinary rules of morality do not apply."  (note 147 [Tr.])

Relied on for: Kant's own statement, in a public-domain translation, of the
view that morals and politics cannot genuinely conflict and that a ruler's
principles must not "conflict with morals" — the Kantian "no" to dirty hands
that Coady (SEP §3) reports under absolutism ("Absolutist positions have
been explicitly held by many philosophers, notably Augustine, Aquinas, and
Kant"); the maxim that Walzer (2004a, as quoted by Coady §7) rejects in the
form "Do justice though the heavens fall".
Context: The sentence before the first excerpt argues from "ultra posse
nemo obligatur" (no one is obliged beyond what they can do). Kant's gloss on
the fiat justitia maxim immediately limits it: it "must not, however, be
misunderstood as allowing anyone to exercise his own rights with the utmost
severity". Campbell Smith's note 147 also quotes Hume (Treatise III.ii.11)
on "a system of morals calculated for princes"; that sentence was checked
in the Hume Gutenberg text (eBook #4705, Book III Part II Sect. XI), where
Hume adds that the morality of princes "has the same extent, yet it has not
the same force as that of private persons". Kant's separate essay on a
supposed right to lie was not fetched and is not cited.

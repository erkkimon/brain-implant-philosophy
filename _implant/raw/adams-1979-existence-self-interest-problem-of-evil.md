# Adams 1979 — "Existence, Self-Interest, and the Problem of Evil": we owe our existence to prior evils; the fuel-conservation case

Source: Robert Merrihew Adams, "Existence, Self-Interest, and the Problem
  of Evil", Noûs 13(1) (1979): 53–65. Pages 53, 54, 57–58.
Original: doi 10.2307/2214795 (DOI content negotiation 2026-09-27: Noûs

> "The first contribution is a proof that if our lives will have been worth living on the whole, we cannot have been injured by"  (p. 53; continues on p. 54: "most of the evils that preceded our coming to be")

> "If it had not been for the First World War, for example, my par-ents would probably never have met and married, and I would not have been born."  (p. 54)

> "But if their lives are worth living it would not have been better for them if we had followed a policy of fuel conservation. For they would not have existed in that case."  (p. 57; "they" are descendants living "in the year 2140" after "we continue to squander the earth's resources")

> "If it followed further that there is nothing wrong with squandering the earth's resources, that consequence would discredit my argument. But it does not follow."  (p. 57)

> "The chief reason why we ought to conserve is to be found in the concern we ought to have about the kind of society to which our actions will lead in the future."  (p. 58; the scan prints "T h e chief reason")

Relied on for: Adams's statement of the non-identity point (1979) in a
  theodicy context, and his claim that the wrongness of squandering does
  not follow from it — conservation is grounded in concern for "the kind
  of society" our actions lead to.
Context: the paper opens with Leibniz's reply to the complaint "why didn't
  God give you more strength" ("you would not be you") and argues (§I)
  that "we would not be ourselves without many and great evils". The
  energy-policy passage is point (1) of §II "The Ethics of Creation",
  which also says "Squandering may even be unfair to future individuals if
  it increases the probability of their having lives so miserable that
  one would wish for their sake that they had not existed" (p. 57). Kavka
  1982 (n. 1) cites p. 57 of this paper as Adams's statement of the
  argument; Parfit 1984 (Part Four, n. 10) writes "I follow ADAMS (3)".

# Entman 1993 — "To frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality…"

Source: Robert M. Entman (1993), "Framing: Toward Clarification of a
  Fractured Paradigm", Journal of Communication 43(4): 51–58 (Autumn
  1993).
Original: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (typeset PDF of the journal pages read at
  https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/J-Communication-1993-Entman.pdf
  — a course-reading copy on Frank Baumgartner's UNC teaching page; page
  numbers from the printed folios 51–58. The scan's OCR has minor
  artefacts (e.g. "aperceived", "i n"), corrected silently below to the
  printed wording. DOI verified by content negotiation: Journal of
  Communication 43(4) 51–58, issued 1993-12-01.)

> "Framing essentially involves selection and salience. To frame is to
>  select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient
>  in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular
>  problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or
>  treatment recommendation for the item described."  (p. 52; the second
>  sentence is italicised in the original)

> "Frames, then, define problems—determine what a causal agent is doing
>  with what costs and benefits, usually measured in terms of common
>  cultural values; diagnose causes—identify the forces creating the
>  problem; make moral judgments—evaluate causal agents and their
>  effects; and suggest remedies—offer and justify treatments for the
>  problems and predict their likely effects. A single sentence may
>  perform more than one of these four framing functions, although many
>  sentences in a text may perform none of them. And a frame in any
>  particular text may not necessarily include all four functions."
>  (p. 52)

> "The cold war example also suggests that frames have at least four
>  locations in the communication process: the communicator, the text,
>  the receiver, and the culture."  (p. 52)

> "Frames highlight some bits of information about an item that is the
>  subject of a communication, thereby elevating them in salience. The
>  word salience itself needs to be defined: It means making a piece of
>  information more noticeable, meaningful, or memorable to audiences."
>  (p. 53)

Relied on for: the standard definition of framing in communication
studies (selection + salience); the four framing functions (problem
definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, treatment
recommendation); the four locations of frames; the definition of
salience.
Context: A programmatic essay proposing that communication become a
"master discipline" that synthesises scattered concepts, with framing as
the case study. The definition opens the section "Of Frames and Framing"
(p. 52) and is illustrated by the "cold war" news frame; "How Frames
Work" (pp. 53–54) develops salience and schemata. The paper is
conceptual and reports no experiments.

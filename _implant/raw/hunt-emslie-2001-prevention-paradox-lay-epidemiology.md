# Hunt & Emslie 2001 — the prevention paradox in lay epidemiology (commentary on Rose 1985)

Source: Kate Hunt and Carol Emslie, "Commentary: The prevention paradox in lay
  epidemiology—Rose revisited", International Journal of Epidemiology 30(3)
  (2001), 442–446.
Original: https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/30.3.442
  (copyrighted, © International Epidemiological Association; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-09 (DOI verified by CSL-JSON content negotiation; text read
  from https://web.archive.org/web/20060621190144/http://ije.oxfordjournals.org/cgi/content/full/30/3/442 ,
  the snapshot linked from the Wikipedia article)

> "Our use of ‘lay epidemiology’ follows Davison et al. who describe this as ‘a scheme in which individuals interpret health risks through the routine observation and discussion of cases of illness and death in personal networks and in the public arena’"  (section 1)

> "The first inexplicable ‘anomaly’ is ‘the unwarranted survivor’, the ‘candidate’ who survives to a ‘ripe old age’ against the odds."  ("Lay epidemiology, ‘coronary candidacy’ and the prevention paradox")

> "Davison et al. conclude that, ‘It is ironic that such evidently fatalistic cultural concepts should be given more rather than less explanatory power by the activities of modern health education, whose stated goals lie in the opposite direction’."  (same section; Davison et al. 1991, p. 16)

> "We would argue that the causes of individual cases (why this person and why now?) take greater precedence for the lay epidemiologist."  ("Lay and professional epidemiology")

> "In conclusion, we would argue that there are many parallels between lay and professional epidemiology, and that the ‘prevention paradox’ is a key site of disquiet within both."  (conclusion)

Relied on for: the lay-epidemiology reading of the prevention paradox (Davison,
  Davey Smith & Frankel 1991, as Hunt & Emslie report it) and Hunt & Emslie's
  own conclusion.
Context: The commentary accompanies the 2001 reprint of Rose 1985. It quotes
  Rose 1981 p. 1850 (the "49 out of 50" passage) and draws on qualitative
  interviews in South Wales (Davison et al.) and the west of Scotland
  (Emslie, Hunt & Watt). Reference 1: Davison C, Davey Smith G, Frankel S,
  Sociology of Health and Illness 1991; 13: 1–19.

> "Preparedness paradox: After preparing to avoid a catastrophe and lessening the damage, the perception regarding the catastrophe would be much less serious due to the limited damage caused after."  (List of paradoxes, "Decision theory")

> "The preparedness paradox is the proposition that if a society or individual acts effectively to mitigate a potential disaster such as a pandemic, natural disaster or other catastrophe so that it causes less harm, the avoided danger will be perceived as having been much less serious because of the limited damage actually caused."  (article, lead)

> "The paradox is the incorrect perception that there had been no need for careful preparation as there was little harm, although in reality the limitation of the harm was due to preparation."  (article, lead; cited to the NPR interview with Kayyem, 2022)

> "Several cognitive biases can consequently hamper proper preparation for future risks."  (article, lead; cited to the NPR interview with Kayyem, 2022)

> "The term "preparedness paradox" has been used occasionally since at least 1949 in different contexts, usually in the military and financial system."  (article, "Background")

> "There is a preparedness paradox that continually confronts military planners."  (article, quotation field of the citation to the 1949 Senate Armed Services subcommittee hearings, p. 79, attributed to General Bradley; Google Books id W4E0AAAAIAAJ — the hearing record was not read here)

> "A preparedness paradox exists. On the one hand, to prepare the public to be able to act"  (article, quotation field of the citation to Ruggiero & Vos 2014, Journal of Contingencies and Crisis Management 23(3) 138–148, doi:10.1111/1468-5973.12065 — DOI verified by content negotiation 2026-10-09; the paper's abstract was read via Crossref and does not contain the phrase; the full text was not read)

> "Organisms with faster life histories and shorter lives are disproportionately affected by chaotic or hostile environments."  (article, "Cognitive biases"; cited to Kruger, Fernandes, Cupal & Homish 2019, Evolutionary Behavioral Sciences 13(3) 242–253, doi:10.1037/ebs0000129 — DOI verified 2026-10-09; no abstract available, the paper was not read)

> "However, organisms with slower life histories, such as humans, may have less urgency in dealing with these types of events."  (article, "Cognitive biases"; same citation)

> "Achilles had overtaken the Tortoise, and had seated himself comfortably on its back."  (p. 278)

> "Well now, would you like to hear of a race-course, that most people fancy they can get to the end of in two or three steps, while it really consists of an infinite number of distances, each one longer than the previous one?"  (p. 278)

> "(A)  Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other."  (p. 278)

> "(B)  The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same."  (p. 278)

> "(Z)  The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other."  (p. 278)

> "Readers of Euclid will grant, I suppose, that Z follows logically from A and B, so that any one who accepts A and B as true, must accept Z as true?"  (p. 278)

> "Well, now, I want you to consider me as a reader of the second kind, and to force me, logically, to accept Z as true."  (p. 279)

> "(C)  If A and B are true, Z must be true."  (p. 279)

> "Then I must ask you to accept C."  (p. 279)

> "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated.  "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it?  And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"  (p. 279)

> "(D)  If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true."  (p. 279)

> "Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!"  (Achilles, p. 280)

> "Logic would tell you 'You can't help yourself.  Now that you've accepted A and B and C and D, you must accept Z!'  So you've no choice, you see."  (Achilles, p. 280)

> "Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down," said the Tortoise.  (p. 280)

> "(E)  If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true."  (p. 280)

> "Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z.  So it's quite a necessary step, you see?"  (p. 280)

> "Unless I've lost count, that makes a thousand and one.  There are several millions more to come."  (p. 280)

Relied on for: the dialogue's setting, the propositions A, B, Z, C, D, E,
the Tortoise's challenge and the regress; Achilles' appeal to Logic.
Context: the dialogue opens on Zeno's race-course ("an infinite series of
distances"), then the Tortoise offers "a race-course" of "an infinite number
of distances, each one longer than the previous one", taken from Euclid's
First Proposition. The Tortoise distinguishes a reader who does not accept
A and B and one who accepts A and B "but I don't accept the Hypothetical";
it asks to be treated as the second. It accepts each hypothetical once
written down and still withholds Z. The narrator returns months later to
find the note-book "nearly full"; the piece ends with puns on "Taught-Us"
and "A Kill-Ease".

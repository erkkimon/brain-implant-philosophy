# SEP "Social Choice Theory" (List) — §3.4 The liberal paradox

Source: Christian List, "Social Choice Theory", The Stanford Encyclopedia
  of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.);
  first published 2013-12-18, substantive revision 2022-10-14. §3.4.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/social-choice/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (read in a text extraction of the archived page;
  every quotation below checked against it)

> "Although the weak Pareto principle is arguably one of the least contentious ones of Arrow’s conditions, Sen (1970a) offered a critique of it that applies when the aggregation rule is interpreted not as a voting method, but as a social evaluation method which a social planner can use to rank social alternatives in an order of social desirability."  (§3.4)

> "Sen showed that, in this setting, the Pareto principle conflicts with a ‘liberal’ principle, which—he suggested—a social planner ought to respect."  (§3.4)

> "Intuitively, each individual has a personal sphere in which this individual alone should be able to decide what happens."  (§3.4)

> "The requirement is ‘minimal’ because we would ideally want not just two individuals, but everyone to have such rights, and we would ideally want those rights to concern more than a single pair of alternatives each."  (§3.4)

> "So, we are faced with a social preference cycle"  (§3.4; after the Lewd/Prude example)

> "Sen generalized this problem—now known as the ‘liberal paradox’—as follows."  (§3.4)

> "Theorem (Sen 1970a): There exists no preference aggregation rule satisfying universal domain, acyclicity of social preferences, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism."  (§3.4)

> "This result suggests that if we wish to respect individual rights, we may sometimes have to sacrifice Paretian efficiency."  (§3.4)

> "An alternative conclusion is that the weak Pareto principle can be rendered compatible with minimal liberalism only if the domain of admissible preference profiles is suitably restricted, for instance to preferences that are ‘tolerant’ or not ‘meddlesome’ (Blau 1975; Craven 1982; Gigliotti 1986; Sen 1983)."  (§3.4)

> "Lewd’s and Prude’s preferences in Sen’s example are ‘meddlesome’."  (§3.4)

> "However, several authors have challenged the relevance of Sen’s result, by arguing that his ‘minimal liberalism’ condition uses an inadequate formalization of the notion of individual rights (e.g., Gaertner, Pattanaik, and Suzumura 1992; Dowding and van Hees 2003)."  (§3.4)

Relied on for: the encyclopedia's statement of the theorem (with
  acyclicity), the "liberal paradox" name, the two conclusions it reports
  (sacrifice Pareto; restrict the domain), and the rights-formalization
  challenge with its named authors.
Context: §3.4 follows §§3.1–3.3 on Arrow's theorem and precedes §3.5 on

  personal sphere, a formal definition of minimal liberalism, and the
  Lewd/Prude version (x = Prude reads, y = Lewd reads, z = neither) of
  Sen's book example. Bibliography: Blau 1975, Rev. Econ. Stud. 42: 395–401;
  Craven 1982, Theory and Decision 14: 351–360; Dowding & van Hees 2003,
  Am. Pol. Sci. Rev. 97: 281–293; Gaertner, Pattanaik & Suzumura 1992,

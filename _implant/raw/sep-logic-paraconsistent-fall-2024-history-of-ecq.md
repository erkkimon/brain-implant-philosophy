> "It is now standard to view ex contradictione quodlibet as valid."  (§1.2)

> "It was towards the end of the nineteenth century, when the study of logic achieved mathematical articulation, that an explosive logical theory became the standard."  (§1.2)

> "In antiquity, however, no one seems to have endorsed the validity of ECQ."  (§1.2)

> "Aristotle presented what is sometimes called the connexive principle: “it is impossible that the same thing should be necessitated by the being and by the not-being of the same thing” (Prior Analytic II 4 57b3)."  (§1.2)

> "In fact, it was a member of the Parvipontanians, William of Soissons, who discovered in the twelfth century what we now call the C.I. Lewis (independent) argument for ECQ (see Martin 1986)."  (§1.2)

> "The Cologne School of the late fifteenth century argued against ECQ by rejecting disjunctive syllogism (see Sylvan 2000)."  (§1.2)

> "Moreover, the logics developed by the major Buddhist logicians, Dignāga (5th century) and Dharmakīrti (7th century) do not embrace ECQ."  (§1.2)

Relied on for: the history of explosion as the authors give it —
nineteenth-century standardisation, Aristotle's connexive principle,
Abelard's containment account and its difficulty (Alberic of Paris),
William of Soissons and the "Lewis (independent) argument", the Cologne
School, Dignāga and Dharmakīrti.
Context: between the Aristotle and Soissons sentences the entry reports
Boethius and Abelard's two accounts of consequence (truth-preservation;
containment of sense), Abelard's view that containment captures
Aristotle's principle, Alberic of Paris's difficulty in the 1130s, and
the Parvipontanians who "embraced the truth-preservation account of
consequences and the ‘paradoxes’ that are associated with it".

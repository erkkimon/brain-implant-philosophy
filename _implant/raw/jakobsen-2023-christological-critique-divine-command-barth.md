# Jakobsen 2023 — A Christological critique of divine command theory: Barth as divine-command theorist

> "Both Karl Barth and John Hare take this route. According to Barth, ethics is an aid to hearing God’s voice, hearing what God commands. Where do we hear God’s voice? In the Word of God, that is, in Jesus Christ (Barth 1957b, vol. II.2, p. 559)."

> "When Barth structures his Ethics in a trinitarian manner—writing about the commands of God the creator, the redeemer, and the reconciler—the commands of God the reconciler form the material heart of Barth’s divine command theory, making Christology central to his applied ethics (Barth 1961, vol. III.4, pp. 24–25; 2013; Biggar 1993, p. 48)."

> "Barth is a divine-command theorist, holding that God’s command constitutes what is a right and wrong action"

> "As opposed to the other divine command theorists mentioned in this article, Barth also holds that what is good for humans is constituted by God’s command (Barth 1957b, vol. II.2, p. 547; McKenny 2021, p. 54)."  (note 5)

> "Although his normative and applied ethics are Christologically determined, his metaethics is not."

> "Because Hare and Barth do not connect the metaethical theory to Christ, they have not shown that divine command theory qualifies as a Christian moral theory."

Relied on for: Jakobsen's classification of Barth as a divine-command theorist and his criticism (attributed); locators CD II/2 pp. 547, 559 and III/4 pp. 24–25 as he cites them.
Context: Jakobsen argues divine command theory is a metaethical theory that is not specifically Christian, and proposes an alternative based on imitation of Christ. "Barth 1957b" is Church Dogmatics II/2 (Peabody: Hendrickson); "Barth 2013" is Ethics (Wipf & Stock).

# Hume 1739–40 — Treatise III.ii.11 "Of the Laws of Nations": a system of morals calculated for princes

Source: David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section XI
  "Of the Laws of Nations" (SBN 567–569).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4705/pg4705.txt
  (Project Gutenberg eBook #4705; English original; public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain-text Gutenberg file read; pointer found in
  Campbell Smith's translator's note 147 to Kant, Perpetual Peace, App. I)

> "There is a maxim very current in the world, which few politicians are willing to avow, but which has been authorized by the practice of all ages, that there is a system of morals calculated for princes, much more free than that which ought to govern private persons."  (III.ii.11)

> "The meaning, therefore, of this political maxim is, that though the morality of princes has the same extent, yet it has not the same force as that of private persons, and may lawfully be trangressed from a more trivial motive."  (III.ii.11)

> "we must necessarily give a greater indulgence to a prince or minister, who deceives another; than to a private gentleman, who breaks his word of honour."  (III.ii.11)

Relied on for: Hume's report of the maxim that princes have a freer morality,
and his own reading of it (same extent, less force), in the Thinkers list.
Context: Hume grounds the reading in his account of justice: the natural
obligation to justice between states is "not so strong as among
individuals" because commerce between states is less necessary. He does
not use the phrase "dirty hands". "trangressed" is the Gutenberg text's
spelling.

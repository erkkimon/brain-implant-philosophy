> "But these moral duties, it turns out, are not owed to the other animals, but rather to ourselves."  (p. 90)

> ". . . a human being has duties only to human beings (himself and others), since his duty to any subject is moral constraint by that subject’s will."  (p. 91, quoting Kant, Metaphysics of Morals 6:442 as her note 39 gives it)

> "On Kant’s conception of rationality, most of the beings I just mentioned are rational beings. Some of them are, for various reasons, unable to reason well; some of them are at stages of their lives when reason is undeveloped, inert, or non-functional. These conditions, I believe, do not affect their standing as rational beings under the Kantian conception."  (p. 82)

> "I am going to argue that despite appearances, and despite what he himself thought, Kant’s arguments reveal the ground of our obligations to the other animals."  (p. 82)

> "In taking ourselves to be ends-in-ourselves we legislate that the natural good of a creature who matters to itself is the source of normative claims. Animal nature is an end-in-itself, because our own legislation makes it so. And that is why we have duties to the other animals."  (p. 106)

Relied on for: Korsgaard's reading of Kant's indirect duties (her
citations: MM 6:442–44; LE 27:458–60, 27:710); her claim that Kant's
arguments ground obligations to animals; her 2004 treatment of infants
and the cognitively impaired as rational beings under the Kantian
conception.
Context: p. 82 continues "Those claims require defense, but I won't be
giving that defense in this essay." Her notes 38 and 30 add her own
criticisms of Kant's view of the emotions.

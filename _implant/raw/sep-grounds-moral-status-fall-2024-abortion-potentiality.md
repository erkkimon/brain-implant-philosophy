# SEP "The Grounds of Moral Status" (Jaworska & Tannenbaum) — abortion, cognitive-capacity accounts, potentiality and future-like-ours

Source: Agnieszka Jaworska and Julie Tannenbaum, "The Grounds of Moral
  Status", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), first
  published 2013-03-14, substantive revision 2021-03-03 (pubinfo line;
  authors from the copyright line). §§1, 5.1, 5.2.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/grounds-moral-status/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (HTML of the archive edition read)

> "Debates concerning abortion, stem cell research (see the entry on the ethics of stem cell research), and the question of what to do with unused frozen embryos from in vitro fertilization also rest on the theoretical question of the moral status of extremely underdeveloped human beings at various stages of development: zygote, embryo, and fetus (see section 5.2)."  (§1)

> "A stock objection to Sophisticated Cognitive Capacities accounts is their underinclusiveness."  (§5.1)

> "For example, infants lack sophisticated cognitive capacities, and so fail to meet this necessary condition for FMS."  (§5.1; FMS = full moral status)

> "This is usually labeled the “potential” account in the literature (e.g., Stone 1987), although some authors do not use this terminology, but rather speak, for example, of the wrongness of killing due to the loss of a “future like ours” (Marquis 1989 and 1995)."  (§5.2)

> "A potential US president has neither rights nor even a claim to command the military; likewise in the case of potentially cognitively sophisticated beings and the rights associated with moral status (Feinberg 1980, p.193)."  (§5.2)

> "While this particular analogy has been contested (Wilkins 1993, pp. 126–127 and Boonin 2003, pp. 46–49), one can appeal to other analogies"  (§5.2)

> "With respect to a future-like-ours, some argue that the loss of this potentiality is morally problematic only if the being is sufficiently psychologically connected to that future person, and a fetus arguably lacks this sufficient connection (McInerney 1990)."  (§5.2)

Relied on for: the SEP's statement that the abortion debate rests on the
  moral status of underdeveloped human beings; the underinclusiveness
  objection (infants) to cognitive-capacity accounts such as Warren's and
  Tooley's; its classing of Marquis's argument with potential accounts; the
  Feinberg president analogy and McInerney's connectedness objection, as
  the SEP reports them (Feinberg 1980 and McInerney 1990 not read).
Context: §5.1 lists Tooley 1972 p. 44 among cognitive-capacity accounts;
  §5.2 adds Boonin's (2003) view combining a future-like-ours with actual
  conscious desires, and notes that the Feinberg analogy "has been
  contested (Wilkins 1993, pp. 126–127 and Boonin 2003, pp. 46–49)". The
  entry has no section devoted to abortion.

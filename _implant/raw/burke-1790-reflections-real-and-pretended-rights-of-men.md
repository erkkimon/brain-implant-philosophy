# Burke 1790 — Reflections on the Revolution in France: "real" and "pretended" rights of men

Source: Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), in
  The Works of the Right Honourable Edmund Burke, vol. III (of 12), Project
  Gutenberg eBook #15679 (release date April 22, 2005), the passage on the
  "real rights of men" and the "pretended rights of these theorists".
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15679 (public domain — Burke
  d. 1797)
Retrieved: 2026-10-15 (plain-text eBook fetched with curl and grepped;
  quotations matched by script; the Gutenberg italics markers around
  "real" and "middle" are omitted)

> "In denying their false claims of right, I do not mean to injure those which are real, and are such as their pretended rights would totally destroy."

> "If civil society be made for the advantage of man, all the advantages for which it is made become his right."

> "Men cannot enjoy the rights of an uncivil and of a civil state together."

> "Government is not made in virtue of natural rights, which may and do exist in total independence of it,--and exist in much greater clearness, and in a much greater degree of abstract perfection: but their abstract perfection is their practical defect."

> "The pretended rights of these theorists are all extremes; and in proportion as they are metaphysically true, they are morally and politically false."

> "The rights of men in governments are their advantages; and these are often in balances between differences of good,--in compromises sometimes between good and evil, and sometimes between evil and evil."

Relied on for: positions/human-rights.md — Burke's objection to abstract
  ("metaphysic") rights of man and his own account of "real" rights as the
  advantages of civil society.
Context: the passage opens "Far am I from denying in theory, full as far
  is my heart from withholding in practice, (if I were of power to give or
  to withhold,) the real rights of men." Between the quoted sentences Burke
  argues that civil society is "the offspring of convention" and that
  "Government is a contrivance of human wisdom to provide for human wants".

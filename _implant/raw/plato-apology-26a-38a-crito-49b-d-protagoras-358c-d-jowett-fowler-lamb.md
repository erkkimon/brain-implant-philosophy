## Apology 26a — corrupting the young involuntarily

> "But either I do not corrupt them, or I corrupt them unintentionally; and on either view of the case you lie."  (Jowett)

> "but either I do not corrupt them, or if I corrupt them, I do it involuntarily, so that you are lying in both events. But if I corrupt them involuntarily, for such involuntary errors the law is not to hale people into court, but to take them and instruct and admonish them in private."  (26a, Fowler)

## Apology 38a — the examined life

> "and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you are still less likely to believe me."  (Jowett)

> "is the greatest good to man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you will believe me still less."  (38a, Fowler)

## Crito 49b–d — never do wrong, not even in return

> "Then we ought not to do wrong at all."  (49b, Fowler)

> "Then we ought neither to requite wrong with wrong nor to do evil to anyone, no matter what he may have done to us."  (49c, Fowler)

> "for I know that there are few who believe or ever will believe this. Now those who believe this, and those who do not, have no common ground of discussion"  (49d, Fowler)

> "Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to any one, whatever evil we may have suffered from him."  (Jowett)

## Protagoras 358c–d — no one willingly goes after evil

> "Then surely, I went on, no one willingly goes after evil or what he thinks to be evil;"  (358c, Lamb)

> "it is not in human nature, apparently, to do so—to wish to go after what one thinks to be evil in preference to the good"  (358d, Lamb)

> "Then, I said, no man voluntarily pursues evil, or that which he thinks to be evil."  (Jowett)

Relied on for: the texts behind "no one does wrong willingly", the
involuntary-error defence, the examined life and the Crito principle.
Context: Apology 25c–26a is Socrates' cross-examination of Meletus; 38a
is from the penalty phase, after the verdict; Crito 49 is the principle
Socrates asks Crito to accept before the personified laws speak;
Protagoras 358 follows the argument that "being overcome by pleasure" is
ignorance.

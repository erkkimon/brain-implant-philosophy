> "Jonathan Dancy (2000), who discusses the history of this distinction, notes that this contemporary classification doesn’t always map neatly onto their alleged ancestors, for instance, Francis Hutcheson’s (1728)."  (§1)

> "On an influential view (Baier 1958; Dancy 2000; Hieronymi 2011), this is just a distinction concerning different things reasons can do."  (§1)

> "When I call a reason “motivating”, all that I am doing is issuing a reminder that the focus of our attention is on matters of motivation, for the moment."  (§1, quoting Dancy 2000: 2–3)

> "Another important argument turns on the idea that any account of motivating reasons must meet what Dancy (1995; 2000) calls “the normative constraint”:"  (§3.1)

> "Dancy’s point is that psychologism fails to meet this constraint—in fact, he says, psychologism has the consequence that “the reasons why we act can never be among the reasons in favour of acting” (2000: 105)."  (§3.1)

> "The view is defended or endorsed by many, including Dancy (2000, 2008, 2014), Hornsby (2007, 2008), Setiya (2007), Schroeder (2008), McDowell (2013), Comesaña & McGrath (2014), and Singh (2019)."  (§3.1, the view that a motivating reason is "a putative fact that the agent takes to obtain")

> "Against this, it has been argued that the proposal leads to paradox or infelicitous claims."  (§3.1)

> "A version of the ability condition features in Dancy’s influential argument against psychologism about motivating reasons."  (§4.1)

> "[i]n the best case, there is some good reason for doing the action, and the reason that motivates the agent coincides with that reason. (2000: 3)"  (§4.2, quoting Dancy)

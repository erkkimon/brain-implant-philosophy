> "Dewey’s ethics replaces the goal of identifying an ultimate end or supreme ethical principle with the goal of identifying a method for improving our value judgments."  (preamble)

> "Dewey argued that ethical inquiry is the use of reflective intelligence to revise our judgments in light of the consequences of acting on them."  (preamble)

> "The conditions of warrant for value judgments lie in human conduct, not in any a priori fixed reference point outside of conduct, such as God’s commands, Platonic Forms, pure reason, or nature. Dewey offers a naturalistic metaethic of value judgments, grounded in developmental and social psychology."  (preamble)

> "There are three levels of conduct: impulse, habit, and reflective action."  (§1)

> "Dewey’s metaethics is grounded in the distinction between valuing and evaluation (also denoted as “prizing” vs. “appraising”, or “esteeming” vs. “estimating,”)."  (§2.1)

> "Similarly, we test value judgments by acting on them and seeing if we value the consequences in the way the judgment predicted."  (§2.4)

> "Even the best confirmed value judgments can hold only provisionally."  (§2.4)

> "Dewey’s moral epistemology is contextualist."  (§2.5)

> "He rejected any conception of intrinsic value as some kind of existence or property that has value in itself, regardless of context, which is the object of practice to bring about, realize, or conform to."  (§2.5)

> "The standard objection to Dewey’s instrumental theory of value judgments is that it concerns the value of things as means only, and not as ends."  (§3)

> "or Dewey’s theory reduces to a form of Humean instrumentalism, in which ends are given by our desires or immediate likings, and the only question is how to satisfy them."  (§3)

> "Dewey’s reply to this objection goes to the heart of his moral philosophy. He argued that the character and value of means and ends was reciprocally determined."  (§3)

> "It is irrational to take one’s end as fixed before investigating the costs of the means and the consequences of achieving the end (TV 214)."  (§3.1)

> "This is the truth that moralities of intention grasp, which narrowly consequentialist theories do not (E 173–5, 286–9)."  (§3.4)

> "Dewey declined to offer substantive answers to the traditional questions posed by these theories, arguing that no fixed ends or moral rules could be adequate in a world of constant change and plural and conflicting values."  (§4)

> "Hence, the tension among the three types of moral consideration is permanent and cannot be resolved by reducing one to another or insisting that one automatically overrides the others (TIF)."  (§4)

> "Informed desire theories of the good, which define the good in terms of what an individual would desire if fully informed, come closest to Dewey’s own account."  (§4.1)

> "Pragmatism in ethics is often regarded as a form of teleology or consequentialism. Yet Dewey rejected accounts of the right that defined it in terms of promoting the good (E 214–216)."  (§4.2)

> "Dewey endorsed the “empty formalism” critique of Kant’s Categorical Imperative, insofar as it aspires to reach moral conclusions without presupposing anything to be good."  (§4.2)

> "This forward-looking view of praise and blame enabled Dewey to avoid the problem of free will in its connection with responsibility."  (§4.3)

> "Democracy, in Dewey’s view, is the means by which we practice intelligent moral inquiry together, seeking solutions to the problems we face together (PP)."  (§4.4)

> "His and James Tufts’s Ethics begins, not with a review of rival moral theories, but with a survey of anthropology and a brief history of the moral problems and practices of the ancient Hebrews, Greeks, and Romans."  (§6)

> "Dewey, like Hegel, stressed the ways abstract philosophical doctrines are socially embodied."  (§6)

> "Thus, in contrast with his voluminous political commentaries, Dewey published very little on personal “applied ethics.”"  (§6)

> "Dewey was a founding member of the NAACP and the ACLU."  (§6)

> "Experimentalism in ethics leads to a democratic political philosophy."  (§6)

> "[TIF] “Three Independent Factors in Morals,” in Later Works, vol. 5 (1930)."  (Abbreviations)

# Tooley 1972 — "Abortion and Infanticide": the self-consciousness requirement and the potentiality principle

Source: Michael Tooley, "Abortion and Infanticide", Philosophy & Public
  Affairs 2(1) (Autumn 1972), pp. 37–65.
Original: JSTOR stable URL http://www.jstor.org/stable/2264919 (no DOI found

> "The problem the liberal encounters is essentially that of specifying a cutoff point which is not arbitrary: at what stage in the development of a human being does it cease to be morally permissible to destroy it?"  (p. 38)

> "The claim I wish to defend is this: An organism possesses a serious right to life only if it possesses the concept of a self as a continuing subject of experiences and other mental states, and believes that it is itself such a continuing entity."  (p. 44)

> "Why should it be seriously wrong to kill an unborn member of the species Homo sapiens but not seriously wrong to kill an unborn kitten? Difference in species is not per se a morally relevant difference."  (p. 51)

> "The second claim-which I shall refer to as the potentiality principle-is critical to the conservative's defense."  (p. 56)

> "In any case, the important point here is that the conservative position on abortion is acceptable only if the potentiality principle is sound."  (p. 57)

> "One is therefore forced to conclude that the conservative's potentiality principle is false."  (p. 62)

> "This is obviously a matter for detailed psychological investigation, but everyday observation makes it perfectly clear, I believe, that a newborn baby does not possess the concept of a continuing self, any more than a newborn kitten possesses such a concept. If so, infanticide during a time interval shortly after birth must be morally acceptable."  (p. 63)

Relied on for: the self-consciousness requirement in Tooley's words; his
  framing of the liberal's cutoff problem; his claim that the conservative
  position depends on the potentiality principle and his conclusion that
  the principle is false; his conclusion on infanticide.
Context: pp. 40–41 introduce the kitten contrast (torturing vs. killing a
  newborn kitten); pp. 59–62 argue against the potentiality principle with a
  hypothetical chemical that would make kittens develop into rational beings
  and a "moral symmetry principle"; p. 63 then asks "where is the line to be
  drawn?" and leaves the cutoff after birth as an empirical question. Tooley's
  later book, Abortion and Infanticide (Oxford, 1983), was not read.

# Minsky 1986 — The Society of Mind §5.5 "Fashion and Style": Fredkin's Paradox

> "It can save a lot of mental work if one makes each arbitrary choice the way one did before. The more difficult the decision, the more this policy can save. The following observation by my associate, Edward Fredkin, seems important enough to deserve a name:"  (§5.5, p. 52)

> "Fredkin’s Paradox: The more equally attractive two alternatives seem, the harder it can be to choose between them—no matter that, to the same degree, the choice can only matter less."  (§5.5, p. 52)

> "No wonder we often can’t account for “taste”—if it depends on hidden rules that we use when ordinary reasons cancel out!"  (§5.5, p. 52)

> "When should we quit reasoning and take recourse in rules of style? Only when we're fairly sure that further thought will just waste time."  (§5.5, p. 52)

> "Perhaps they’re how our minds remind themselves not to abandon thought too recklessly."  (§5.5, p. 52)

> "Predictability: It makes no difference whether a single car drives on the left or on the right. But it makes all the difference when there are many cars! Societies need rules that make no sense for individuals."  (§5.5, p. 52)

Relied on for: the statement of Fredkin's paradox, its attribution to
Fredkin by Minsky, the name given by Minsky, its locator, and the context in
which Minsky uses it (styles, fashions and habitual arbitrary choices as
savers of "mental work").
Context: §5.5 opens with an epigraph from Artur Schnabel and asks "Why do we
like so many things that seem to us to have no earthly use?"; it lists
"Recognizability", "Uniformity" and "Predictability" as practical reasons
for choosing by style; after the paradox Minsky says fashion, style and art
"often share this strategy of using forms that lie beneath the surface of our
thoughts", and the last sentence quoted above concerns "fleeting hints of
guilt we sometimes feel for “just liking” art". Line breaks of the scan have
been joined; hyphenation at line ends removed. The archive.org scan's own
metadata gives 1988 (the Touchstone printing); the copyright page reads
"Copyright © 1985, 1986 by Marvin Minsky".

> "I ask what it would take for conscious AI to be a realistic prospect,
>  challenging the assumption that computation provides a sufficient basis
>  for consciousness. I'll instead make the case that consciousness
>  depends on our nature as living organisms – a form of biological
>  naturalism. I lay out a range of scenarios for conscious AI, concluding
>  that real artificial consciousness is unlikely along current
>  trajectories, but becomes more plausible as AI becomes more brain-like
>  and/or life-like."  (Abstract)

> "If we sell our minds too cheaply to our machine creations, we not only
>  overestimate them – we underestimate ourselves."  (Abstract)

> "A definitive answer to this question is not currently possible, given
>  the lack of consensus about the minimally sufficient conditions for
>  consciousness."  (§1.0)

The thesis, defined:

> "In its original formulation, introduced by John Searle, it proposes
>  that conscious states are higher-level irreducible properties of
>  lower-level neurobiological states (Searle, 2017). I use biological
>  naturalism in a slightly different but now widespread way, as the claim
>  that consciousness is a property of only (but not necessarily all)
>  living systems. Biological naturalism should be distinguished from
>  'biopsychism', which is that claim that all living systems are
>  conscious"  (§4.0)

> "The fact that mental processes can often be usefully described in terms
>  of computation is not sufficient to conclude that the brain actually
>  computes, or that consciousness is a form of computation. For conscious
>  AI to be possible for conventional digital systems, it needs to be the
>  case that the relevant computations provide a sufficient basis for
>  consciousness. This requires both that computational functionalism
>  holds, and that AI systems can implement the relevant computations. The
>  fact that computational functionalism is frequently assumed does not
>  mean it is true."  (§3)

Relied on for: the definition of (Seth's) biological naturalism and its
contrast with Searle's and with biopsychism; the counter-position to the
computational-functionalist working assumption of Butlin et al. 2023;
Seth's verdict that conscious AI is "unlikely along current
trajectories" but not impossible in principle.
Context: the article is a BBS target article (with open peer commentary
to follow); §2 lists "biases" (e.g. the tendency to equate intelligence
with consciousness), §4 argues from predictive processing and the free
energy principle that consciousness is tied to self-maintaining living
systems, and Table 1 lays out five scenarios for conscious AI. The
closing sections on "seemingly conscious AI" ethics are not reproduced.

# Schopenhauer 1840 — On the Basis of Morality: compassion, critique of Kant, animals, lies (Bullock tr.)

Source: Arthur Schopenhauer, *The Basis of Morality* (Preisschrift über
  die Grundlage der Moral, dated Copenhagen 30 January 1840), translated
  with introduction and notes by Arthur Brodrick Bullock, London: Swan
  Sonnenschein, 1903. Locators are Part.Chapter of Bullock's contents.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/44929 (Project Gutenberg eBook
  #44929; public domain); scans: https://archive.org/details/basisofmoralitys00schouoft
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (Gutenberg plain text fetched with curl and grepped;
  line breaks joined; Gutenberg's =bold= markers removed, _italics_ kept)

> "Is the fountain and basis of Morals to be sought for in an idea of morality which lies directly in the consciousness (or conscience), and in the analysis of the other leading ethical conceptions which arise from it? or is it to be found an some other source of knowledge?"  (The Question; the Society's prize question, sic "an")

> "It is, what we see every day,--the phaenomenon of Compassion; in other words, the direct participation, independent of all ulterior considerations, in the sufferings of another, leading to sympathetic assistance in the effort to prevent or remove them;"  (III.v)

> "It is this Compassion alone which is the real basis of all voluntary justice and all genuine loving-kindness. Only so far as an action springs therefrom, has it moral value; and all conduct that proceeds from any other motive whatever has none."  (III.v)

> "It is, in fact, the great mystery of Ethics, its original phaenomenon, and the boundary stone, past which only transcendental speculation may dare to take a step."  (III.v)

> "_Neminem laede; immo omnes, quantum potes, juva._ (Do harm to no one; but rather help all people, as far as lies in your power.)"  (III.v)

> "The absence of all egoistic motives is thus the Criterion of an action of moral value."  (III.iv)

> "For as the fundamental springs of all human conduct (_v_. Chapter V. of this Part), are three, namely, Egoism, Malice, Compassion;"  (III.vii)

> "Boundless compassion for all living beings is the surest and most certain guarantee of pure moral conduct, and needs no casuistry."  (III.viii)

> "This conception, equally with the kindred notions of Law, Command, Obligation, etc., taken thus unconditionally, has its source in theological morals, and it will remain a stranger to philosophical morals, so long as it fails to furnish sufficient credentials drawn either from man's nature, or from the objective world."  (II.ii; "this conception" is that of duty)

> "From this analysis it is abundantly clear that Kant's famous leading principle is not--as he maintains with tireless repetition--a categorical, but in reality a hypothetical Imperative;"  (II.vi)

> "its concealed hypothetical nature, whereby its basis turns out to be nothing else than Egoism, the latter being the secret interpreter of the direction which it contains."  (II.vi)

> "In the critical part of the present treatise I have sufficiently demonstrated that the conception of ought, in other words, the imperative form of Ethics, is valid only in theological morals, outside of which it loses all sense and meaning."  (III.ii)

> "So one is only to have compassion on animals for the sake of practice, and they are as it were the pathological phantom on which to train one's sympathy with men!"  (II.vi, on Kant, Tugendlehre §§16–17)

> "There is another proof that the moral incentive disclosed by me is the true one. I mean the fact that animals also are included under its protecting aegis."  (III.viii, point 7)

> "Compassion for animals is intimately connected with goodness of character, and it may be confidently asserted that he, who is cruel to living creatures, cannot be a good man."  (III.viii)

> "Every mendacious word is a wrong, excepting only when the occasion arises of defending oneself against violence or cunning."  (III.vi)

> "The writer attempts to show that compassion is the ultimate source of morality; but neither does his mode of discussion appear satisfactory to us, nor has he, in point of fact, succeeded in proving that such a foundation is adequate."  (Judgment of the Danish Royal Society of Sciences, Bullock's English)

> "Lastly, the Society cannot pass over in silence the fact that he mentions several recent philosophers of the highest standing in an unseemly manner, such as to justly occasion serions offence."  (Judgment; sic "serions")

> "[1] Thus, when the first gleam of _Mitleid_ stole into her heart, Brünhilde could no longer remain a Walküre;"  (III.vi, translator's note; the German word Bullock renders "Compassion")

Relied on for: compassion (Mitleid) as the sole moral incentive and the
  basis of justice and loving-kindness; the three springs of action; the
  criterion of moral worth; the principle Neminem laede; the critique of
  Kant (duty/ought as theological, the categorical imperative as
  hypothetical and egoistic); animals inside morality and his objection
  to Kant's indirect duty; lies in self-defence; the Danish Society's
  judgment.
Context: Part II is "Critique of Kant's Basis of Ethics", Part III "The
  Founding of Ethics", Part IV the metaphysical explanation, which the
  Society said should have come first. In II.vi and III.viii Schopenhauer
  attributes the exclusion of animals to "Judaism" and to Christian and
  Western ethics, and in III.viii praises Indian and Buddhist ethics on
  this point; those sentences are his and are not quoted on the page.
  The lies passage (III.vi) gives cases (a traveller asked about his
  money, a suitor) and adds that "there are cases where a falsehood is a
  duty"; it does not discuss Kant's murderer case, though nearby it calls
  Kant's arguments against lying inadequate.

> "Rights are entitlements (not) to perform certain actions, or (not) to be in certain states; or entitlements that others (not) perform certain actions or (not) be in certain states."  (preamble)

> "Moral rights are grounded in moral reasons, legal rights derive from the laws of the society, customary rights exist by local convention."  (§1)

> "For instance, natural rights are the sub-class of moral rights that humans have because of their nature."  (§1)

> "There has been much discussion, for example, of whether human rights are natural rights, whether the right to privacy is a legal right, and whether the legal right to life is a forfeitable right."  (§1)

> "The Hohfeldian system for describing the form of rights is widely accepted, although there are scholarly quarrels about its details."  (§2)

> "The four basic components of rights are known as “the Hohfeldian incidents” after Wesley Hohfeld (1879–1918), the American legal theorist who discovered them."  (§2.1)

> "These four basic “elements” are the privilege, the claim, the power, and the immunity."  (§2.1)

> "A has a privilege to φ if and only if A has no duty not to φ."  (§2.1.1)

> "A has a claim that B φ if and only if B has a duty to A to φ."  (§2.1.2)

> "A has a power if and only if A has the ability to alter her own or another’s Hohfeldian incidents."  (§2.1.3)

> "B has an immunity if and only if A lacks the ability to alter B’s Hohfeldian incidents."  (§2.1.4)

> "Hohfeld arranged the four incidents in tables of “opposites” and “correlatives” so as to display the logical structure of his system."  (§2.1.5)

> "Not all (collections of) Hohfeldian incidents are rights; it is only those (collections of) Hohfeldian incidents that have a certain function (or perhaps certain functions) that are rights."  (§2.2.1)

> "“All rights are claims” positions cannot recognize, for example, the rights in the Hobbesian state of nature, in which each person has unlimited privilege-rights of self-defense yet no claim-rights against attack."  (§2.2.1)

> "There are two main theories of the function of rights: the will theory and the interest theory."  (§2.2.2)

> "Will theorists maintain that a right makes the rightholder “a small scale sovereign” (Hart 1982, 183)."  (§2.2.2)

> "In Hohfeldian terms, will theorists assert that every right includes a Hohfeldian power over a claim."  (§2.2.2)

> "Interest theorists maintain that the function of a right is to further the right-holder’s interests."  (§2.2.2)

> "Influential will theorists include Kant, Savigny, Hart, Kelsen, Wellman, and Steiner. Important interest theorists include Bentham, Ihering, Austin, Lyons, MacCormick, Raz, and Kramer."  (§2.2.2)

> "The will theory captures the powerful link between rights and normative control."  (§2.2.2)

> "Within the will theory, there can be no such thing as an unwaivable right: a right over which its holder has no power."  (§2.2.2)

> "Within the will theory, it is impossible for incompetents like infants, animals, and comatose adults to have rights."  (§2.2.2)

> "The interest theory is more capacious than the will theory."  (§2.2.2)

> "However, the interest theory is also misaligned with any ordinary understanding of rights."  (§2.2.2)

> "In Raz’s account, “X has a right if X can have rights, and, other things being equal, an aspect of X’s well-being (his interest) is a sufficient reason for holding some other person(s) to be under a duty” (Raz 1986, 166)."  (§2.2.2)

> "Kramer’s version of the interest theory, which specifies necessary conditions for holding a legal right, is the major contemporary alternative (Kramer 2013, 2017)."  (§2.2.2)

> "The seemingly interminable debate between these two major theories has driven some to conclude that the debate itself rests on the mistaken premise that there is a single concept of a right for which these theories provide rival analyses (Van Duffel 2012b, Hayward 2013)."  (§2.2.2)

> "“Right” in its older, objective sense means “what is just” or “what is fair” (Finnis 1980, 206)."  (§3)

> "Status theories hold that human beings have attributes that make it fitting to ascribe certain rights to them, and make respect for these rights appropriate. Instrumental theories hold that respect for particular rights is a means for bringing about some optimal distribution of advantages."  (§6)

> "They are rather, in Nozick’s phrase, side constraints on the pursuit of good consequences (Nozick 1974, 29)."  (§6)

> "Status theories belong to the tradition of natural rights theories. All natural rights theories fix upon features that humans have by their nature, which make respect for certain rights appropriate."  (§6.1)

> "Natural rights theory reached its high point in the early modern era, in the work of Grotius, Hobbes, Pufendorf, and especially Locke."  (§6.1)

> "Locke argued that men have rights to “life, liberty, and estate” in a pre-political state of nature, and that these natural rights put limits on the legitimate authority of the state."  (§6.1)

> "This is a quiet echo of Bentham’s protest that the doctrine of natural rights “is from beginning to end so much flat assertion: it lays down as a fundamental and inviolable principle whatever is in dispute” (Bentham 1796, 66)."  (§6.1)

> "The most common objection to grounding rights in such a theory is that the resulting rights will be too flimsy."  (§6.2)

> "While status-based rights can appear to be too strong, instrumental rights can appear to be too weak."  (§6.2)

> "The rights-bearing individual is an “isolated monad… withdrawn behind his private interests and whims and separated from the community” (Marx 1844, 146)."  (§7.1)

> "Which theory offers the better account of the functions of rights has been the subject of spirited dispute, literally for ages."  (§2.2.2)

> "Yet it would be odd to say that you have a right against your city council awarding you a pension."  (§2.2.1, example from Hart 1982, 191–92)

> "Mill’s statement is better seen not as an analysis of the everyday concept, but as a stipulative definition of the term “right.”"  (§2.2.1, on Mill 1861, 54)

> "This objective sense of “right” is not the same as our modern idea of “a right.”"  (§3)

> "The theories differ over precisely which attributes of humans give rise to rights, although non-religious theories tend to fix upon the same sorts of attributes described in more or less metaphysical or moralized terms: free will, rationality, autonomy, or the ability to regulate one’s life in accordance with one’s chosen conception of the good life."  (§6.1)

> "To take an example from the scholarly literature, it is not uncommon to encounter a general statement that all rights are (or at least include) claim-rights (see, e.g., Raz 1986, 166, 173–75; Steiner, 1994, 55; Kramer, Steiner and Simmonds 1999, 9–14). These “all rights are claims” statements are better interpreted as stipulative, rather than as analyses of common usage."  (§2.2.1)

> "Why must your Majesty use that word "profit?" What I am provided with, are counsels to benevolence and righteousness, and these are my only topics."  (1A1)

> "The king said, Let it go. I cannot bear its frightened appearance, as if it were an innocent person going to the place of death."  (1A7)

> "Now here is kindness sufficient to reach to animals, and no benefits are extended from it to the people. How is this?"  (1A7)

> "All men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others."  (2A6)

> "even now-a-days, if men suddenly see a child about to fall into a well, they will without exception experience a feeling of alarm and distress. They will feel so, not as a ground on which they may gain the favour of the child's parents, nor as a ground on which they may seek the praise of their neighbours and friends, nor from a dislike to the reputation of having been unmoved by such a thing."  (2A6)

> "The feeling of commiseration is the principle of benevolence. The feeling of shame and dislike is the principle of righteousness. The feeling of modesty and complaisance is the principle of propriety. The feeling of approving and disapproving is the principle of knowledge. Men have these four principles just as they have their four limbs."  (2A6)

> "Let them have their complete development, and they will suffice to love and protect all within the four seas. Let them be denied that development, and they will not suffice for a man to serve his parents with."  (2A6)

> "He who would not so rescue the drowning woman is a wolf. For males and females not to allow their hands to touch in giving and receiving is the general rule; when a sister-in-law is drowning, to rescue her with the hand is a peculiar exigency."  (4A17)

> "The philosopher Gao said"  (6A1, opening words)

> "Man's nature is like the qi-willow , and righteousness is like a cup or a bowl."  (6A1)

> "You must do violence and injury to the willow, before you can make cups and bowls with it."  (6A1)

> "Man's nature is indifferent to good and evil, just as the water is indifferent to the east and west."  (6A2)

> "The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards."  (6A2)

> "Life is what we call nature!"  (6A3)

> "Is the nature of a dog like the nature of an ox, and the nature of an ox like the nature of a man?"  (6A3)

> "Benevolence is internal and not external; righteousness is external and not internal."  (6A4)

> "From the feelings proper to it, it is constituted for the practice of what is good. This is what I mean in saying that the nature is good. If men do what is not good, the blame cannot be imputed to their natural powers."  (6A6)

> "Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and knowledge are not infused into us from without. We are certainly furnished with them. And a different view is simply owing to want of reflection."  (6A6)

> "The trees of the Niu mountain were once beautiful. Being situated, however, in the borders of a large State, they were hewn down with axes and bills - and could they retain their beauty?"  (6A8)

> "but then came the cattle and goats and browsed upon them. To these things is owing the bare and stripped appearance of the mountain, and when people now see it, they think it was never finely wooded. But is this the nature of the mountain?"  (6A8)

> "Therefore, if it receive its proper nourishment, there is nothing which will not grow. If it lose its proper nourishment, there is nothing which will not decay away."  (6A8)

> "In regard to inferior creatures, the superior man is kind to them, but not loving. In regard to people generally, he is loving to them, but not affectionate. He is affectionate to his parents, and lovingly disposed to people generally."  (7A45)

Context: 1A7 is Mencius's interview with the king Legge's text introduces as
  "Xuan of Qi"; the king spared
  the ox and had a sheep substituted. 2A6 continues after the four
  principles with "fire which has begun to burn". In 6A1–4 Gaozi ("the
  philosopher Gao") speaks; 6A6 is the disciple Gong Du reporting Gaozi's
  "neither good nor bad" and two other views. 6A8 ends with a saying
  attributed to Confucius about the mind. 4A17 is a dialogue with Chunyu
  Kun (Legge: "Chun Yu Kun"), who then says the whole kingdom is drowning.

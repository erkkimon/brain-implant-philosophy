> "He has, however, been unable to identify firmly a sixth stage, in longitudinal studies conducted in America and in other cultures, such as Turkey and Israel."  (p. 6)

> "What Heinz did was not wrong. The distribution of scarce drugs should be regulated by principles of fairness. In the absence of such regulations, the druggist was within his legal rights, but in the circumstances he has no moral complaint. While what Heinz did was not wrong, it was not his duty to do it. In this case it was not wrong for Heinz to steal the drug, but it goes beyond the call of duty; it is a deed of supererogation."  (p. 7, "an example from a Stage 5 moral philosopher responding to the Heinz dilemma")

> "Having stated the philosophic claims of Stage 6, we must add that Stage 6 is poorly defined, empirically. All of our own Stage 6 data come from interviews with moral philosophers over 25."  (p. 10)

> "Given the absence of confirming longitudinal data from adulthood, what was once termed Stage 6 is now tentatively identified as an advanced sub-stage of stage 5."  (p. 10)

> "Critics like John Gibbs (1979) question the existence of adult structural stages on the grounds that our Stage 5 is not a Piagetian structure of new operations of moral judgment. Gibbs claims that Stage 5 is merely a philosophic theory."  (p. 11)

> "We think that Stage 5 is a universal "natural" stage of moral development rather than a mere philosophic theory justifying a more sophisticated type of Stage 4 judgment."  (p. 11; OCR "cf" read as "of")

> "The subject makes his decision based on a universal hierarchy of rights. It is this which leads his reasoning to be scored Stage 5, and not his use of a philosophic, social contract theory to justify his judgments." > "It is a stage found in all complex societies studied, including non-Western countries like India, Turkey, Israel and Zambia."  (p. 12, of Stage 5, citing "Blasi and Broughton, 1980")

> "In the Heinz dilemma, for instance, the loving willingness of Heinz to sacrifice himself to save the life of his wife or his friend or acquaintance does not settle the justice choice between the right to life of a dying person and the right to property of the druggist."  (p. 67; OCR "@/" read as "or", "“dying" as "a dying")

Relied on for: two philosophers' answers to the Heinz question and the
stages Kohlberg assigned them; scoring by the reasoning, not the theory
invoked or the answer; the 1981 status of Stage 6; Gibbs's objection as
Kohlberg reports it; Kohlberg's cross-cultural claim for Stage 5.
Context: The paper, presented as drafted for Kohlberg's *Essays* volume
(it refers to "Volume I" and "Chapter 8 of this volume"), asks whether
adult development continues past Stage 5; the ERIC résumé summarises
that "after attainment of Stage 5 post-conventional morality, two paths
of further development are possible. One leads to Stage 6 morality of
rational justice; the other to an exemplary ethic of agape".

## §3 Nicod's criterion (pp. 9–11)

> "We shall refer to this criterion as Nicod’s criterion."  (p. 10)

> "In other words, an object confirms a universal conditional hypothesis if and only if it satisfies both the antecedent (here: ‘ P(x).’) and the consequent (here: ‘ Q(x) ’) of the conditional ; it disconfirms the hypothesis if and only if it satisfies the antecedent, but not the consequent of the conditional ; and (we add this to Nicod’s statement) it is neutral, or irrelevant, with respect to the hypothesis if it does not satisfy the antecedent."  (p. 10)

> "S1: ‘ (x)(Raven(x) ⊃ Black(x))’ ; S2: ‘ (x)(~ Black(x) ⊃ ~ Raven(x)) ’ (i.e. “ All ravens are black’ and ‘“‘ Whatever is not black is not a raven ’’)"  (p. 11)

> "But S1 and S2 are logically equivalent; they have the same content, they are different formulations of the same hypothesis. And yet, by Nicod’s criterion, either of the objects a and d would be confirming for one of the two sentences, but neutral with respect to the other."  (p. 11; a is a black raven, d "neither a raven nor black")

> "This difficulty was pointed out, in substance, in my article “ Le problème de la vérité ’’, Theoria (Göteborg), vol. 3 (1937), esp. p. 222."  (p. 11, n. 2)

## §4 The equivalence condition (p. 12)

> "Equivalence condition : Whatever confirms (disconfirms) one of two equivalent sentences, also confirms (disconfirms) the other."  (p. 12)

## §5 The "paradoxes" of confirmation (pp. 13–15)

> "5. The “Paradoxes” of Confirmation"  (p. 13, section heading; OCR "‘“‘ Paradoxes”’" normalised)

> "Thus, we shall agree that if a is both a raven and black, then a certainly confirms S1: ‘ (x) (Raven(x) ⊃ Black(x))’, and if d is neither black nor a raven, d certainly confirms S2 : ‘ (x) (~ Black(x) ⊃ ~ Raven(x)).’"  (p. 14)

> "Consequently, any red pencil, any green leaf, and yellow cow, etc., becomes confirming evidence for the hypothesis that all ravens are black."  (p. 14)

> "This implies that any non-raven represents confirming evidence for the hypothesis that all ravens are black."  (p. 14)

> "We shall refer to these implications of the equivalence criterion and of the above sufficient condition of confirmation as the paradoxes of confirmation."  (p. 14)

> "Renouncing the equivalence condition would not represent an acceptable solution, as is shown by the considerations presented in section 4."  (p. 14)

> "If this is not so, there seems to be only one alternative left, namely to show that the impression of the paradoxical character of those consequences is due to misunderstanding and can be dispelled, so that no theoretical difficulty remains."  (p. 15)

## §5.11–5.12 Existential import, field of application (pp. 15–18)

> "Thus, the hypothesis that all ravens are black would be expressed by means of the sentence S1: ‘ (x) (Raven(x) ⊃ Black(x)) . (Ex)Raven(x)"  (p. 15; the existential-clause proposal, which Hempel then rejects)

> "Yet this method of avoiding the paradoxes of confirmation is open to serious objections"  (p. 15)

> "This method of meeting the paradoxes would therefore amount to dodging the problem by means of an ad hoc device which, cannot be justified by reference to actual scientific procedure."  (p. 18; on restricting a hypothesis to a "field of application")

## §5.2 Hempel's diagnosis (pp. 18–20)

> "The impression of a paradoxical situation is not objectively founded ; it is a psychological illusion."  (p. 18)

> "Indeed, a hypothesis of the form “ Every P is a Q” forbids the occurrence of any objects having the property P but lacking the property Q"  (p. 18)

> "Suppose that in support of the assertion “ All sodium salts burn yellow ” somebody were to adduce an experiment in which a piece of pure ice was held into a colourless flame and did not turn the flame yellow."  (p. 19)

> "the seemingly paradoxical cases of confirmation, we are often not actually judging the relation of the given evidence, E alone to the hypothesis H (we fail to observe the “ methodological fiction’, characteristic of every case of confirmation, that we have no relevant evidence for H other than that included in E) ; instead, we tacitly introduce a comparison of H with a body of evidence which consists of E in conjunction with an additional amount of information which we happen to have at our disposal"  (p. 20)

> "And now—no matter whether a is ice or some other substance—it is clear that the answer has to be in the affirmative; and the paradoxes vanish."  (p. 20)

> "the “ paradoxes of confirmation’, as formulated above, are due to a misguided intuition in the matter rather than to a logical flaw in the two stipulations from which the “ paradoxes” were derived."  (pp. 20–21)

## Notes (p. 21): Goodman and Hosiasson-Lindenbaum

> "The basic idea of sect. (b) in the above analysis of the ‘‘ paradoxes of confirmation ’’ is due to Dr. Nelson Goodman"  (p. 21, n. 1)

> "To my knowledge, hers has so far been the only publication which presents an explicit attempt to solve the problem. Her solution is based on a theory of degrees of confirmation"  (p. 21, n. 2, on Janina Hosiasson-Lindenbaum, "On Confirmation", J. Symbolic Logic 5, 1940)

> "it consists in the suggestion that the finding of one non-black object which is no raven, while constituting confirming evidence for the hypothesis, would increase the degree of confirmation of the hypothesis by a smaller amount than the finding of one raven which is black. This is said to be so because the class of all ravens is much less numerous than that of all non-black objects"  (p. 21, n. 2)

> "But is this last numerical assumption actually warranted in the present case and analogously in all other “ paradoxical” cases ?"  (p. 21, n. 2)

Relied on for: Hempel's statement of Nicod's criterion, the equivalence
condition, the derivation and name "paradoxes of confirmation", the two
rejected reformulations (existential import; field of application), his
"psychological illusion" diagnosis with the sodium-salt/ice example, and
his report of Hosiasson-Lindenbaum's degree-of-confirmation answer and his
question about its numerical assumption.
Context: the paper seeks "purely formal criteria of confirmation" (p. 9);
§§3–5 criticise Nicod's criterion, introduce the equivalence condition as a
condition of adequacy, derive the paradoxes, reject two reformulations, and
argue the paradoxical impression comes from tacit background knowledge.

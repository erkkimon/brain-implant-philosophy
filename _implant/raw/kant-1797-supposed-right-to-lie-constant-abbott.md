## Abbott's headnote and footnotes (p. 361)

> "[Rosenkranz, vol. vii., p. 295. This Essay was published in a Berlin periodical in 1797.]"  (p. 361, n. 1)

> "In the work called France, for the year 1797, Part VI., No. 1, on Political Reactions, by Benjamin Constant, the following passage occurs, p. 123 :—"  (p. 361)

## Constant, as quoted by Kant (p. 361)

> "The moral principle that it is one's duty to speak the truth, if it were taken singly and unconditionally, would make all society impossible. We have the proof of this in the very direct consequences which have been drawn from this principle by a German philosopher, who goes so far as to affirm that to tell a falsehood to a murderer who asked us whether our friend, of whom he was in pursuit, had not taken refuge in our house, would be a crime."  (p. 361)

> "It is a duty to tell the truth. The notion of duty is inseparable from the notion of right. A duty is what in one being corresponds to the right of another. Where there are no rights there are no duties. To tell the truth then is a duty, but only towards him who has a right to the truth. But no man has a right to a truth that injures others."  (p. 361)

Footnotes on p. 361 (Cramer's note on Constant's text, and Kant's note on Cramer's):

> "J. D. Michaelis, in Göttingen, propounded the same strange opinion even before Kant. That Kant is the philosopher here referred to, I have been informed by the author of this work himself."—K. F. Cramer.  (p. 361, n. 2)

> "I hereby admit that I have really said this in some place which I cannot now recollect.—I. Kant."  (p. 361, n. *)

## Kant's reply

> "It is to be remarked, first, that the expression "to have a right to the truth" is unmeaning."  (p. 361)

> "Now, the first question is whether a man—in cases where he cannot avoid answering Yes or No—has the right to be untruthful. The second question is whether, in order to prevent a misdeed that threatens him or some one else, he is not actually bound to be untruthful in a certain statement to which an unjust compulsion forces him."  (p. 362)

> "Truth in utterances that cannot be avoided is the formal duty of a man to everyone, however great the disadvantage that may arise from it to him or any other; and although by making a false statement I do no wrong to him who unjustly compels me to speak, yet I do wrong to men in general in the most essential point of duty, so that it may be called a lie (though not in the jurist's sense), that is, so far as in me lies I cause that declarations in general find no credit, and hence that all rights founded on contract should lose their force; and this is a wrong which is done to mankind."  (p. 362)

> "For it always injures another; if not another individual, yet mankind generally, since it vitiates the source of justice."  (p. 362)

> "For instance, if you have by a lie hindered a man who is even now planning a murder, you are legally responsible for all the consequences. But if you have strictly adhered to the truth, public justice can find no fault with you, be the unforeseen consequence what it may."  (p. 362)

> "Whoever then tells a lie, however good his intentions may be, must answer for the consequences of it, even before the civil tribunal, and must pay the penalty for them, however unforeseen they may have been; because truthfulness is a duty that must be regarded as the basis of all duties founded on contract, the laws of which would be rendered uncertain and useless if even the least exception to them were admitted."  (p. 363)

> "To be truthful (honest) in all declarations is therefore a sacred unconditional command of reason, and not to be limited by any expediency."  (p. 363)

Kant's footnote to the "formal duty" sentence:

> "I do not wish here to press this principle so far as to say that "falsehood is a violation of duty to oneself." For this principle belongs to Ethics, and here we are speaking only of a duty of justice."  (p. 362, n. 1)

On Constant's "middle principles":

> "In every case in which a principle proved to be true seems to be inapplicable, it is because we do not know the middle principle which contains the medium of its application."  (p. 363, Kant quoting Constant p. 123)

> "Retaining the names of the persons as they have been here brought forward, "the French philosopher" confounds the action by which one does harm (nocet) to another by telling the truth, the admission of which he cannot avoid, with the action by which he does him wrong (lædit)."  (p. 364)

> "for truth is not a possession the right to which can be granted to one, and refused to another"  (p. 364)

> "the duty of veracity (of which alone we are speaking here) makes no distinction between persons towards whom we have this duty, and towards whom we may be free from it; but is an unconditional duty which holds in all circumstances."  (p. 364)

> "and, although by a certain lie I in fact do no wrong to any person, yet I infringe the principle of justice in regard to all indispensably necessary statements generally (I do wrong formally, though not materially)"  (p. 365)

> "All practical principles of justice must contain strict truths, and the principles here called middle principles can only contain the closer definition of their application to actual cases (according to the rules of politics), and never exceptions from them, since exceptions destroy the universality, on account of which alone they bear the name of principles."  (p. 365)

Relied on for: the statement of the case in Kant's and Constant's words; Kant's two questions;
Kant's argument (wrong to mankind, contract, "source of justice"); the legal-responsibility
passage; "duty of justice" footnote; Kant's reply to "right to the truth" and middle principles.
Context: the essay replies to Constant's pamphlet, which Kant cites from the German periodical
*Frankreich im Jahre 1797* (Abbott: "France, for the year 1797"). After the middle-principle
exchange Kant sets out an axiom, postulate and problem of public right (pp. 364–365); the
Appendix continues with a separate piece, "On the saying 'Necessity has no law'" (p. 365).
Abbott's title differs from the Cambridge edition's "On a supposed right to lie from
philanthropy" (Gregor tr., 1996), cited by Mahon (SEP) and Varden 2010.

## The view Cudworth opposes (I.i.5, pp. 9–10)

> "certain it is, that divers Modern Theologers do not only ferioufly, but zealoufly contend in like Manner, That there is nothing Abfolutely, Intrinfecally and Naturally Good and Evil, Juft and Unjuft, antecedently to any pofitive Command or Prohibition of God; but that the Arbitrary Will and Pleafure of God, (that is, an Omnipotent Being devoid of all Effential and Natural Juftice) by its Commands and Prohibitions, is the firft and only Rule and Meafure thereof."  (I.i.5, p. 9)

> "Whence it follows unavoidably, that nothing can be imagined fo grofsly wicked, or fo fouly unjuft or difhoneft, but if it were fuppofed to be commanded by this Omnipotent Deity, muft needs upon that Hypothefis forthwith become Holy, Juft and Righteous."  (I.i.5, pp. 9–10)

> "yet it crept up afterward in the Scholaftick Age, Ockham being among the firft that maintained That there is no Act Evil but as it is prohibited by God, and which cannot be made good if it be commanded by God. And fo on the other hand as to Good. And herein Petrus Alliacus and Andreas de Novo Caftro, with others, quickly followed him."  (I.i.5, p. 10; footnote a gives the Latin: "Nullum actum Malum effe nifi quatenus a Deo prohibitum, et qui non poffit fieri bonus fi a Deo præcipiatur; et e Converfo.")

## Cudworth's argument (I.ii.1, pp. 14–15)

> "cannot poffibly be Arbitrary things, made by Will without Nature; becaufe it is Univerfally true, That things are what they are, not by Will but by Nature."  (I.ii.1, p. 14; the subject is "Moral Good and Evil, Juft and Unjuft, Honeft and Difhoneft", p. 13)

> "Now things may as well be made White or Black by meer Will, without Whitenefs or Blacknefs, Equal and Unequal, without Equality and Inequality, as Morally Good and Evil, Juft and Unjuft, Honeft and Difhoneft, Debita and Illicita, by meer Will, without any Nature of Goodnefs, Juftice, Honefty."  (I.ii.1, p. 15)

> "For though the Will of God be the Supreme Efficient Caufe of all things, and can produce into Being or Exiftence, or reduce into Nothing what it pleafeth, yet it is not the Formal Caufe of any Thing befides itfelf"  (I.ii.1, p. 15)

Relied on for: Cudworth's statement of the voluntarist view and its
consequence; his naming of Ockham (with Pierre d'Ailly and Andreas de Novo
Castro) as early holders; his argument that moral natures cannot be made
by will, by analogy with whiteness, triangularity and equality.
Context: Chapter i opens with ancient "pretended Philosophers" who held
justice to be by law and not by nature (Plato, Laws X; Theaetetus), then
Hobbes (§§3–4), then the "Modern Theologers" (§5). Cudworth names only
Joannes Szydlovius among "late Authors" (p. 12). Cudworth does not mention
the Euthyphro in these pages. Murphy (SEP 2024, §3.2) points to Schroeder
2005 for discussion of "a similar objection raised against theological
voluntarism by Ralph Cudworth (1731)".

> "The most common form of ascriptivist theory holds that nations as such have a right of self-determination that includes the right to secede in order to have their own state."  (§2)

> "Plebiscitary theorists ground the right to secede in the right to self-determination (Wellman 2005: ch. 3)."  (§2.2)

> "For them, self-determination is in itself of such great value that it can ground a right to complete political autonomy given certain conditions."  (§2.2)

> "According to the prevalent understanding of self-determination in mainstream political philosophy and international law, however, the right to self-determination includes only two features: freedom from external interference in self-governance and some minimally democratic form of government such that each member of the polity enjoys equal political participation rights."  (§2.2)

> "Some critics have objected that the remedial right only approach to unilateral secession is disturbingly irrelevant to the concerns of many groups seeking self-determination."  (§2.1)

> "The point is not simply that the ascriptivist view is unfeasible; its support for the idea of the ethnically exclusive state is an incitement to ethnic cleansing if not genocide."  (§2.1)

Relied on for: the ascriptivist (national) and plebiscitary groundings of a
  right to secede in self-determination; the "prevalent understanding" of the
  right to self-determination in political philosophy and international law
  as the authors report it; the critics' objection to remedial-right-only
  theories and the authors' objection to ascriptivist theories.
Context: §2 divides theories into remedial right only theories (a right to
  secede only as a remedy for injustice) and primary right theories
  (ascriptivist and plebiscitary). The objection to ascriptivism is the
  authors' own; §2.1 answers the critics on behalf of remedial right only
  theories; §2.2 names Christopher Wellman as "The most prominent proponent
  of the plebiscitary view".

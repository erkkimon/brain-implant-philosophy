# Locke, Essay IV.xvii.19–22 — the four sorts of arguments

Source: John Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), Book
  IV, chapter xvii ("Of Reason"), §§19–22.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10616  (Project Gutenberg
  transcription, volume 2; public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25

> "The first is, to allege the opinions of men, whose parts, learning,
>  eminency, power, or some other cause has gained a name, and settled their
>  reputation in the common esteem with some kind of authority. When men are
>  established in any kind of dignity, it is thought a breach of modesty for
>  others to derogate any way from it, and question the authority of men who
>  are in possession of it. […] This I think may be called ARGUMENTUM AD
>  VERECUNDIAM."  (IV.xvii.19)

> "The fourth is the using of proofs drawn from any of the foundations of
>  knowledge or probability. This I call ARGUMENTUM AD JUSTICIUM. This alone,
>  of all the four, brings true instruction with it, and advances us in our
>  way to knowledge. For, 1. It argues not another man's opinion to be right,
>  because I, out of respect, or any other consideration but that of
>  conviction, will not contradict him."  (IV.xvii.22)

Relied on for: the textual claim that Locke coined *argumentum ad
verecundiam* for the appeal to established reputation, that he presents it
as one of four ways men "prevail on their assent", and that he contrasts it
with *ad judicium* (spelled "ad justicium" in the Gutenberg transcription)
as the one that "advances us in our way to knowledge". Note that the section
does not use the word "fallacy".
Context: §§20–21 give *ad ignorantiam* and *ad hominem*; the passage sits at
the end of the chapter on reason, before the discussion of things above,
contrary and according to reason.

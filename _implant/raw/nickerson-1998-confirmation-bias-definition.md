# Nickerson 1998 — the definition of confirmation bias, and the reports it relies on

Source: Raymond S. Nickerson (1998), "Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous
  Phenomenon in Many Guises", Review of General Psychology 2(2), 175–220.
Original: https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (DOI verified by content negotiation; text read in
  an open teaching copy at pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/nickersonConfirmationBias.pdf;
  page numbers are the journal's)

> "Confirmation bias, as the term is typically used in the psychological
>  literature, connotes the seeking or interpreting of evidence in ways that
>  are partial to existing beliefs, expectations, or a hypothesis in hand."
>  (p. 175, abstract)

> "As the term is used in this article and, I believe, generally by
>  psychologists, confirmation bias connotes a less explicit, less
>  consciously one-sided case-building process. It refers usually to
>  unwitting selectivity in the acquisition and use of evidence."  (p. 175)

> "Many have written about this bias, and it appears to be sufficiently
>  strong and pervasive that one is led to wonder whether the bias, by
>  itself, might account for a significant fraction of the disputes,
>  altercations, and misunderstandings that occur among individuals,
>  groups, and nations."  (p. 175)

> "This does not necessarily mean completely ignoring the counterindicative
>  information but means being less receptive to it than to supportive
>  information—more likely, for example, to seek to discredit it or to
>  explain it away."  (p. 178)

On Wason's task and Klayman & Ha's reinterpretation:

> "Given the triplet 2-4-6, for example, people were likely to come up with
>  the hypothesis successive even numbers and then proceed to test this
>  hypothesis by generating additional sets of successive even numbers."
>  (p. 179)

> "Klayman and Ha (1987) have suggested that many phenomena of human
>  hypothesis testing can be accounted for by the assumption of a general
>  positive-test strategy."  (p. 200)

On biased assimilation (Nickerson's report of Lord, Ross & Lepper 1979):

> "…two people with initially conflicting views can examine the same
>  evidence and both find reasons for increasing the strength of their
>  existing opinions (Lord, Ross, & Lepper, 1979)."  (p. 187)

On debiasing:

> "They could be encouraged to attempt to identify reasons why the
>  complement of the hypothesis in hand might be true. Again, the evidence
>  provides reason for optimism that the approach can work (C. A. Anderson,
>  1982; C. A. Anderson & Sechler, 1986; Lord, Lepper, & Preston, 1984)."
>  (p. 211)

On the adaptive reading:

> "An idea common to several of these explanations is that people's
>  performance of the task is indicative of behavior that has proved to be
>  adaptively effective in analogous real-world situations."  (p. 186)

Relied on for: the descriptive definition of the term; the distinction
from deliberate case-building; the 2-4-6 report; the report of biased
assimilation; the consider-the-opposite debiasing report; the existence of
adaptive interpretations.
Context: a 46-page review. The definition sentence opens the abstract; the
"unwitting selectivity" sentence follows a paragraph distinguishing
deliberate advocacy from the bias. The Wason report is in the section on
"Restriction of attention to a favored hypothesis"; p. 186 discusses the
Wason selection task (a different task) and Cosmides's social-contract
account of it. The review's own conclusion is not excerpted.

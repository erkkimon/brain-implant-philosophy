> "The purpose of this paper is to present an impossibility result that seems to have some disturbing consequences for principles of social choice."  (§I, p. 152)

> "A common objection to the method of majority decision is that it is illiberal."  (§I, p. 152)

> "Similarly, whether you should sleep on your back or on your belly is a matter in which the society should permit you absolute freedom, even if a majority of the community is nosey enough to feel that you must sleep on your back."  (§I, p. 152)

> "Arrow used a weak version of the Pareto principle."  (§II, p. 153)

> Condition P: "If every individual prefers any alternative x to another alternative y, then society must prefer x to y."  (§II, p. 153)

> Condition L (Liberalism): "For each individual i, there is at least one pair of alternatives, say (x, y), such that if this individual prefers x to y, then society should prefer x to y, and if this individual prefers y to x, then society should prefer y to x."  (§II, p. 153)

> "The intention is to permit each individual the freedom to determine at least one social choice, for example, having his own walls pink rather than white, other things remaining the same for him and the rest of the society."  (§II, p. 153)

> Theorem I: "There is no social decision function that can simultaneously satisfy Conditions U, P, and L."  (§II, p. 153)

> n. 1: "The term “ liberalism'' is elusive and is open to alternative interpretations."  (p. 153)

> n. 1: "I do not wish to engage in a debate on the right use of the term."  (p. 153)

> "Hence, we demand such freedom for at least two individuals."  (§II, p. 154; preceded by "if it includes only one then we might have a dictatorship")

> Condition L* (Minimal Liberalism): "There are at least two individuals such that for each of them there is at least one pair of alternatives over which he is decisive"  (§II, p. 154)

> Theorem II: "The following theorem is stronger than Theorem I and subsumes it."  (§II, p. 154)

> Proof, distinct-pairs case: "But by Condition L* society should prefer x to y and z to w, while by the Pareto principle society must prefer w to x, and y to z."  (§II, p. 154)

> §III: "There is one copy of a certain book, say Lady Chatterly's Lover, which is viewed differently by 1 and 2."  (p. 155; spelling as printed)

> "The three alternatives are: that individual 1 reads it (x), that individual 2 reads it (y), and that no one reads it (z)."  (p. 155)

> "In decreasing order of preference, his ranking is z, x, y."  (p. 155; of person 1, "who is a prude")

> "His ranking is, therefore, x, y, z."  (p. 155; of person 2)

> "Thus, the society should prefer z to x."  (p. 155)

> "Hence y should be judged socially better than z."  (p. 155)

> "That is, the society should prefer y to z, and z to x."  (p. 155)

> "the view of both persons, i.e., x is Pareto superior to y."  (p. 155)

> "Every solution that we can think of is bettered by some other solution, given the Pareto principle and the principle of liberalism, and we seem to have an inconsistency of choice."  (p. 155)

> §IV: "It is, of course, not necessarily disturbing for every conceivable society, since the conflict arises with only particular configurations of individual preferences."  (p. 155)

> "The ultimate guarantee for individual liberty may rest not on rules for social choice but on developing individual values that respect each other's"  (pp. 155–156; continues "personal choices.")

> "However, unlike in the theorem of Arrow, we have not required transitivity of social preference."  (p. 156; printed "transi-tivity" across a line break)

> "This way out is not open here, for the theorem holds without imposing this condition."  (p. 156; of independence of irrelevant alternatives)

> "What is the moral? It is that in a very basic sense liberal values conflict with the Pareto principle."  (§IV, p. 157)

> "Or, to look at it in another way, if someone does have certain liberal values, then he may have to eschew his adherence to Pareto optimality."  (p. 157)

> "While the Pareto criterion has been thought to be an expression of individual liberty, it appears that in choices involving more than two alternatives it can have consequences that are, in fact, deeply illiberal."  (p. 157)

> n. 4: "It may appear that one way of solving the dilemma is to dispense with the social choice function based on a binary relation, that is, to relax not merely transitivity but also acyclicity."  (p. 156; Sen goes on that P and L would then have to be redefined and "the choice set ... may be rendered empty even without bringing in acyclicity")

> n. 5: "Gibbard's Theorem is disturbing, for the conditions look appealing but the resultant oligarchy seems revolting, and it is a major extension of the problem posed by Arrow (1951, 1963)."  (p. 156; a then-unpublished theorem of A. Gibbard using independence of irrelevant alternatives)

Relied on for: the conditions U, P, L, L*; Theorems I and II; the Lady
  Chatterley rankings and the resulting cycle; Sen's own statements of
  scope (§IV) and of the moral; n. 4 on non-binary choice; n. 5 on Gibbard.
Context: §I motivates via majority rule and private matters (walls, sleeping
  position); §II defines collective choice rules and social decision
  functions (choice-generating, requiring only a best element) and proves
  Theorem II in two cases (pairs sharing one alternative; four distinct
  alternatives); §III is the two-person, three-alternative book example;
  §IV notes that transitivity and independence are not required, and that
  the Pareto principle is used in its weak form.

# Hart 1993 — The Barth–Brunner debate revisited: context of Nein!, point of contact, Hart's conclusion

> "The purpose of this paper is to consider again the very public disagreement between Karl Barth and Emil Brunner which reached its zenith in 1934"  (p. 289)

> "Christian theology, Christian ethics, therefore, could in no sense be held to derive their message from two distinct sources: revelation on the one hand, and nature or reason on the other."  (p. 290, Hart summarising Barth's conviction)

> "Gratia non tollit naturam sed perfecit: 'Grace does not destroy nature, but perfects it'."  (p. 291, the answer Barth "saw Roman Catholicism and Protestant liberalism alike as having given")

> "It is important, when reading the response of Barth to Brunner in 1934 always to keep this context firmly in mind: otherwise the passion and invective may seem to be getting rather too worked up about an abstract issue of merely academic interest."  (p. 292)

> "Brunner's insistent differentiation between a 'formal' and 'material' image of God in humans, and his affirmation of the need for a 'point of contact' for grace in human nature as created and fallen."  (summary)

> "'If we are prepared to call the fact that man is man and not a cat the 'point of contact', 'the objective possibility of divine revelation', then all objection to these concepts is nonsensical. For this truth is incontrovertible' (p. 88)."  (p. 300, Hart quoting Barth, Natural Theology p. 88)

> "My concluding contention would be that Barth, notwithstanding his strong denials, cannot, as long as he adheres to a doctrine of incarnation, or to the belief that God has revealed himself to humans, avoid positing a point of contact in this second, carefully qualified, sense."  (p. 305)

Relied on for: the dates and texts of the exchange (Brunner, "Nature and Grace"; Barth, "Nein!", both 1934, Eng. 1946); Hart's account of the context; Barth on the "point of contact" as Hart quotes him; Hart's criticism of Barth (attributed).
Context: Hart's first section stresses the 1934 setting (Barmen, the "German Christians"); the middle sets out Brunner's formal/material distinction; the end argues an ambiguity in "capacity for revelation" lets one read Brunner's point in a way Barth "cannot avoid conceding".

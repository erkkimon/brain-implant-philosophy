(a) List entry:

> "Knower paradox: "This sentence is not known.""  (List of paradoxes, "Logic", self-reference subsection)

(b) Article:

> "Informally, it consists in considering a sentence saying of itself that it is not known, and apparently deriving the contradiction that such sentence is both not known and known."  (lead)

> "A version of the paradox occurs already in chapter 9 of Thomas Bradwardine’s Insolubilia."  (History; cites Bradwardine, Insolubilia, ed. and tr. Stephen Read, Peeters, Leuven, 2010)

> "(KF): If the sentence ' P ' is known, then P"  (Formulation)

> "(PK): If the sentence ' P ' has been proved, then ' P ' is known"  (Formulation)

> "(K): (K) is not known"  (Formulation)

> "absurdity can only be avoided either by rejecting one of the two principles of knowledge (KF) and (PK) or by rejecting classical logic (which validates the reasoning from (KF) and (PK) to absurdity)."  (Solutions)

> "One approach takes its inspiration from the hierarchy of truth predicates familiar from Alfred Tarski's work on the Liar paradox and constructs a similar hierarchy of knowledge predicates."  (Solutions; footnote: Anderson 1983, J. Phil. 80, 338–355)

> "Another approach upholds a single knowledge predicate but takes the paradox to call into doubt either the unrestricted validity of (PK) or at least knowledge of (KF)."  (Solutions; footnotes: Maitzen 1998, Synthese 114, 337–354, for (PK); Cross 2001, Mind 110, 319–333, for knowledge of (KF))

> "One approach rejects the law of excluded middle and consequently reductio ad absurdum."  (Solutions; footnote: Morgenstern 1986, in Halpern (ed.), Theoretical Aspects of Reasoning about Knowledge, pp. 99–114)

> "Another approach upholds reductio ad absurdum and thus accepts the conclusion that (K) is both not known and known, thereby rejecting the law of non-contradiction."  (Solutions; footnote: Priest 1991, Notre Dame J. Formal Logic 32, 193–211)

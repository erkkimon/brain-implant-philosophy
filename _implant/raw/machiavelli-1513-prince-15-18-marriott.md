# Machiavelli 1513 — The Prince ch. XV and XVIII: learning "how to do wrong", keeping faith, the fox and the lion

Source: Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, trans. W. K. Marriott, chapters XV
  ("Concerning Things for which Men, and Especially Princes, Are Praised or
  Blamed") and XVIII ("Concerning the Way in which Princes Should Keep
  Faith"). Date 1513 as in Coady's SEP bibliography ("Machiavelli, Niccolo,
  1513, The Prince").
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1232/pg1232.txt
  (Project Gutenberg eBook #1232; Marriott translation; public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain-text Gutenberg file read)

> "for many have pictured republics and principalities which in fact have never been known or seen, because how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation"  (ch. XV)

> "Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity."  (ch. XV)

> "for if everything is considered carefully, it will be found that something which looks like virtue, if followed, would be his ruin; whilst something else, which looks like vice, yet followed brings him security and prosperity."  (ch. XV, last sentence)

> "Therefore a wise lord cannot, nor ought he to, keep faith when such observance may be turned against him, and when the reasons that caused him to pledge it exist no longer. If men were entirely good this precept would not hold, but because they are bad, and will not keep faith with you, you too are not bound to observe it with them."  (ch. XVIII)

> "Therefore it is unnecessary for a prince to have all the good qualities I have enumerated, but it is very necessary to appear to have them."  (ch. XVIII)

> "to appear merciful, faithful, humane, religious, upright, and to be so, but with a mind so framed that should you require not to be so, you may be able and know how to change to the opposite."  (ch. XVIII)

Relied on for: Machiavelli's own wording of the claim Coady (SEP §2) renders
from the Bondanella translation as the Prince "must learn how not to be
good"; the ch. XVIII passage on breaking faith and on appearing virtuous.
Context: ch. XV opens by saying Machiavelli will "depart from the methods of
other people" and "follow up the real truth of the matter than the
imagination of it"; the list of praised and blamed qualities follows. In
ch. XVIII the fox-and-lion image precedes the faith passage, and Marriott's
note 1 quotes Burd: "The present chapter has given greater offence than any
other portion of Machiavelli’s writings." Marriott renders the phrase "know
how to do wrong"; Coady's "how not to be good" is from a different
translation (Bondanella, Oxford 1984, p. 52), which was not read.

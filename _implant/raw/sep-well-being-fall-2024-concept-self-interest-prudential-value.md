# SEP Fall 2024 — Crisp, "Well-Being" §1 and preamble: well-being as what is good for a person, "self-interest" as a correlate term, prudential value

Source: Roger Crisp, "Well-Being", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published Tue Nov 6, 2001; substantive revision Wed Sep 15, 2021.
  Preamble, §1 "The Concept".
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/well-being/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-15 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page fetched with
  curl, tags stripped; quotations matched by script)

> "Well-being is most commonly used in philosophy to describe what is non-instrumentally or ultimately good for a person."  (preamble)

> "It has become standard to distinguish theories of well-being as either hedonist theories, desire theories, or objective list theories."  (preamble)

> "One correlate term worth noting here is ‘self-interest’: my self-interest is what is in the interest of myself, and not others."  (§1)

> "Well-being is a kind of value, sometimes called ‘prudential value’, to be distinguished from, for example, aesthetic value or moral value."  (§1)

> "But it remains an open question whether my being morally good is good for me; and, if it is, its being good for me is still conceptually distinct from its being morally good."  (§1)

Relied on for: the definition of self-interest as a correlate of
well-being; prudential vs moral value; the threefold classification of

# Schopenhauer 1818 — The World as Will and Idea, vol. I: will, pessimism, sympathy, denial of the will (Haldane & Kemp tr.)

Source: Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea* (Die Welt als
  Wille und Vorstellung), vol. I, translated by R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp,
  7th edition, London: Kegan Paul, Trench, Trübner & Co., 1909. Locators
  are Schopenhauer's section numbers (§).
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38427 (Project Gutenberg eBook
  #38427; public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (Gutenberg plain text fetched with curl and grepped;
  line breaks joined)

> "§ 1. “The world is my idea:”—this is a truth which holds good for everything that lives and knows, though man alone can bring it into reflective and abstract consciousness."  (§1)

> "This truth, which must be very serious and impressive if not awful to every one, is that a man can also say and must say, “the world is my will.”"  (§1)

> "the access to which, opened to us through the Upanishads, is in my eyes the greatest advantage which this still young century enjoys over previous ones,"  (Preface to the First Edition; "which" is the Vedas)

> "Thus its life swings like a pendulum backwards and forwards between pain and ennui."  (§57)

> "It is this: “All love (αγαπη, _caritas_) is sympathy.”"  (§66)

> "by the formula of the Vedas already quoted: “Tat twam asi!” (This thou art!) Whoever is able to say this to himself, with regard to every being with whom he comes in contact, with clear knowledge and firm inward conviction, is certain of all virtue and blessedness, and is on the direct road to salvation."  (§66)

> "it is actual renunciation, denial of the will to live,—asceticism, of which we shall speak last."  (§66)

> "Rather do we freely acknowledge that what remains after the entire abolition of will is for all those who are still full of will certainly nothing; but, conversely, to those in whom the will has turned and has denied itself, this our world, which is so real, with all its suns and milky-ways—is nothing."  (§71, last sentence of vol. I)

Relied on for: the opening theses (world as idea, world as will); his
  own statement on the Upanishads; the pain/ennui pendulum (pessimism);
  love as sympathy and "Tat twam asi" in the ethics of Book IV; denial of
  the will to live; the closing "nothing" of vol. I.
Context: Haldane & Kemp render Vorstellung as "idea" and Mitleid as
  "sympathy"; Wicks (SEP) uses "representation" and "compassion". §57
  begins "At every grade that is enlightened by knowledge, the will
  appears as an individual". Note (92) to §71 adds "This is also just the
  Prajna—Paramita of the Buddhists". In the same §71 sentence's lead-in he
  says we must not evade the nothingness "like the Indians, through myths
  and meaningless words, such as reabsorption in Brahma or the Nirvana of
  the Buddhists".

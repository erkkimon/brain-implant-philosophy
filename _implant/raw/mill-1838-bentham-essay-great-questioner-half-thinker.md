# Mill 1838 — "Bentham" (London and Westminster Review): "the great questioner of things established", "a great reformer in philosophy", "one-eyed men", the missing conscience

Source: John Stuart Mill, "Bentham", London and Westminster Review, August
  1838; reprinted in Mill, Dissertations and Discussions: Political,
  Philosophical, and Historical, vol. I (New York: Henry Holt and Company,
  1882), pp. 355–417 (the essay begins p. 355 per the volume's contents
  "BENTHAM 355"; page numbers below from the OCR running heads).

> "Bentham has been in this age and country the great questioner of things established."  (p. 357)

> "Bentham: he is the great subversive, or, in the language of Continental philosophers, the great critical, thinker of his age and country."  (p. 359; preceded by "The father of English innovation, both in doctrines and in institutions, is")

> "he was not a great philosopher; but he was a great reformer in philosophy."  (p. 364)

> "It was not his doctrines which did this: it was his mode of arriving at them."  (p. 364)

> "For our own part, we have a large tolerance for one-eyed men, provided their one eye is a penetrating one"  (p. 382)

> "Almost all rich veins of original and striking speculation have been opened by systematic half-thinkers"  (p. 382)

> "Man is never recognized by him as a being capable of pursuing spiritual perfection as an end; of desiring, for its own sake, the conformity of his own character to his standard of excellence, without hope of good, or fear of evil, from other source than his own inward consciousness."  (p. 384)

> "Nothing is more curious than the absence of recognition, in any of his writings, of the existence of conscience, as a thing distinct from philanthropy, from affection for God or man, and from self-interest in this world or in the next."  (p. 384)

Relied on for: thinkers/bentham.md, Reception — Mill's 1838 assessment,
  both its praise (method, critical spirit) and its criticism (an
  incomplete view of human nature: no self-culture, no conscience).
Context: the essay opens by pairing two "recently deceased" men "to whom
  their country is indebted"; the volume's preface refers to "the
  subsequent essays on Bentham and on Coleridge". Between the praise and
  the criticism Mill writes that "To reject his half of the truth because
  he overlooked the other half would be to fall into his error without
  having his excuse." Page numbers were read from the OCR running heads (even pages
  print the number before "BENTHAM.", odd pages after).

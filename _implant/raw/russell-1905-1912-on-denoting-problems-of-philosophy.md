# Russell 1905 and 1912 — "On Denoting" (the principle and the puzzles) and The Problems of Philosophy (acquaintance, the chicken, the value of philosophy)

## A. "On Denoting" (1905)

Source: Bertrand Russell, "On Denoting", Mind 14 (n.s.), issue 4, 1905,
  pp. 479–493; text as reprinted in Logic and Knowledge (ed. R. C.
  Marsh, 1956), transcribed on Wikisource; no page numbers in the
  transcription.
Original: https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XIV.4.479 (verified by DOI content
  negotiation: "II.—ON DENOTING", Mind vol. XIV, issue 4, pp. 479–493,
  1905, Oxford University Press); transcription
  https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Denoting
  (published 1905; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (Wikisource raw wikitext fetched with curl; wiki
  italics markup removed from the quotations)

> "A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'."  (numbered remark (1), opening paragraph)

> "This is the principle of the theory of denoting I wish to advocate: that denoting phrases never have any meaning in themselves, but that every proposition in whose verbal expression they occur has a meaning."  (statement of the theory)

> "A logical theory may be tested by its capacity for dealing with puzzles, and it is a wholesome plan, in thinking about logic, to stock the mind with as many puzzles as possible, since these serve much the same purpose as is served by experiments in physical science."  (introduction of the three puzzles)

> "Hegelians, who love a synthesis, will probably conclude that he wears a wig."  (puzzle (2), the present King of France)

Relied on for: Russell's own statement of the theory of descriptions
("denoting phrases never have any meaning in themselves") and of the test
by puzzles; the present King of France example.
Context: the article defines denoting phrases, gives the theory, then three
puzzles (George IV and the author of Waverley; the present King of France
and excluded middle; the difference of A and B), solves them, and criticises
Meinong and Frege.

## B. The Problems of Philosophy (1912)

Source: Bertrand Russell, The Problems of Philosophy, London: Williams and
  Norgate / Home University Library, 1912; chapters V, VI, XV.
Original: Project Gutenberg eBook #5827, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5827
  (public domain in the US; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-08 (plain text fetched with curl; underscores mark
  italics in the Gutenberg text)

> "We shall say that we have _acquaintance_ with anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths."  (ch. V, "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description")

> "The man who has fed the chicken every day throughout its life at last wrings its neck instead, showing that more refined views as to the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken."  (ch. VI, "On Induction")

> "Philosophy is to be studied, not for the sake of any definite answers to its questions, since no definite answers can, as a rule, be known to be true, but rather for the sake of the questions themselves;"  (ch. XV, "The Value of Philosophy", closing paragraph)

Relied on for: acquaintance (ch. V), the chicken example on induction
(ch. VI), and Russell's statement of why philosophy is studied (ch. XV).
Context: the Preface says the book is confined "in the main to those
problems of philosophy in regard to which I thought it possible to say
something positive and constructive"; the chicken passage illustrates that
expectations of uniformity "are liable to be misleading".

> "But consequentialism says you must keep giving as long as the benefit to them is greater than the cost to you, which can be until you’ve reduced yourself to their welfare level (Singer 1972: 241)."  (§1)

> "A different version appeals to intuitions about particular cases (e.g., Singer 1972; Unger 1996)."  (§2)

> "most famously one of Peter Singer’s where you can save a child from drowning in a pond at the cost of dirtying your clothes (1972: 231)."  (§2)

> "It then argues that cases where many deny there’s a duty to benefit, such as ones where you can save a life by contributing to famine relief, don’t differ in any morally significant way from this one and so likewise involve a requirement; there too you must sacrifice your good."  (§2)

> "As Singer recognizes, a defence starting from his pond case can’t support a duty as strong as the fully maximizing one he himself prefers."  (§2)

> "But the defence can, if successful, support a stronger duty of aid than everyday morality recognizes, one that makes substantial contributions to charity not just generous or supererogatory but in the strict sense required."  (§2)

> "The child in the pond is just a short distance from you while those suffering from famine are far away; you know who in particular will benefit if you save the child—you can see him—but you know only that someone or other will be saved if you contribute to famine relief; and only you can save the child whereas many others can contribute to charity."  (§2)

> "Those who defend a strong duty of aid deny that these differences, either individually or together, are morally significant (e.g., Singer 1972; Unger 1996; Kagan 1998: 134–35; Pummer 2023: 99–125); thus, they deny that this duty is weakened by physical distance (contrast Kamm 2000)."  (§2)

> "Others argue that one or more of the differences do weaken the duty (e.g., Woollard 2015: 129–43)."  (§2)

> "More specifically, they argue that the pond case invokes a duty of immediate or emergency rescue that’s distinct from, and stronger than, any general duty of beneficence (Kagan 1998; Igneski 2006)."  (§2)

> "Imagine that you encounter a drowning child while on your way to make a charitable donation that will save two lives and must be made without delay."  (§2)

> "But many think that would be wrong; your stronger duty is to save the child, which implies that it’s a distinct duty (e.g., Woollard 2015: 132, 142)."  (§2)

> "But then the verdict about the original pond case doesn’t extend, at least without some loss of demandingness, to non-rescue cases like ones of charitable giving."  (§2)

> "Arguments for a strong duty to promote the good, whether maximizing or somewhat weaker, are revisionist of everyday morality."  (§2)

> "It’s as if you encountered a long series of ponds, each requiring you to abandon what you were doing to save a child and leaving you no time for yourself (Kuper 2002; Timmerman 2015; Woollard 2015: 126; J. A. Thomson 2022)."  (§6)

> "Then the more you’ve sacrificed or will sacrifice through time, the larger the difference between the benefits and costs of a further contribution must be for you to be required to make it now (Pummer 2023: 138–44)"  (§6)

> "Unlike co-operative views, they require you to spend $20 to save a drowning person even if someone who could split the cost with you declines to."  (§5)

> "Kuper, Andrew, 2002, “More Than Charity: Cosmopolitan Alternatives to the ‘Singer Solution’”, Ethics & International Affairs, 16(1): 107–120."  (Bibliography)

> "In fact, those who urge such a permission may just be trying to justify retaining their privileged social position (Wilson 1993)."  (§2)

> "Richard Mervyn Hare was born at Backwell Down, outside Bristol, on 21 March 1919."  (§1)

> "He was to be known professionally as R.M. Hare, and personally as Dick Hare."  (§1)

> "He was awarded a scholarship to Balliol College in 1937, where he read two years of Greats before the outbreak of war."  (§1)

> "He was finally taken prisoner when Singapore fell in February 1942."  (§1)

> "He was eventually imprisoned with fellow officers in Singapore, whence he was released after exactly three and a half years when the War ended."  (§1)

> "four of whom, Bernard Williams, David Pears, Richard Wollheim, and John Lucas, were to join him both as professional philosophers, and as Fellows of the British Academy."  (§1)

> "It was still during his time there, in 1964, that he was elected a Fellow of the British Academy."  (§1)

> "However, ineluctable promotion eventually removed him, in 1966, to the White’s Chair of Moral Philosophy at Corpus Christi."  (§1)

> "All this made less improbable his early retirement from Oxford in 1983, and his appointment as Graduate Research Professor of Philosophy at the University of Florida at Gainesville."  (§1)

> "When a Society for Applied Philosophy was formed, he became its first President."  (§1)

> "Better known is the last chapter of Freedom and Reason (1963), which addressed the issue, then (it seemed) wholly recalcitrant, of apartheid."  (§1)

> "He died suddenly but peacefully on 29 January 2002."  (§1)

> "his marriage in 1947 to Catherine Verney"  (§1)

> "It was his ambition to have united elements from Aristotle, Kant, and Mill in a logically cogent way that solved the fundamental problems of ethics (though leaving unfinished business), and he usually believed himself to have achieved this."  (preamble)

> "For much of his career, his “prescriptivism” formed an important part of the curriculum, certainly in Britain."  (preamble)

> "and posterity, while unlikely to ratify the logical validity of his theory, will have reason to admire it for its uniting of apparent opposites, freedom and reason, tradition and rationalism, eclecticism and rigor."  (preamble)

> "Philosophically, Hare came under two influences that together led him to views that he could always retain. One was emotivism."  (§2)

> "It became a recurrent theme that emotivism went wrong by connecting the meaning of moral language with its perlocutionary, rather than its illocutionary, force (1997a: 112–14),"  (§2)

> "The other influence was Kantian."  (§2)

> "The Language of Morals introduced a distinction between prescriptive and descriptive meaning."  (§2)

> "Prescriptive meaning is defined in relation to imperatives: a statement is prescriptive if it entails, if necessary in conjunction with purely factual statements, at least one imperative; and to assent to an imperative is to prescribe action."  (§2)

> "Hare never said that ethical statements are imperatives;"  (§2)

> "This at once gave a clear sense to his endorsement of Hume’s denial that one can derive an “ought” from an “is”."  (§2)

> "and this could avoid what is everyone’s first objection to prescriptivism, that, intuitively, “I ought to φ” does not entail “I will φ” (expressing intention)."  (§2)

> "Hare’s attempt to ground a logic of imperatives was innovative, but problematic."  (§3)

> "(Hare discusses the following instance, “Post the letter; so post the letter or burn it”, 1967: 25–34)."  (§3)

> "The objection was made strongly by Peter Geach in an article (1965) citing Gottlob Frege; so it has come to be known as the “Frege-Geach” objection."  (§3)

> "Hare’s response is to be found in Hare (1970). His solution to the problem as it arises with conditionals derives from Gilbert Ryle’s notion of “inference tickets” (1950)."  (§3)

> "The two features of prescriptivity and universalizability remained the twin pillars of Hare’s theory ever afterwards."  (§4)

> "Hare’s clarity on the matter is his most important non-disputable contribution to philosophy."  (§4)

> "The next important development came in a second book, Freedom and Reason (1963), in which the formal features of prescriptivity and universalizability generate a “Golden Rule” form of argument"  (§4)

> "suppose that A owes money to B, who owes money to C, and that the law allows creditors to exact their debts by putting their debtors into prison."  (§4)

> "In Freedom and Reason, Hare allows the argument to be evaded by the “fanatic” who is so committed to some impersonal ideal (say that debtors deserve a hard time) that he is willing to disregard his own personal interests"  (§4)

> "The emergent ethical theory is a distinctive variety of utilitarianism, one that identifies the moral good with the maximization not of some subjective state such as happiness, but of the satisfaction of preferences."  (§4)

> "The argument excited almost as much skepticism as attention. It seemed implausible that the very activity of prescribing universally should commit a speaker to a substantive ethical position, let alone one so distinctive."  (§4)

> "Hence, the speaker can rationally assent to a particular “ought”-statement only if it is derivable from some universal principle that he will accept if he gives impartial and positive weight to all preferences whose satisfaction would be affected by its observance."  (§4)

> "Thus moral reflection generates a universalized prudence."  (§4)

> "Human decision remains free, however rational and informed, because anyone can avoid the constraints of morality by declining to moralize; for this reason, it remains true that no “is” entails an “ought”."  (§4)

> "This is an extraordinary, and extraordinarily bold, intellectual construction, which invites debate at many points."  (§4)

> "Zeno Vendler (1988: 181) urged that we keep apart the semantic thesis (which may be true or nearly true) that “I” is a pure indexical, from any metaphysical claim (which may baffle us) that it denotes a pure subject which can take on any state or role."  (§4)

> "Hare reasons that, if I am glad that I exist, I tenselessly prescribe, ceteris paribus, that my parents bring me into existence; universalizing the prescription, I must prescribe, ceteris paribus, the bringing into existence of others relevantly like me (1975, 1988b, 1988c)."  (§6)

> "A positive answer has implications—though not, Hare argued, very radical ones—for population policy, and the morality of such practices as abortion and IVF."  (§6)

> "is a distinction between a “critical” level of thinking, conducted by “archangels” with the use of “Golden Rule” arguments, and an “intuitive” level, conducted by “proles” with the use of simple principles (often articulating emotional responses) whose acceptance can be justified at the critical level."  (§7)

> "These two levels define not two social castes, but two roles between which each of us learns to alternate as appropriate."  (§7)

> "A different feature of his theory, first presented (in different terminology) in “Ethical Theory and Utilitarianism” (1976), and fully explored in Moral Thinking (1981),"  (§7)

> "as when an American sheriff might judicially execute one suspect in order to prevent a mass lynching of others."  (§7)

> "He could now hope to accommodate these at the “intuitive” level of thinking."  (§7)

> "This objection was to be raised both by Williams (1988) and by J.L. Mackie (1985: 110–11)"  (§7)

> "To say that it is impossible to keep critical and intuitive thinking going in the same thought-process is like saying that in a battle a commander cannot at the same time be thinking of the details of tactics, the overall aim of victory, and the principles (economy of force, concentration of force, offensive action, etc.) which he has learnt when learning his trade."  (§7, quoting Hare 1981: 52)

> "Force still attaches to an objection by Williams (1988: 190):"  (§7)

> "Rather, they constitute a way of seeing the situation; and you cannot combine seeing the situation in that way, from the point of view of those dispositions, with seeing it in the archangel’s way, in which all that is important is maximum preference satisfaction, and the dispositions themselves are merely a means to that."  (§7, quoting Williams 1988: 190)

> "He called himself a “Christian empiricist”, but thought the question whether he was really a Christian terminological."  (§8)

> "Hare pursued this skepticism in two published papers, “Nothing Matters” (1959), and “Ontology in Ethics” (1985)."  (§8)

> "What makes Hare arguably unique, though at the same time closer in approach to Kant than to the utilitarians whose ally he became, was that he combined this insistence upon the ineluctability of individual choice with an optimistic view of the possibilities of making choices rationally."  (§9)

> "(Indeed, he played with the idea that Kant could have been a utilitarian, 1993b.)"  (§9)

> "Though he was keen to claim ancestry (in a late encyclopedia article he counts Socrates and Aristotle as, in part, the first prescriptivists; 1998: 20),"  (§1)

> "His disappointment was not to have persuaded others"  (preamble)

> "John Hare (2002: 307) connects the inhibitions that held his father back from belief not just with modern skepticism, but with “a philosophical doctrine about meaning which he inherited from Carnap and the logical positivists”;"  (§8)

> "a topic already being explored in Scandinavia (especially by Alf Ross), but unfamiliar in Britain."  (§2)

> "However, Hare came to deprecate that phrase (1981: 38), and to emphasize that the “prima facie principles” he prefers to speak of have the power to produce compunction even where their neglect, since well grounded, cannot produce remorse (1981: 30)."  (§7; "that phrase" is “rules of thumb”)

> "This is evidently true of what he had called “rules of thumb”, which an agent applies just so long as this remains the best way in context of achieving his goals."  (§7)

> "The term “universalisability” was to become the title of a slightly later paper (1954/5)"  (§4)

# SEP "Moral Dilemmas" (McConnell) — examples, the concept, the two consistency arguments, responses

Source: Terrance McConnell, "Moral Dilemmas", The Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.);
  first published 2002-04-15, substantive revision 2022-07-25. §§1, 2, 4,
  5, 9.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-dilemmas/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-26 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read)

> "In Book I of Plato’s Republic, Cephalus defines ‘justice’ as speaking
>  the truth and paying one’s debts. Socrates quickly refutes this account
>  by suggesting that it would be wrong to repay certain debts—for example,
>  to return a borrowed weapon to a friend who is not in his right mind."
>  (§1)

> "Sartre (1957) tells of a student whose brother had been killed in the
>  German offensive of 1940. The student wanted to avenge his brother and
>  to fight forces that he regarded as evil. But the student’s mother was
>  living with him, and he was her one consolation in life."  (§1)

> "In Aeschylus’s Agamemnon, the protagonist ought to save his daughter
>  and ought to lead the Greek troops to Troy; he ought to do each but he
>  cannot do both. And Antigone, in Sophocles’s play of the same name,
>  ought to arrange for the burial of her brother, Polyneices, and ought to
>  obey the pronouncements of the city’s ruler, Creon"  (§1)

> "The crucial features of a moral dilemma are these: the agent is
>  required to do each of two (or more) actions; the agent can do each of
>  the actions; but the agent cannot do both (or all) of the actions. The
>  agent thus seems condemned to moral failure; no matter what she does,
>  she will do something wrong (or fail to do something that she ought to
>  do)."  (§2)

> "When one of the conflicting requirements overrides the other, we have a
>  conflict but not a genuine moral dilemma. So in addition to the features
>  mentioned above, in order to have a genuine moral dilemma it must also
>  be true that neither of the conflicting requirements is overridden
>  (Sinnott-Armstrong 1988, Chapter 1)."  (§2)

> "that \(OA\) and \(OB\) are both true is not itself inconsistent, even if
>  one adds that it is not possible for the agent to do both \(A\) and
>  \(B\). And even if the situation is appropriately described as \(OA\)
>  and \(O\neg A\), that is not a contradiction; the contradictory of
>  \(OA\) is \(\neg OA\). (See Marcus 1980 and McConnell 1978, 273.)"  (§4)

> "What is troubling is that theories that allow for dilemmas fail to be
>  uniquely action-guiding."  (§4)

First argument (§4), verbatim labels: principle of deontic consistency
"(PC) OA → ¬O¬A"; "(PD) □(A → B) → (OA → OB)"; premises "1. OA 2. OB
3. ¬C(A & B) [where ‘¬C’ means ‘cannot’]"; McConnell derives "OA and O¬A"
(line 10), which "directly conflicts with PC". Note in §4: "In standard
deontic logic, the ‘□’ in PD typically designates logical necessity. Here
I take it to indicate physical necessity". Also: "Principles OP and D are
basic; they seem to be conceptual truths (Brink 1994, section IV). From
these two principles, one can deduce PC".

Second argument (§4), verbatim:

> "1. OA  2. OB  3. ¬C(A & B)  4. OA → CA (for all A)  5. (OA & OB) →
>  O(A & B) (for all A and all B)  6. O(A & B) → C(A & B) (an instance of
>  4)  7. OA & OB (from 1 and 2)  8. O(A & B) (from 5 and 7)  9. ¬O(A & B)
>  (from 3 and 6)"

> "So if one assumes that ‘ought’ implies ‘can’ and if one assumes the
>  principle represented in (5)—dubbed by some the agglomeration principle
>  (Williams 1965)—then again a contradiction can be derived."  (§4)

> "Opponents of moral dilemmas have generally held that the crucial
>  principles in the two arguments above are conceptually true, and
>  therefore we must deny the possibility of genuine dilemmas. (See, for
>  example, Conee 1982 and Zimmerman 1996.) Most of the debate, from all
>  sides, has focused on the second argument."  (§5)

> "Among the earlier contributors to this debate, some took the existence
>  of dilemmas as a counterexample to ‘ought’ implies ‘can’ (for example,
>  Lemmon 1962 and Trigg 1971); others, as a refutation of the
>  agglomeration principle (for example, Williams 1965 and van Fraassen
>  1973). A common response to the first argument is to deny PD."  (§5)

> "They are apt to argue that the pertinent, all-things-considered
>  requirement in such a case is disjunctive: Sophie should act to save
>  one or the other of her children, since that is the best that she can
>  do (for example, Zimmerman 1996, Chapter 7)."  (§5)

> "Among supporters of dilemmas, Walter Sinnott-Armstrong
>  (Sinnott-Armstrong 1988, Chapters 4 and 5) has gone to the greatest
>  lengths to provide independent reasons for questioning some of the
>  relevant principles."  (§5)

> "Opponents of dilemmas must show why appearances are deceiving. [...]
>  Supporters must show why several of many apparently plausible
>  principles should be given up—principles such as PC, PD, OP, D,
>  ‘ought’ implies ‘can’, and the agglomeration principle. [...] Much
>  progress has been made, but the debate is apt to continue."  (§9)

Bibliography entries (verbatim): "Marcus, Ruth Barcan, 1980, “Moral
Dilemmas and Consistency,” The Journal of Philosophy, 77: 121–136";
"Sinnott-Armstrong, Walter, 1988, Moral Dilemmas, Oxford: Basil
Blackwell"; "Williams, Bernard, 1965, “Ethical Consistency,” Proceedings of
the Aristotelian Society (Supplement), 39: 103–124".

Relied on for: the definition of a (genuine) moral dilemma; the standard
examples; both consistency arguments and the principles each uses; the map
of responses (deny dilemmas; deny PD; deny ought-implies-can; deny
agglomeration; disjunctive obligation); McConnell's own closing statement
that the debate continues.

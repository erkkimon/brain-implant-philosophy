> "For the Almighty God, who, as even the heathen acknowledge, has supreme power over all things, being Himself supremely good, would never permit the existence of anything evil among His works, if He were not so omnipotent and good that He can bring good even out of evil."  (ch. 11)

> "For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?"  (ch. 11)

> "Just in the same way, what are called vices in the soul are nothing but privations of natural good."  (ch. 11)

## B. Calder, SEP "The Concept of Evil", §2.1

> "The Manichaean solution to the problem of evil is that God is neither all-powerful nor the sole creator of the world."  (§2.1)

> "Since its inception, Manichaean dualism has been criticized for providing little empirical support for its extravagant cosmology."  (§2.1)

> "For these reasons influential, early Christian philosophers such as Saint Augustine, who initially accepted the Manichaean theory of evil, eventually rejected it in favor of the Neoplatonist approach."  (§2.1)

> "The Neoplatonist theory of evil provides a solution to the problem of evil because if evil is a privation of substance, form, and goodness, then God creates no evil."  (§2.1)

> "One problem with the privation theory’s solution to the problem of evil is that it provides only a partial solution to the problem of evil since even if God creates no evil we must still explain why God allows privation evils to exist (See Calder 2007a; Kane 1980)."  (§2.1)

> "Pain is a distinct phenomenological experience which is positively bad and not merely not good."  (§2.1)

Relied on for: Augustine's privation claim in his own words; the reading
of privation as a response to the problem of evil and the two objections
to it, as Calder states them.
Context: Augustine's chapter continues with the example of disease as
absence of health; Calder's §2.1 sets privation theory beside Manichaean
dualism, which he reports as denying that God is all-powerful.

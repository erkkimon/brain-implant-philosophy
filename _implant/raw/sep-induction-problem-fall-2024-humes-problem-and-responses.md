> "The original source of what has become known as the “problem of induction” is in Book 1, part iii, section 6 of A Treatise of Human Nature by David Hume, published in 1739 (Hume 1739)."  (preamble)

> "In 1748, Hume gave a shorter version of the argument in Section iv of An enquiry concerning human understanding (Hume 1748)."  (preamble)

> "Kant famously argued in response to Hume that such synthetic a priori knowledge is possible (Kant 1781, 1783)."  (§3.1)

> "Reichenbach did think Hume’s argument unassailable, but nonetheless he attempted to provide a weaker kind of justification for induction."  (§5.3)

> "Karl Popper, for instance, regarded the problem of induction as insurmountable, but he argued that science is not in fact based on inductive inferences at all (Popper 1935 [1959])."  (§6)

> "Some philosophers have however seen his argument as unassailable, and have thus accepted that it does lead to inductive skepticism, the conclusion that inductive inferences cannot be justified."  (§6)

Relied on for: Henderson's location of the problem's source in T 1.3.6 and
E 4; Kant's synthetic a priori, Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication and
Popper's deductivism as responses she reports.
Context: §3.1 presents Kant's reply as denying premise P3 of her
reconstruction (a demonstrative argument establishes a conclusion whose
negation is a contradiction); §5.3 calls Reichenbach's approach a
"vindication" rather than a justification; §6 opens "Living with Inductive
Skepticism".

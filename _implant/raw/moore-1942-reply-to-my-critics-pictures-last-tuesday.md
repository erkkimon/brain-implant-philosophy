# Moore 1942 — "I went to the pictures last Tuesday, but I don't believe that I did" (reply to Stevenson)

Source: G. E. Moore, "A Reply to My Critics", in P. A. Schilpp (ed.), The
  Philosophy of G. E. Moore (The Library of Living Philosophers, vol. IV),
  Evanston and Chicago: Northwestern University, 1942, pp. 535–677; the
  passages are on pp. 541–543 (in Moore's reply to C. L. Stevenson on the
  ethical sense of "right").
Original: archive.org scan, identifier in.ernet.dli.2015.46303
  (https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.46303), First Edition 1942
  (copyright status not determined here; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (djvu text downloaded; each quotation checked against
  the rendered page images of printed pp. 541, 542 and 543, PDF pages
  562–564; Moore's italics are not reproduced)

> "If, for instance, I assert, on a particular day, that I went to the pictures the preceding Tuesday, I imply, by asserting this, that, at the time of speaking, I believe or know that I did, though I do not say that I believe or know it."  (p. 541)

> "if a person were to assert that it was right of Brutus to stab Caesar, though he would be implying that, at the time of speaking, he approved, or had some similar attitude towards, this action of Brutus’, yet he would not be asserting this"  (p. 541)

> "In the first case, that you do imply this proposition about your present attitude, although it is not implied by (i.e., does not follow from) what you assert, simply arises from the fact, which we all learn by experience, that in the immense majority of cases a man who makes such an assertion as this does believe or know what he asserts: lying, though common enough, is vastly exceptional."  (pp. 542–543)

> "And this is why to say such a thing as “I went to the pictures last Tuesday, but I don’t believe that I did” is a perfectly absurd thing to say, although what is asserted is something which is perfectly possible logically"  (p. 543)

> "But nevertheless your saying that you did, does imply (in another sense) that you believe you did; and this is why “I went, but I don’t believe I did” is an absurd thing to say."  (p. 543)

Relied on for: Moore's first published statement of the omissive sentence
  (the page Sorensen cites as "1942, 543"); Moore's own explanation — a
  non-entailing sense of "imply" grounded in the fact that speakers
  generally believe what they assert; the context in metaethics (the same
  sense of "imply" is used for approval implied by "right").
Context: The passage answers Stevenson's claim that a man who asserts that
  Brutus' action was right is asserting that he approves of it. Moore
  replies that the speaker only "implies" approval, in the same sense in
  which asserting a past fact implies believing it; the pictures sentence is
  his illustration of that sense of "imply". The word "paradox" on p. 542
  refers to Stevenson's view, not to the pictures sentence.

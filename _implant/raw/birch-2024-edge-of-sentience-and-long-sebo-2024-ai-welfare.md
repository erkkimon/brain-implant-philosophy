# Birch 2024 & Long, Sebo et al. 2024 — "sentience candidate", the precautionary framework, and the AI-welfare recommendations

## A. Birch, *The Edge of Sentience*

Source: Jonathan Birch (2024), *The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution
  in Humans, Other Animals, and AI*, Oxford: Oxford University Press
  (published online 19 July 2024; print 15 August 2024); "Summary of the
  Framework and Proposals" (pp. 1–6), ch. 7 (p. 124), ch. 17 "The
  Run-Ahead Principle" (pp. 323–324).
Original: https://doi.org/10.1093/9780191966729.001.0001
  (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, open access; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (DOI verified by content negotiation; full PDF read
  from the OAPEN Library deposit, handle 20.500.12657/93755)

The two definitions:

> "A sentient being (in the sense relevant to the present framework) is a
>  system with the capacity to have valenced experiences, such as
>  experiences of pain and pleasure."  (Summary, p. 1)

> "A system S is a sentience candidate if there is an evidence base that
>  (a) implies a realistic possibility of sentience in S that it would be
>  irresponsible to ignore when making policy decisions that will affect
>  S, and (b) is rich enough to allow the identification of welfare risks
>  and the design and assessment of precautions."  (Summary, p. 1; also
>  ch. 7, p. 124)

The precautionary framing:

> "Framework Principle 2. Sentience candidature can warrant precautions.
>  If S is a sentience candidate, then it is reckless/negligent to make
>  decisions that create risks of suffering for S without considering the
>  question of what precautions are proportionate to those risks.
>  Reasonable disagreement about proportionality is to be expected, but we
>  ought to reach a policy decision rather than leaving the matter
>  unresolved indefinitely."  (Summary, p. 2)

> "the logic of my framework is not the following: 'the precautionary
>  principle' is the correct general decision rule, so we must apply it to
>  this particular set of decision problems. That is not what I'm saying.
>  The idea is rather that the asymmetry of risk that stares us in the
>  face when we think about cases at the edge of sentience presents us
>  with strong and obvious reasons to start thinking about precautions"
>  (ch. 1, p. 17)

On AI specifically:

> "It is why I have been emphasizing the importance of having discussions
>  now, when no artificial sentience candidates yet exist."  (ch. 17,
>  p. 323)

> "Proposal 25. The run-ahead principle. At any given time, measures to
>  regulate the development of sentient AI should run ahead of what would
>  be proportionate to the risks posed by current technology, considering
>  also the risks posed by credible future trajectories."  (Summary, p. 6;
>  ch. 17, p. 324)

## B. Long, Sebo et al., "Taking AI Welfare Seriously"

Source: Robert Long*, Jeff Sebo*, Patrick Butlin, Kathleen Finlinson,
  Kyle Fish, Jacqueline Harding, Jacob Pfau, Toni Sims, Jonathan Birch &
  David Chalmers (2024), "Taking AI Welfare Seriously", arXiv:2411.00986v1
  (4 November 2024), 62 pp.; abstract and §3 "Recommendations".
Original: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (PDF read at https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00986)

> "In this report, we argue that there is a realistic possibility that
>  some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near
>  future. [...] We also recommend three early steps that AI companies and
>  other actors can take: They can (1) acknowledge that AI welfare is an
>  important and difficult issue (and ensure that language model outputs
>  do the same), (2) start assessing AI systems for evidence of
>  consciousness and robust agency, and (3) prepare policies and
>  procedures for treating AI systems with an appropriate level of moral
>  concern."  (Abstract, p. 1)

> "To be clear, our argument in this report is not that AI systems
>  definitely are — or will be — conscious, robustly agentic, or otherwise
>  morally significant. Instead, our argument is that there is substantial
>  uncertainty about these possibilities [...]. Otherwise there is a
>  significant risk that we will mishandle decisions about AI welfare,
>  mistakenly harming AI systems that matter morally and/or mistakenly
>  caring for AI systems that do not."  (Abstract, p. 1)

Relied on for: Birch's definitions of "sentient being" and "sentience
candidate", his statement that no AI system was a sentience candidate at
the time of writing, and the run-ahead principle; the three
acknowledge/assess/prepare recommendations and the explicit
non-assertion of AI consciousness in the Long–Sebo report; the overlap of
authors (Birch, Chalmers, Butlin, Long, Fish) between the 2023
indicator-properties report, this report and Anthropic's later model
welfare programme (Fish is listed as "Eleos AI, Anthropic", with the note
that his work was done "prior to joining Anthropic in Fall 2024").
Context: Birch's book treats humans with disorders of consciousness,
fetuses, organoids, invertebrates and AI under one framework; ch. 16
argues LLMs pose a "gaming problem" for behavioural criteria and ch. 17
proposes "deep computational markers" (Proposal 24) drawn from global
workspace and perceptual-reality-monitoring theories. The Long–Sebo
report's §3.1 also recommends that LLMs "express at least rough degrees
of confidence instead of providing all-or-nothing answers" when asked
about their own consciousness; the body's "marker method" and its "two
routes" (consciousness, robust agency) are not reproduced.

## Death and dates

> "Professor Emerita Judith Jarvis Thomson, a philosopher of morality and metaphysics, died on Nov. 20 at her home in Cambridge, Massachusetts. She was 91."  (¶1)

> "Thomson was a faculty member at MIT for 40 years, retiring in 2004."  (¶2)

> "After receiving a BA in philosophy from Barnard College in New York in 1950, Thomson (then Judith Jarvis) went to Newnham College at Cambridge University on a Fulbright Scholarship."  ("From Cambridge to Cambridge")

> "Thomson was especially impressed by John Wisdom, a follower of Wittgenstein and Thomson’s Cambridge supervisor."  ("From Cambridge to Cambridge")

> "She later came to reject completely Wittgenstein’s view of philosophy, but she retained the idea that philosophers should take careful note of our ordinary ways of speaking and thinking."  ("From Cambridge to Cambridge")

> "a daytime class in the history of philosophy at Columbia University revived her enthusiasm for the subject and she started her PhD program there, graduating in 1959."  ("From Cambridge to Cambridge")

> "Judith Thomson also joined the Institute as an associate professor in 1964 and was promoted to full professor in 1969."  ("From Cambridge to Cambridge")

> "She served as the president of the American Philosophical Association’s Eastern Division in 1992-93, and chair of the APA’s Board of Officers from 2002 to 2005."  ("Service and honors")

> "Her many invited lectures include the Tanner Lectures at Princeton University in 1999 and the APA’s Carus Lectures in 2003."  ("Service and honors")

> "That year a conference was held at MIT to mark the publication of “Normativity,” a book based on her Carus Lectures."  ("Service and honors"; "that year" is 2010)

> "A Festschrift, “Fact and Value: Essays on Ethics and Metaphysics for Judith Jarvis Thomson,” was published in 2001."  ("Service and honors")

## Work, as the obituary describes it

> "Thomson transformed the philosophical discussion of abortion with her 1971 article “ A Defense of Abortion ” and devised the standard form of the “trolley problem.”"  (¶2; spaces inside the quotation marks as on the page)

> "She published six highly regarded books, including “The Realm of Rights” (1990), and “Goodness and Advice” (2001), but what shot her to prominence early on was her 1971 article on abortion, which has been anthologized numerous times and remains a staple of any course in applied ethics."  ("Violinists and trolleys")

> "The philosophical debate about abortion had focused on the question of whether a fetus is a person; Thomson argued that even if it is granted that a fetus is a person, abortion is still morally permissible under some circumstances."  ("Violinists and trolleys")

> "Another landmark essay was “The Trolley Problem,” which appeared in the Yale Law Journal in 1985, a sequel to her 1976 essay “Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem.”"  ("Violinists and trolleys")

> "First, Thomson replaced the trolley driver with a bystander on the side of the track next to a switch"  ("Violinists and trolleys")

> "Second, she contrasted this case with another, where a bystander can bring the trolley to a halt before it kills the five, by pushing a fat man off a footbridge and into the trolley’s path."  ("Violinists and trolleys")

> "Thomson’s examples have generated a vast “trolleyology” literature, spanning disciplines including psychology and neuroscience."  ("Violinists and trolleys")

## Colleagues' assessments (quoted in the obituary)

> "she has had an enormous influence on fellow faculty and on generations of graduate students who now populate the philosophy departments of the English-speaking world."  (Richard Holton, then chair of the MIT Philosophy Section, as quoted)

> "But the idea that Judy might change her mind was completely unexceptional, not noteworthy at all, because she was so intellectually honest and would follow the argument wherever it led."  (Alex Byrne, as quoted in a photo caption)

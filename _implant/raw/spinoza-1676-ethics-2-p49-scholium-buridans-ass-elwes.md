# Spinoza, Ethics (completed 1676) — II P49 scholium: the fourth objection, Buridan's ass

Source: Benedict de Spinoza, Ethics (Ethica; "completed 1676" per Zupko,
  SEP "John Buridan" n. 57), Part II, Proposition 49,
  scholium ("Note"). Translated by R. H. M. Elwes; public-domain
  translation.
Original: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800 (Project Gutenberg
  eBook #3800, Elwes tr.) (public domain)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (plain-text Gutenberg file read)

The proposition:

> "PROP. XLIX.  There is in the mind no volition or affirmation and negation, save that which an idea, inasmuch as it is an idea, involves."  (II P49)

The objection, as Spinoza states it:

> "Fourthly, it may be objected, if man does not act from free will, what will happen if the incentives to action are equally balanced, as in the case of Buridan's ass?  Will he perish of hunger and thirst?  If I say that he would, I shall seem to have in my thoughts an ass or the statue of a man rather than an actual man.  If I say that he would not, he would then determine his own action, and would consequently possess the faculty of going and doing whatever he liked."  (II P49 schol.)

His reply:

> "As for the fourth objection, I am quite ready to admit, that a man placed in the equilibrium described  (namely, as perceiving nothing but hunger and thirst, a certain food and a certain drink, each equally distant from him) would die of hunger and thirst.  If I am asked, whether such an one should not rather be considered an ass than a man; I answer, that I do not know, neither do I know how a man should be considered, who hangs himself, or how we should consider children, fools, madmen, &c."  (II P49 schol.)

Relied on for: Spinoza's statement of the objection as a dilemma (either
he starves and seems no man, or he determines himself and has free will);
his reply that such a man "would die of hunger and thirst"; the naming
"Buridan's ass" in a 1677 text (Zupko, SEP n. 57, cites this scholium as
the association with Buridan).
Context: The scholium defends P49 (will and intellect are one) against
four objections: that the will extends further than the intellect, that
we can suspend judgement, that affirmations do not differ in reality, and
this one. Spinoza's preceding sentence says he will refute "those I have
mentioned" as he is "not bound to put in evidence everything that anyone
may dream".

# Rose 1981 — "Strategy of prevention": population attributable risk, high-risk vs mass strategy, the "prevention paradox"

Source: Geoffrey Rose, "Strategy of prevention: lessons from cardiovascular
  disease", British Medical Journal 282 (6 June 1981), 1847–1851
  ("Occasional Review").
Original: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.282.6279.1847 (PMC1506445; PMID 6786649)
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-09 (DOI verified by CSL-JSON content negotiation: BMJ 282,
  1847–1851, 1981; text read from the PMC scan PDF via a Wayback Machine copy,
  through pdftotext; line breaks of the two-column scan joined below)

> "They add up to 34 extra deaths per 1000 of this population over a 10-year"  (p. 1849; attributable coronary deaths by serum cholesterol, Framingham, fig 3; the sentence continues after the figure: "period, of which only three arise at concentrations at or above 310 mg/100 ml (8 mmol/l)")

> "The rest (90%) arise from the many people in the middle part of the distribution who are exposed to a small risk."  (p. 1849)

> "This illustrates a fundamental principle in the strategy of prevention."  (p. 1849; introduces the next quotation)

> "A large number of people exposed to a low risk is likely to produce more cases than a small number of people exposed to a high risk."  (p. 1849, "Population risk")

> "In business the same principle underlies the mass market: profits are larger when small amounts are taken from the masses than when large amounts are taken from the few rich people; and this principle of the mass market applies to many community health hazards."  (p. 1849)

> "But this "high-risk" strategy, however successful it may be for individuals, cannot influence that large proportion of deaths occurring among the many people with slightly raised blood pressure and a small risk."  (p. 1849)

> "The mass approach is inherently the only ultimate answer to the problem of a mass disease."  (p. 1850, "The individual gains little")

> "A measure applied to many will actually benefit few."  (p. 1850)

> "399 would have worn a seat belt every day for 40 years without benefit to their survival."  (p. 1850; male British doctors, one life saved per about 400 who always wear seat belts)

> "49 out of 50 would eat differently every day for 40 years and perhaps get nothing from it."  (p. 1850; Framingham men of average risk lowering cholesterol by 10% up to age 55)

> "For the same preventive measure in a higher-risk group (those with a little hypertension and a slightly raised cholesterol concentration and smoking cigarettes), the ratio rises to one in 25."  (p. 1850)

> "If a preventive measure exposes many people to a small risk, then the harm it does may readily-as in the case of clofibrate-outweigh the benefits, since these are received by relatively few."  (p. 1850, "Safety is paramount"; the hyphen stands for a dash in the scan)

> "Consequently we cannot accept long-term mass preventive medication."  (p. 1850)

> "a measure that brings large benefits to the community offers little to each participating individual."  (p. 1850, section "The 'prevention paradox'"; introduced by "We arrive at what we might call the prevention paradox")

> "It implies that we should not expect too much from individual health education."  (p. 1850)

> "To influence mass behaviour we must look to its mass determinants, which are largely economic and social."  (p. 1850, "Social motivation")

> "Potentially far more effective, and ultimately the only acceptable answer, is the mass strategy, whose aim is to shift the whole population's distribution of the risk variable."  (p. 1851, "Conclusions")

> "Here, however, our first concern must be that such mass advice is safe."  (p. 1851)

Relied on for: Rose's first naming of the "prevention paradox"; the
  attributable-risk argument; the seat-belt, diphtheria and cholesterol
  ratios as Rose's estimates; the safety caveat; social and economic
  determinants of behaviour.
Context: Rose opens with obstetric and hypertension examples; uses Framingham
  and Whitehall data (figs 2–3, tables III–IV); estimates that a 2–3 mm Hg
  downward shift of the population blood-pressure distribution might equal
  the life-saving benefit of current antihypertensive treatment (p. 1850);
  distinguishes removal of an unnatural factor from addition of a new one
  ("Addition and removal").

## pp. 94–95 — the argument and the paradox

> "Thus, in doing so, we make no one worse off (than he otherwise would be) and hence do nothing wrong."  (p. 94; "doing so" is practising the "laissez faire" policy rather than controlled growth)

> "This argument poses a paradox. It moves by a correct route from plausible premises about biology, personal identity, and moral obliga-tion to a strongly counterintuitive conclusion."  (p. 95)

> "Obligation Principle: One can have an obligation to choose act or policy A rather than alternative B only if it is the case that if one chose B, some particular person would exist and be worse off than if one had chosen A."  (p. 95)

## pp. 96–97 — wronging without harming

The argument for the Obligation Principle (p. 96) runs from "One is
obligated to do A (rather than alternative B) only if it would be wrong not
to do it" through "it is wrong to do A (rather than B) only if someone
would thereby be harmed". Kavka then writes (p. 96, continued p. 97, across
the page break):

> "This argument, however, contains an error. It may sometimes be"  (p. 96, last line)

> "possible to act wrongly by wronging someone without harming him."  (p. 97, first line)

## pp. 100–101 — the slave child

> "In a society in which slavery is legal, a couple that is planning to have no children is offered $50,000 by a slaveholder to produce a child to be a slave to him. They want the money to buy a yacht."  (p. 100)

> "But acting in this manner is outrageous."  (p. 101)

## pp. 104–105 — restricted lives

> "Let us introduce the notion of a restricted life, a life that is significantly deficient in one or more of the major respects that generally make human lives valuable and worth living."  (p. 105)

> "Now, suppose that we adopt the principle that, other things being equal, conditions of society or the world are intrinsically undesirable from a moral point of view to the extent that they involve people living restricted lives."  (p. 105)

Relied on for: the name "Paradox of Future Individuals", the credit to
  Adams, Parfit and Schwartz as independent discoverers, the Obligation
  Principle, the laissez-faire argument, the slave child case, the
  "wronging without harming" reply and the restricted-lives principle.
Context: Kavka's p. 95 n. 6 says the conclusion "leaves open the
  possibility that we have an obligation to our ancestors, our
  contemporaries, or our immediate descendants". Between pp. 97 and 100 he
  considers a nuclear-versus-solar energy case and an "Extended Obligation
  Principle" plus a restricted maximizing principle; he argues these
  cannot handle the slave child. On pp. 101–102 he answers two objections
  (that only the surrender of the child, or only the couple's motives, is
  wrong). His concluding paragraph (p. 112) says his principles "provide
  grounds for rejecting the conclusion of the Paradox of Future
  Individuals".

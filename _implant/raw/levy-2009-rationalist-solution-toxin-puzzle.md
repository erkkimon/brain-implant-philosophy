> "Gregory Kavka’s ‘Toxin Puzzle’ suggests that I cannot intend to perform a counter-preferential action A even if I have a strong self-interested reason to form this intention."  (abstract, p. 267)

> "(5) I cannot intend to do what I (rightly) believe to be fundamentally irrational – i.e. something that I have no good reason to do and a (substantial) reason not to do"  (p. 268, step 5 of "Kavka's Argument")

> "(6) and (7) are counter-intuitive. They both conflict with our intuition that I can form the intention – especially for $1m."  (p. 268)

> "(8) that I can form the intention to drink the toxin; (9) that I can drink the toxin; or (10) that it is rational – i.e. that I have a sufficiently good reason, all things considered – to drink the toxin."  (p. 268, the three strategies)

> "The Rationalist Solution is arguably the most popular approach to TP, at least in the philosophical literature."  (p. 269)

> "I ultimately face only two options tonight – what Kavka (1978, p. 295) refers to as a ‘cruel dilemma’ and what McClennen (1990, p. 231) refers to as an intra-personal ‘coordination problem.’"  (p. 269)

> "For the inference from (1) to (2) in Kavka’s Argument is invalid."  (p. 271, stating the Rationalist Solution)

> "if it is rational for me to intend to A at future time t when I anticipate circumstances C at t, then, whatever my preferences at t, it is rational for me to A at t if C obtain at t"  (pp. 271–272, the Rational Action Principle, RAP)

> "We simply cannot ‘bootstrap’ the rationality of a self-destructive act from the rationality of a self-benefiting intention."  (p. 272, stating the Anti-RAP Objection)

> "But Gauthier also subscribes to step (5) in Kavka’s Argument, which holds that it is impossible to intend to perform an action that I know or believe to be fundamentally irrational."  (p. 272)

> "In the Third Course, while I intend tonight to drink the toxin tomorrow, I change my mind tomorrow (after I have already won the $1m) and decide to refrain from drinking the toxin."  (p. 276)

> "A course of action is a plan that I end up executing, an intention that ultimately results in the intended action. And I cannot coherently plan to go back on my present intention."  (p. 276, reporting Gauthier's second response)

> "(24) is clearly controversial. Some philosophers, including myself, reject it."  (p. 278)

> "(24) If the Course is rational, then my drinking the toxin is also rational."  (p. 278, the proposition Levy says Gauthier "must either assume or demonstrate")

> "The Self-Promise Argument fails because my rational intention to drink the toxin construed as a self-promise imposes at most a practical, not a moral, obligation upon me to act in accord with it."  (p. 282)

> "For while I win $1m either way, changing my mind spares me the day of pain that following through would cost me."  (p. 282)

> "Bratman (1996, pp. 55–56; 1998a, 57, pp. 62–63) and Farrell (1989) appear to be sympathetic to Kavka’s Argument."  (p. 283, n. 3)

> "Bratman seems tacitly to assume throughout his discussions of TP that if it is rational for me to intend to A, then I can intend to A."  (p. 283, n. 6)

> "The Rationalist Solution is best known in the literature as the theory of ‘resolute planning.’ The resolute approach is supposed to constitute a way around (or through) the false dichotomy between being ‘myopic’ and being ‘sophisticated.’"  (p. 284, n. 7)

> "Instead, I cannot help but be sophisticated."  (p. 283, n. 2)

> "Bratman (1987, pp. 103–104) concludes from this argument that I have good reason not to intend to drink the toxin but only to cause myself to have the intention to drink the toxin, which he regards as different."  (p. 284, n. 14)

> "Bratman (1987, pp. 105–06; 1998b, pp. 61 ff.) rejects RAP, even though he endorses two similar propositions – the ‘Intention-action principle’ (1987, pp. 54–55) and the ‘linking principle’ (1998a, p. 55; 1998b, p. 62)."  (p. 285, n. 20)

> "Although he distinguishes between several different versions of RAP, Parfit (1984, pp. 37–40; 2001, pp. 82 ff., 91–92) ultimately rejects it."  (p. 285, n. 20)

> "I agree with Holton (2004, p. 512), Kavka (1984, pp. 156–57), and Parfit (2001, pp. 91–92) that rational irrationality is possible."  (p. 284, n. 13)

Relied on for: Levy's reconstruction of Kavka's Argument (1)–(7) and the
three strategies (8)–(10); the label "Rationalist Solution" and its owners
(n. 6: Gauthier 1984b, 1994, 1998a, 1998b; Harman 1998; Holton 2004;
McClennen 1990); RAP and the Anti-RAP Objection; the Self-Promise Argument
and Gauthier's Rational Self-Interest Argument; the Third Course and
Gauthier's two responses as Levy reports them; Levy's own conclusion; his
reports of Bratman's, Parfit's and Kavka's stances on RAP and on rational
irrationality.
Context: Levy writes as a critic of the Rationalist Solution ("I will
ultimately argue, however, that the Rationalist Solution fails", p. 269).
Kavka's Argument as Levy states it: (1) whether I win does not depend on
what I do tomorrow afternoon; (2) so I will have no good reason to drink;
(3) I will have a substantial reason not to (pain); (4) I will recognise
this tonight; (5) as quoted; (6) so I cannot form the intention; (7) so I
cannot win (p. 268). He cites Kavka 1984, pp. 156–57 alongside 1983. Levy
holds the intention itself rational (§2.2: the $1m outweighs a likely day
of pain), and denies that this rationality transfers to the drinking. n. 6
adds that Bratman's own attempt "does not clearly follow any one of the
three strategies". The Gauthier passages Levy quotes (notes 30, 43) are from
Gauthier 1997a and 1998a and are not used on the page.

# SEP "Hedonism" (Andrew Moore) — the experience machine as a non-necessity objection, and hedonist replies

Source: Andrew Moore, "Hedonism", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published 2004-04-20, substantive revision 2013-10-17. §2.3.1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/hedonism/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read; copyright

> "Nozick (1971) and Nagel (1970) present schematic descriptions of lives that have all the appearance but none of the reality of self-understanding, achievement, loving relationships, self-directedness, and so on, alongside lives that have these appearances and also the corresponding realities."  (§2.3.1)

> "hedonism is committed to the hedonic equality and thus the equal value of these lives."  (§2.3.1)

> "Commenting on his more fantastical and more famous ‘experience machine’ case, Nozick added further detail, claiming that it is also good in itself “to do certain things, and not just have the experience [as if] of doing them”, “to be a certain way, to be a certain sort of person” and not just to be an “indeterminate blob” floating in a tank, and “to make a difference in the world” rather than merely to appear to oneself to do so. He concluded: “something matters to us in addition to experience” (Nozick 1974: 43–44)."  (§2.3.1)

> "The objectors' claim is that there is something that is sufficient for value and that is missing from the life of perfect pleasure. If the objection stands then pleasure is not necessary for value."  (§2.3.1)

> "There is a range of possible hedonist responses to non-necessity objections. One reply is that the allegedly non-hedonic item on which the objector focuses just is an instance of pleasure, so its being valuable is just what a hedonist would expect."  (§2.3.1)

> "A third reply hedonists might make to non-necessity objections is to allow that the item in question is or includes non-pleasure that has value, but then to argue that this is merely instrumental value."  (§2.3.1)

> "A fourth and more concessive reply is that the item in question might be a non-pleasure and might be sufficient for non-instrumental value of some sort (e.g., moral value), but to add that there is also at least one sort of value (e.g., prudential value) for which pleasure is necessary."  (§2.3.1)

> "His conclusion was that “your life would be the life, not of a man, but of an oyster” (Philebus 21a)."  (§2.3.1)

Relied on for: Moore's classification of the case as a non-necessity
  objection, the Nozick 1971 / Nagel 1970 precursors, his summary of
  Nozick's claims, the four families of hedonist reply, and the Philebus
  precedent.
Context: §2.3.1 surveys "non-necessity objections" to ethical hedonism
  (Plato's oyster, Carlyle's swine, Nozick and Nagel, Moore's beauty,
  Ross's two worlds) and then lists hedonist replies; the entry's
  concluding remarks say it "has not attempted any such systematic
  comparative examination" of hedonism against its rivals.

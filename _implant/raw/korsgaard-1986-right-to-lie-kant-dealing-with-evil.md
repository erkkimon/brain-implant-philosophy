> "The most well-known example of this "rigorism", as it is sometimes called, concerns Kant's views on our duty to tell the truth."  (pp. 325–326; DASH manuscript punctuation; the journal prints "rigorism," with the comma inside the quotation marks)

> "Kant's readers differ about whether Kant's moral philosophy commits him to the claims he makes in these passages."  (p. 327)

> "Unsympathetic readers are inclined to take them as evidence of the horrifying conclusions to which Kant was led by his notion that the necessity in duty is rational necessity"  (p. 327)

> "Sympathetic readers are likely to argue that Kant here mistook the implications of his own theory, and to try to show that, by careful construction and accurate testing of the maxim on which this liar acts, Kant's conclusions can be blocked by his own procedures."  (p. 327)

> "First, I want to argue that Kant's defenders are right in thinking that, when the case is treated under the Formula of Universal Law, this particular lie can be shown to be permissible."  (p. 327)

> "it becomes clear why Kant is committed to the view that lying is wrong in every case"  (p. 327)

> "morality itself sometimes allows or even requires us to do something that from an ideal perspective is wrong"  (p. 327)

> "it shows that we need special principles for dealing with evil"  (p. 327)

> "A murderer who expects to conduct his business by asking questions must suppose that you do not know who he is and what he has in mind."  (p. 329)

> "On the other hand, suppose that the murderer does, contrary to my supposition, announce his real intentions. Then the arguments that I have given do not apply. In this case, I believe your only recourse is refusal to answer (whether or not the victim is in your house, or you know his whereabouts)."  (p. 330, n. 4)

> "It is permissible to lie to deceivers in order to counteract the intended results of their deceptions, for the maxim of lying to a deceiver is universalizable."  (p. 330)

> "He has created a situation which universalization cannot reach."  (p. 330)

> "When we apply the Formula of Humanity, however, the argument against lying that results applies to any lie whatever."  (p. 330)

> "coercion and deception are the most fundamental forms of wrongdoing to others"  (p. 333)

> "In a Kantian theory our responsibility has definite boundaries"  (p. 337)

> "lying to the murderer at the door was not shown to be permissible in a straightforward manner: the maxim did not so much pass as evade universalization"  (p. 337)

> "This result impugns Kant's belief that the formulas are equivalent."  (p. 337)

Korsgaard quoting Kant's *Lectures on Ethics* (Infield tr., LE 228):

> "if I cannot save myself by maintaining silence, then my lie is a weapon of defence"  (p. 338)

> "The common thought that lying to a liar is a form of self-defense, that you can resist lies with lies as you can resist force with force, is according to this analysis correct."  (p. 338)

Korsgaard's note 12 (p. 339), quoting Newman via Bok:

> "Cardinal John Henry Newman, responding to Samuel Johnson's claim that he would lie to a murderer who asked which way his victim had gone, suggests that the appropriate thing to do is "to knock the man down, and to call out for the police""  (p. 339, n. 12)

> "When dealing with evil circumstances we may depart from this ideal."  (p. 346)

> "special principles to use when dealing with evil"  (p. 349)

Relied on for: the sympathetic/unsympathetic map of readers; Korsgaard's Universal Law result
(lying to a deceiver passes), her Humanity result (every lie wrong), her "double-level"
(ideal / non-ideal) reconstruction; Kant's LE 228 "weapon of defence" as she quotes it;
Newman's alternative via Bok.
Context: Korsgaard opens with two Kant passages (the servant case in the Doctrine of Virtue,
6:431, and the essay's murderer case, SRL 427). Her three aims (p. 327): the Universal Law
test permits this lie; the Humanity/Kingdom of Ends formulas explain why Kant holds every lie
wrong; an ethical system needs "special principles" for evil, modelled on Rawls's ideal and
non-ideal theory (pp. 342–349) and on Kant's laws of war (p. 349). Note 4 (printed on p. 330) states
her assumption that a questioner who hides his purpose deceives; if the murderer announces his
intentions, the arguments do not apply (quoted above), and an extorted answer may be a lie.

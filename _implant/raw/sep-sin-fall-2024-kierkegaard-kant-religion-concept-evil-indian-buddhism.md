## (1) Lippitt & Evans, "Søren Kierkegaard"

> "On the surface this book is an inquiry into the nature of original or “hereditary” sin, but in reality it probes the nature of human choice in general, particularly the origins of evil."  (§2, of The Concept of Anxiety)

> "While Haufniensis claims that evil or sin cannot be scientifically explained because it has its origins in freedom, he nonetheless believes that anxiety helps us to understand how sin is possible."  (§2)

> "To accept forgiveness requires “sin-consciousness:” an awareness of oneself as being in the wrong."  (§3.3.3)

> "In The Sickness Unto Death, Anti-Climacus claims that the despair of the forgiveness of sins is itself a sin (“offense”,) which can only be transcended in faith."  (§3.3.3)

## (2) Pasternack & Fugate, "Kant's Philosophy of Religion"

> "In Part One, Kant’s aim is to examine the Christian doctrine of Original Sin, especially as he would have been exposed to it through his Lutheran Pietist upbringing."  (§3.7.2)

> "its understanding of Original Sin is essentially Augustinian while its understanding of moral restoration is Pelagian"  (§3.6.2, reporting the "Conundrum" interpretation of Quinn, Wolterstorff and others)

## (3) Calder, "The Concept of Evil"

> "By this he means that all human beings have a propensity to subordinate the moral law to self-interest and that this propensity is radical, or rooted, in human nature in the sense that it is inextirpable."  (§2.2, on Kant 1793, Bk I)

> "Richard Bernstein argues that Kant cannot coherently hold both of these theses since we could not be responsible for a propensity that is in us originally and that we cannot be rid of (Bernstein 2002, 11–35)."  (§2.2)

## (4) Goodman & Schultz, "Ethics in Indian Buddhism"

> "The Pāli Canon contains the claim that Saints have “abandoned goodness (puñña) and vile actions (pāpa).”"  (§4)

> "“Goodness” and “vile actions” refer to actions which have karmic effects in the future, projecting a future existence that includes happiness or suffering, respectively."  (§4)

Relied on for: Kierkegaard's two books on sin as the SEP authors report
  them; Kant's Religion Part One as an examination of the doctrine of
  Original Sin, and the Conundrum reading; Calder's statement of radical
  evil and Bernstein's objection; pāpa as karmically effective "vile
  actions".
Context: (1) The authors add that Haufniensis holds anxiety "is the state
  that precedes sin" and that a free action comes from a "leap"; they
  call Kosch (2006) "Perhaps the best scholarly treatment" (their
  assessment). (2) The Conundrum school, per the authors, "principally
  flows from Karl Barth’s 1947" history; a rival school reads Kant as a
  successful Christian apologist (§3.6.1). (3) Calder goes on to report
  philosophers (Formosa) who find "important insights" in radical evil.
  (4) The authors report Keown (1992, ch. 4) against reading the passage
  as exempting Saints from ethical norms.

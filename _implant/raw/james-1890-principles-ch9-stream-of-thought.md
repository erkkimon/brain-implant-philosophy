# James 1890 — Principles of Psychology, ch. IX "The Stream of Thought": five characters and the stream metaphor

Source: William James, The Principles of Psychology, vol. I (New York:
  Henry Holt, 1890), chapter IX "The Stream of Thought", pp. 225 and 239.
Original: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin9.htm
  (Classics in the History of Psychology transcription, with the 1890
  pagination in brackets; public domain).
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (read in the HTML at the URL above; page markers
  [p. 225] and [p. 239] in the transcription)

> "How does it go on? We notice immediately five important characters in
>  the process, of which it shall be the duty of the present chapter to
>  treat in a general way:
>  1) Every thought tends to be part of a personal consciousness.
>  2) Within each personal consciousness thought is always changing.
>  3) Within each personal consciousness thought is sensibly continuous.
>  4) It always appears to deal with objects independent of itself.
>  5) It is interested in some parts of these objects to the exclusion of
>  others, and welcomes or rejects — chooses from among them, in a word —
>  all the while."  (Principles I, p. 225)

> "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits. Such
>  words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly as it presents
>  itself in the first instance. It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river'
>  or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described.
>  In talking of it hereafter, let us call it the stream of thought, of
>  consciousness, or of subjective life."  (Principles I, p. 239)

Relied on for: the claims that James characterised consciousness by five
features (personal, changing, continuous, object-directed, selective) and
coined the "stream of thought / stream of consciousness" metaphor against
"chain" or "train" models built from discrete elements.
Context: the chapter opens by rejecting the analytic method that starts
from simple sensations ("nobody ever had a simple sensation by itself");
the stream passage is James's conclusion to the section on continuity
(character 3), after discussing time-gaps such as sleep and the "feelings
of relation" that join thoughts.

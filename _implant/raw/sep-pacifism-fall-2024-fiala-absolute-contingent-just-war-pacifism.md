## The continuum (§1)

> "Pacifism is often located on a continuum for assessing the morality of war that includes realism, just war theory, and pacifism. The fundamental question here is “can war be justified?” (see Fiala and Kling, 2023)."  (§1)

> "Some authors have used the just war theory to derive a version of pacifism described as “contingent pacifism” (May 2015, for example) or “just war pacifism” (Sterba 1998)."  (§1)

> "Holmes argues that the just war tradition typically ignores the central moral issue in war, which is about the presumptive immorality of the “massive, systematic and deliberate killing of human beings” that occurs in war (Holmes 2017a: xvii)."  (§1)

> "Ryan argues that although the just war tradition and pacifism developed through “mutual critique” pacifism has often been marginalized as an “outcast tradition” (Ryan 2017: 125)."  (§1)

## Absolute and contingent pacifism (§2.1)

> "So, absolute pacifism holds that war and violence are always wrong."  (§2.1)

> "He concludes by asserting a “very strong form of pacifism” and admits that this results in some uncomfortable conclusions, such as that “even military action aimed at protecting people against acute and systematic human-rights violations cannot be justified” (Fox 2014, 126)."  (§2.1)

> "Fox asks, “Should immoral actions be used to stop other (perhaps gravely more) immoral actions?”(Fox 2014, 127)."  (§2.1)

> "He answers “No,” and maintains that this shows us that we have to work to find ways to move beyond our reliance on war as the solution to social and political problems."  (§2.1)

> "A similar conclusion has been argued by Holmes who concludes that morality and warfare are “antithetical” (Holmes 2017a p.25)."  (§2.1)

> "While absolute pacifism admits no exceptions to the rejection of war and violence, contingent pacifism is usually understood as a principled rejection of a particular war."  (§2.1)

> "In Indian traditions, it is grounded in the commitment to ahimsa or nonviolence that is derived from a larger metaphysical picture which emphasizes karmic interdependence, ascetic self-abnegation, and compassion."  (§2.1)

## Just war pacifism (§§2.2, 3)

> "As John Rawls says of what he calls “contingent pacifism,” “the possibility of a just war is conceded but not under present circumstances” (Rawls 1971, 382)."  (§2.2)

> "just war pacifism maintains that modern wars are not fought according to the standards of the just war theory because, for example, they make use of aerial bombardment and other means that do not adequately discriminate between combatants and noncombatants."  (§2.2)

> "Perhaps the most important conclusion of just war pacifism is that the burden of proof rests on the proponent of war"  (§3)

## Rights, double effect, Anscombe (§4, §4.1)

> "This idea has been rejected by Anscombe, Narveson, and others who argue that the idea of human dignity or human rights can necessitate the use of violence in defense of these rights."  (§4)

> "As David Luban puts this: “Such rights are worth fighting for. They are worth fighting for not only by those to whom they are denied but, if we take seriously the obligation which is indicated when we speak of human rights, by the rest of us as well” (Luban 1980, 170)."  (§4)

> "As Anscombe puts this, “pacifism teaches people to make no distinction between the shedding of innocent blood and the shedding of any human blood” (Anscombe 1981a, 58)"  (§4.1)

> "Absolute pacifists will argue against this employment of the doctrine of double effect and will claim that the killing of the innocent in war is always wrong, even if it is an unintended effect."  (§4)

> "Although Kant is not himself a pacifist, one might be able to ground pacifism in Kant’s alternative version of the moral law"  (§3)

## Other traditions (§5.1)

> "Hindus, Jains, and Buddhists share a concern for ahimsa or nonviolence as a basic moral virtue."  (§5.1)

> "But scholars such as Cole (2021) and Shadi (2017) show that the diverse Muslim tradition includes a number of pacifist thinkers. And Alvi (2021) explains that a notion of nonviolent jihad can be found in Islam in relation to the idea of jihad as spiritual struggle, and with regard to the role of nonviolent activism in Islamic contexts."  (§5.1)

## Appended 2026-09-27 — Aquinas and the just war use of double effect (§4)

Source and edition as above; §4 re-read in the Fall 2024 archive page

> "The just war tradition, however, allows that innocent noncombatants may be killed according to the principle of double effect. This idea is derived in the Christian tradition from Aquinas, who holds that a single act may have two effects."  (§4)

> "It is significant that Aquinas does not expand this discussion to make it permissible to kill an innocent third party."  (§4, Fiala's assessment)

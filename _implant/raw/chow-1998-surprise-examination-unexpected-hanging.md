> "It has many variants, the earliest probably being Lennart Ekbom’s
>  surprise drill, and the best known to mathematicians (thanks to Quine
>  and Gardner) being an unexpected hanging."  (§1, arXiv p. 1)

> "Confident in this conclusion, they are of course totally surprised when
>  the exam occurs (on Wednesday, say). The announcement is vindicated
>  after all."  (§1, arXiv pp. 1–2)

> "However, it has inspired an amazing variety of philosophical and
>  mathematical investigations that have in turn uncovered links to
>  Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, game theory, and several other logical
>  paradoxes (e.g., the liar paradox and the sorites paradox)."
>  (introduction, arXiv p. 1)

> "In such cases I believe it is misguided to speak of the resolution of
>  the paradox."  (§1, arXiv p. 3; "the" is italicised in the original)

> "But both approaches are reasonable and neither is guilty of evasion."
>  (§1, arXiv p. 3)

> "The meta-paradox consists of two seemingly incompatible facts. The
>  first is that the surprise exam paradox seems easy to resolve."
>  (§1, arXiv p. 2)

> "The second (astonishing) fact is that to date nearly a hundred papers
>  on the paradox have been published, and still no consensus on its
>  correct resolution has been reached."  (§1, arXiv p. 2)

> "For most other trivial-sounding paradoxes there is broad consensus on
>  the proper resolution, whereas for the surprise exam paradox there is
>  not even agreement on its proper formulation."  (§1, arXiv p. 2)

> "For example, there has been much debate between what I call the
>  “epistemological school” (which formalizes the paradox using concepts
>  such as knowledge, belief and memory) and the “logical school” (which
>  avoids such concepts) over who has the “right” formalization."
>  (§1, arXiv p. 3)

> "In other words, the announcement must be formulated as a
>  self-referential statement!"  (§2, arXiv p. 5)

> "Indeed, Shaw concludes his paper [25] with just such a comment."
>  (§2, arXiv p. 5; [25] = R. Shaw, Mind 67 (1958), 382–384)

> "Thus, although self-reference is not illegitimate in all circumstances,
>  it is illegitimate here because this particular self-referential
>  statement is self-contradictory. Fitch’s proof has a satisfying air of
>  definitiveness, and seems to vindicate Shaw."  (§2, arXiv p. 6)

> "The most important is that the proof does not give any explanation for
>  why the teacher’s announcement appears to be vindicated after the
>  fact."  (§2, arXiv p. 6; objection to the logical analysis)

> "This shows that certain plausible assumptions about knowledge—KI, KD,
>  and KE, together with the assumption that the students know that they
>  will know the content of the announcement throughout the week—are
>  inconsistent."  (§3, arXiv p. 8)

> "Perhaps the most popular candidate has been the assumption that after
>  hearing the announcement, the students “know” the content of the
>  announcement."  (§3, arXiv p. 8)

> "This curious phenomenon is known as a “Moore paradox” or a “blindspot,”
>  and the surprise exam paradox may be viewed as simply a more intricate
>  version of this situation."  (§3, arXiv p. 9)

> "This approach is essentially the one offered in [3], [6], [18], [21],
>  [22], [24], and [29]."  (§3, arXiv p. 9; [3] Binkley 1968, [24] Quine
>  1953, [29] Sorensen 1984)

> "Others have argued that the assumption KaKb(‡) is plausible only if one
>  invokes the “temporal retention principle” (the students know that they
>  will not forget the announcement during the week) or “Hintikka’s KK
>  principle” (if one knows something then one knows that one knows it),
>  and that one or both of these assumptions should be discarded."
>  (§3, arXiv p. 9)

> "The significance of this variation is that in our preceding
>  formalization we can let Ka mean “Art knows” and Kb mean “Bob knows”
>  and then KaKb(‡) appears to be immediately plausible without reference
>  to time or the KK principle."  (§3, arXiv p. 9; Sorensen's
>  designated-student variant, [28])

> "A very interesting variant of the epistemological approach, that of
>  Kaplan and Montague [15], is a kind of hybrid of the logical and
>  epistemological schools. They prove a theorem called “the Paradox of
>  the Knower” that is reminiscent of Tarski’s theorem on the
>  indefinability of the truth predicate."  (§3, arXiv p. 9)

> "Some authors have made the fascinating suggestion that the surprise
>  exam paradox may be related to the iterated prisoner’s dilemma."
>  (§4, arXiv p. 10)

> "Indeed, Sorensen [30] has argued that the two are really the same, and
>  has substantially revised his analysis of the surprise exam as a
>  result."  (§4, arXiv p. 11)

> "In the iterated prisoner’s dilemma, the conclusion about invariable
>  defection is counterintuitive, but it does not lead to an explicit
>  contradiction."  (§4, arXiv p. 11)

> "Therefore, I believe that the iterated prisoner’s dilemma is
>  essentially distinct from the surprise examination paradox and is not
>  just a variant"  (§4, arXiv p. 11)

> "However, a more reasonable assumption is that the teacher’s
>  announcement, whatever it means, does not mean (c), and that therefore
>  the students misinterpret the announcement when they make their
>  argument."  (§2, arXiv p. 7; Chow's answer to the objection above)

> "Some have seen connections between the surprise exam and the sorites
>  paradox"  (§5, arXiv p. 12; citing [8], [26], [31] = Williamson 1992)

> "Specifically, I decided that the Kaplan–Montague paradox of the knower
>  was distinct from the surprise examination paradox"  (Exhaustive
>  Bibliography, preface, arXiv p. 14)

Relied on for: the name "unexpected hanging" and the Ekbom/Gardner/Quine
history of variants; Chow's "meta-paradox" (no consensus after ~100
papers, per Chow 1998); the logical school (self-referential
announcement, Shaw, Fitch) and the epistemological school (knowledge
axioms, "know the announcement" as popular candidate, temporal
retention / KK); Sorensen's designated-student variant; the
prisoner's-dilemma and sorites connections; bibliographic data for
O'Connor 1948, Scriven 1951, Wright & Sudbury 1977, Williamson.
Context: Chow is a mathematician writing for mathematicians; he states
his own view that "Most of the controversies have been false
controversies" (§1) because the paradox can admit several adequate
formalizations — that is Chow's position, not a finding. Chow gives
Sorensen's "Recalcitrant versions of the prediction paradox" as
Australasian J. Phil. 69 (1982); Crossref gives volume 60 (1982),
355–362, title "Recalcitrant variations of the prediction paradox" (see
`surprise-examination-paradox-bibliographic-records.md`).

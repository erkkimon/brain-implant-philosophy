# Stroll 2010 — "Moore's paradox revisited": the 1944 talk, Wittgenstein's letter (via Monk 1990), the assertion/belief split

> "In October, 1944 G. E. Moore gave a talk to the Moral Science Club in Cambridge that contained a sentence that has become known as “Moore’s paradox”."  (Introduction)

> "Wittgenstein, who at times made caustic remarks about Moore’s intelligence, immediately wrote to Moore, urging him to publish his “discovery”. In his letter, Wittgenstein explained why he thought that Moore’s finding was so important."  (Introduction)

Wittgenstein's letter as Stroll quotes it, "(Cited in Monk 1990: 545)":

> "You have said something about the logic of assertion."

> "This assertion has to be ruled out and is ruled out by “common sense”, just as a contradiction is. And this just shows that logic isn’t as simple as logicians think it is. In particular: that contradiction isn’t the unique thing people think it is. It isn’t the only logically inadmissible form and it is, under certain circumstances, admissible. And to show that seems to me the chief merit of your paper."

> "As Ray Monk has pointed out, this was not how Moore himself saw it. He thought that, as the paradox did not issue in a formal contradiction, it was an absurdity for psychological, rather than for logical, reasons – an interpretation that Wittgenstein vigorously rejected."  (Introduction)

> "those who claim that Moore’s sentence (which I shall present below) is a paradox but are not sure why it is; and those who think, as I do, that its appearance as paradoxical is apparent only"  (Introduction)

> "In the 1944 essay, Moore formulated the paradox in this way: Smith left the room but I don’t believe he has."  (The paradox)

> "The dissenting parties fall into two categories: those who find – as apparently Wittgenstein did – that the paradox turns on the concept of assertion, and those who think it turns on the concept of belief."  (Assertion and belief)

> "My sense is that commentators on the paradox have split more or less evenly on this issue"  (Assertion and belief)

> "I think both views are incorrect, and here are some arguments to that effect."  (Annulling the paradox)

Relied on for: the October 1944 Moral Science Club talk; Wittgenstein's
  letter (text only as Stroll quotes it from Monk 1990: 545); Monk's
  report of Moore's "psychological" reading; Stroll's two-family grouping
  (assertion vs. belief) and his own dissolving view.
Context: The letter's middle sentence, as the text layer gives it, reads
  "it makes no sense to assert “I-p is the case and I don’t believe that p
  is the case.”" — the "I-p" is left unquoted on pages because it could not
  be checked against Monk or the published letters. The letter's wording
  is "the chief merit of your paper"; no source read for this page has
  Wittgenstein calling the paradox Moore's "most important" contribution
  (the wording in the English Wikipedia article, cited there to Letters to
  Russell, Keynes and Moore, ed. von Wright, 1974, which is lending-only on
  archive.org and was not read). Stroll's "1944 essay" sentence differs in
  wording from the 1944 printed sentence ("I believe he has gone out, but
  he has not", Schilpp vol. V p. 204; see
  moore-1944-russells-theory-of-descriptions-he-has-gone-out.md). Stroll
  gives no reference for his "1944 essay" in the passage read, so pages
  quote his sentence as his and do not identify the essay. Moore's
  "Moore's Paradox", printed in Moore, Selected Writings (ed. Baldwin,
  1993), pp. 207–212 (bibliographic data from the SEP "Introspection"
  bibliography and Smithies 2016), was not read.

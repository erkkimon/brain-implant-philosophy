## The article (1988 reprint text)

> "Let’s get in the car and go to Abilene and have dinner at the cafeteria.”"  (anecdote, p. 17)

> "Since my own preferences were obviously out of step with the rest I replied, “Sounds good to me,”"  (anecdote, p. 17)

> "“Listen, I never wanted to go to Abilene. I just thought you might be bored."  (anecdote, the father-in-law, p. 18)

> "when none of us had really wanted to go. In fact, to be more accurate, we’d done just the opposite of what we wanted to do."  (anecdote, p. 18)

> "Stated simply, it is as follows: Organizations frequently take actions in contradiction to what they really want to do and therefore defeat the very purposes they are trying to achieve."  (introduction, p. 19)

> "It also deals with a major corollary of the paradox, which is that the inability to manage agreement is a major source of organization dysfunction."  (introduction, p. 19)

> "The inability to manage agreement, not the inability to manage conflict, is the essential symptom that defines organizations caught in the web of the Abilene Paradox."  ("Symptoms of the Paradox", p. 19)

> "3. Organization members fail to accurately communicate their desires and/or beliefs to one another. In fact, they do just the opposite and thereby lead one another into misperceiving the collective reality."  ("Symptoms", subsymptom 3, pp. 19–21)

> "4. With such invalid and inaccurate information, organization members make collective decisions that lead them to take actions contrary to what they want to do, and thereby arrive at results that are counterproductive to the organization’s intent and purposes."  ("Symptoms", subsymptom 4, p. 21)

> "6. Finally, if organization members do not deal with the generic issue—the inability to manage agreement—the cycle repeats itself with greater intensity."  ("Symptoms", subsymptom 6, p. 21)

> "In fact, it is my contention that the inability to cope with (manage) agreement, rather than the inability to cope with (manage) conflict, is the single most pressing issue of modern organizations."  ("Symptoms", close, p. 21)

> "Porter replied, “In all honesty, because of the fear of the group pressure that would ensue, of not being a team player,”"  (Case No. 2, "The Watergate", quoting The Washington Post, 8 June 1973, p. 22)

> "The Abilene Paradox can be stated succinctly as follows: Organizations frequently take actions in contradiction to the data they have for dealing with problems and, as a result, compound their problems rather than solve them."  ("Analyzing the Paradox", p. 23)

> "Like all paradoxes, the Abilene Paradox deals with absurdity."  ("Analyzing the Paradox", p. 23)

> "However, as Robert Rapaport and others have so cogently expressed it, paradoxes are generally paradoxes only because they are based on a logic or rationale different from what we understand or expect."  ("Analyzing the Paradox", p. 23)

> "Discovering that different logic not only destroys the paradoxical quality but also offers alternative ways for coping with similar situations."  ("Analyzing the Paradox", p. 23)

> "(1) Action Anxiety; (2) Negative Fantasies; (3) Real Risk; (4) Separation Anxiety; and (5) the Psychological Reversal of Risk and Certainty."  ("Analyzing the Paradox", the five landmarks, p. 23)

> "The concept of action anxiety says that the reasons organization members take actions in contradiction to their understanding of the organization’s problems lies in the intense anxiety that is created as they think about acting in accordance with what they believe needs to be done."  ("Action Anxiety", p. 23)

> "But therein lies a paradox within a paradox, because our very unwillingness to take such risks virtually ensures the separation and aloneness we so fear."  ("The Psychological Reversal of Risk and Certainty", p. 27)

> "In effect, we reverse “real existential risk” and “fantasied risk” and by doing so transform what is a probability statement into what, for all practical purposes, becomes a certainty."  (same section, p. 27)

> "“It takes a real team effort to go to Abilene.”"  (implications, on responsibility, p. 27)

> "Sometimes referred to as Groupthink, it has been damned as the cause for everything from the lack of creativity in organizations"  ("Group Tyranny and Conformity", p. 29)

> "However, analysis of the dynamics underlying the Abilene Paradox opens up the possibility that individuals frequently perceive and feel as if they are experiencing the coercive organization conformity pressures when, in actuality, they are responding to the dynamics of mismanaged agreement."  ("Group Tyranny and Conformity", p. 29)

> "Specifically, many of the events that Janis describes as examples of conformity pressures (that is, group tyranny) I would conceptualize as mismanaged agreement."  ("Selected Bibliography", on Irving Janis, Victims of Groupthink (1972), p. 35)

> "Albert Camus in The Myth of Sisyphus and Other Essays (Vintage Books, Random House, 1955) provides an existential viewpoint for coping with absurdity, of which the Abilene Paradox is a clear example."  ("Selected Bibliography", p. 35)

> "I have found one way in particular to be effective—confrontation in a group setting."  ("Coping with the Paradox", p. 31)

## Epilogue (1988)

> ""The Abilene Paradox" was born October 9, 1971 as part of a presentation I gave to the Organization Development (OD) Network about some variations of ideas contained in a paper Dick Albertson and I had written on neurotic organizations."  ("Abilene Revisited: An Epilogue", opening, p. 35)

> "All this has occurred despite the fact that, to this day, I cannot prove scientifically that the Abilene Paradox actually occurs."  (Epilogue, "The Paradox as Phantom", p. 37)

> "For example, it is altogether possible that my in-laws really wanted to go on the original trip, but changed their stories once we arrived in Abilene and had such a lousy time."  (Epilogue, "The Paradox as Phantom", p. 37)

Relied on for: the anecdote; Harvey's two statements of the paradox; the six subsymptoms; the five landmarks of his explanation; the "paradox within a paradox"; his reconceptualisation of group tyranny / "Groupthink" as mismanaged agreement and his note on Janis; his remark on what makes a paradox (after Rapaport); the Watergate case; his epilogue's statement that he cannot prove the paradox occurs.
Context: Harvey narrates a July Sunday in Coleman, Texas, when his father-in-law proposed the 53-mile trip to Abilene; on return each family member says he or she had not wanted to go. He then states the paradox, lists symptoms, gives two cases (an R&D project at "the Ozyx Corporation" and Watergate), offers a "map" of causes, discusses implications (conflict, collusion, responsibility, group tyranny), diagnosis, coping by confrontation in a group setting, and closes with Camus's Sisyphus.

> "The theist traditions hold a deep fear of idolatry—of giving one’s ‘ultimate concern’ (Tillich 1957 [2001]) to an object unworthy of it."  (Faith §4)

> "So too is Paul Tillich’s account of faith as ‘the state of being ultimately concerned’, since the claim of the object of one’s ultimate concern to ‘promise total fulfilment even if all other claims have to be subjected to it or rejected in its name’ cannot in principle be established on the basis of the evidence (Tillich 1957 [2001, 1 and 21])."  (Faith §7, after Kierkegaard as an example of a "doxastic venture model")

> "Nevertheless, Aquinas’s own assumptions on these matters may leave him closer to Kierkegaard and Tillich than is commonly thought"  (Faith §7)

> "Faith will not, however, be a virtue as such , if it is accepted that faith can be misplaced or, even, ‘demonic’, directed upon a ‘false ultimate’ (Tillich 1957 [2001, 21])."  (Faith §10)

> "Tillich’s (1951) theory that religious language is symbolic is perhaps an early expression of the view, and Anthony Kenny and Sally McFague are more recent proponents of a view of this type (see also Jüngel 1974; Sarot 1992). William Alston, the chief critic of the theory,"  (Religious Language §3.1, on the view that talk of God is "irreducibly metaphorical")

> "The identification of God with being itself is a common Christian view, from Augustine to Tillich, but it is not exclusive to Christian thought."  (Pantheism §4)

> "Its engagement with the relationship between faith and freedom and the incomprehensibility of God shaped theological debates through the lectures and writings of Karl Barth, Paul Tillich, and Martin Buber, among others."  (Existentialism, preamble)

Relied on for: Tillich's place in SEP accounts of faith (doxastic venture, idolatry, faith as virtue), religious language (symbolic theory and its critic Alston), God as being itself, and existentialism (with Barth and Buber).
Context: Bishop & McKaughan class Kierkegaard and Tillich together as "doxastic venture" models; Scott names Alston as chief critic of the irreducibly-metaphorical view.

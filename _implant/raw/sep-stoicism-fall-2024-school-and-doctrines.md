# SEP "Stoicism" (Durand) — founding, scholarchs, three parts of philosophy, the telos, the five indemonstrables

Source: Marion Durand, "Stoicism", The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
  (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.); first
  published 2023-01-20 (replacing the former entry by the previous author).
  Preamble, §1.1, §1.2, §3.4, §4.1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/stoicism/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only). The entry cites ancient texts by the
  numbering of Long & Sedley, The Hellenistic Philosophers (e.g. "Stobaeus,
  63B").
Retrieved: 2026-09-25 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read)

> "Stoicism was one of the dominant philosophical systems of the
>  Hellenistic period. The name derives from the porch (stoa poikilê) in
>  the Agora at Athens decorated with mural paintings, where the first
>  generation of Stoic philosophers congregated and lectured."  (preamble)

> "The Stoic school was founded around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium [...]
>  The Stoa competed with the school founded only a little before in
>  Athens by Epicurus, and Stoic and Epicurean views are often compared and
>  contrasted. Zeno was succeeded in the leadership of the Stoa first by
>  Cleanthes of Assos and then by Chrysippus of Soli, who headed the school
>  from around 230 until 206 BCE and was by all accounts its foremost
>  theorist and systematizer."  (§1.1)

> "Following Chrysippus, the position of “scholarch” passed to his former
>  students, the last of which being Diogenes of Babylon in the middle of
>  the 2nd century BCE."  (§1.1)

> "Diogenes and Antipater also heralded increased engagement with Platonic
>  (Sedley 2003; Reydams-Schils 1999) and possibly Aristotelian doctrine,
>  arguably peaking with Panaetius in the second half of 2nd century BCE and
>  his student Posidonius in the first century BCE."  (§1.1)

> "Some scholars see this moment as marking a shift in the Stoic school,
>  from the so-called “Old Stoa” to “Middle Stoicism”, though the relevance
>  and accuracy of this nomenclature is debated (see Inwood 2022). [...] A
>  significant feature of the second century is the sharp and powerful
>  attacks against Stoic views formulated by the Academic Carneades, to
>  which Antipater of Tarsus, Diogenes’ successor, responded at length."
>  (§1.1)

> "Appointed to the court of Emperor Augustus in the late 1st century BCE
>  was the Stoic philosopher Arius Didymus [...] Also active in the second
>  century CE was the Stoic philosopher Hierocles"  (§1.1)

> "Although never identifying as a Stoic himself, Cicero [...] engaged
>  extensively with Stoic theory [...] Seneca served as advisor to Nero.
>  Epictetus, a former slave, was expelled from Rome by Domitian along with
>  other philosophers in 93 CE, and his works, recorded by his student
>  Arrian, heavily influenced Marcus Aurelius, Roman Emperor from 161–180
>  CE."  (§1.1)

> "The nature and extent of these intra-school developments and
>  disagreements [...] remains the subject of ongoing scholarly debate."
>  (§1.1)

> "Stoic philosophy was, from Zeno onwards, conceived of as comprising
>  three parts: physics (phusikê), logic (logikê), and ethics (êthikê). [...]
>  all three parts of philosophy were thought by the Stoics to work together
>  to form an interconnected and coherent system (exactly how strongly the
>  claim of systematization is to be taken is disputed; see below)."  (§1.2)

> "This definition of arguments made the Stoic system one of what we might
>  now call propositional logic, by contrast with Aristotle’s logic of terms
>  or predicate logic. Syllogisms are arguments that take the form of one of
>  the five so-called “indemonstrables” [...] if p then q; p; therefore q
>  (modus ponens); if p then q; not-q; therefore not-p (modus tollens)"
>  (§3.4)

> "Zeno represented the end as: ‘living in agreement’. [...] Cleanthes,
>  [Zeno’s] first successor, added ‘with nature’, and represented it as
>  follows: ‘the end is living in agreement with nature’. (Stobaeus, 63B)"
>  (§4.1, quoting Stobaeus in Long & Sedley's numbering)

Relied on for: founding date, place and founder; the succession Zeno →
Cleanthes → Chrysippus and Chrysippus as systematizer; the contested Old /
Middle Stoa labels; Roman Stoics (Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius) and
Cicero as a non-Stoic engaging the school; the rivalry with Epicureans and
Academic sceptics; the three-part system and the dispute over its
systematicity; Stoic logic as propositional, with modus ponens and modus
tollens among the indemonstrables; the telos formula.

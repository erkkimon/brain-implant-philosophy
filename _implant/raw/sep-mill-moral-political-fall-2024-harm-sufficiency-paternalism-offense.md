> "To constitute a harm, an action must be injurious or set back important interests of particular people, interests in which they have rights (I 12; III 1; IV 3, 10, 12; V 5)."  (§3.6)

> "Weak Sufficiency: Harm to others is a pro tanto justification of regulation. Strong Sufficiency: Harm to others is a conclusive justification of regulation."  (§3.6)

> "Once we distinguish these options, there is a pretty compelling case for thinking that Mill rejects strong sufficiency but embraces weak sufficiency."  (§3.6)

> "But notice that if Mill rejects strong sufficiency then this compromises his one very simple principle."  (§3.6)

> "First, state power is liable to abuse."  (§3.7)

> "Second, even well intentioned rulers will misidentify the good of citizens."  (§3.7)

> "In particular, these arguments provide no principled objection to paternalism—no objection to successful paternalistic restrictions on B’s liberty that do in fact benefit B."  (§3.7)

> "It seems impossible to square this with Mill’s blanket prohibition on offense regulation."  (§3.8)

> "For the most part, both sides conceded that these activities were immoral but harmless and debated whether it could be permissible to regulate them as a matter of enforcing sexual morality."  (§3.9)

> "Other Millian liberals, such as Feinberg, treat the rejection of legal moralism as a constitutive commitment of liberalism."  (§3.9)

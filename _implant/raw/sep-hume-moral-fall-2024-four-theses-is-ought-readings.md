> "Hume’s position in ethics, which is based on his empiricist theory of the mind, is best known for asserting four theses:"  (preamble)

> "There is heated debate about what Hume intends by each of these theses and how he argues for them."  (preamble)

> "Hume sides with the moral sense theorists: we gain awareness of moral good and evil by experiencing the pleasure of approval and the uneasiness of disapproval when we contemplate a character trait or action from an imaginatively sensitive and unbiased point of view."  (§1)

> "Hume roundly criticizes Hobbes for his insistence on psychological egoism or something close to it, and for his dismal, violent picture of a state of nature."  (§1)

> "Hume explicitly favors an ethic of character along “ancient” lines."  (§1)

> "Interpreters disagree as to whether Hume is an instrumentalist or a skeptic about practical reason."  (§3)

> "Either way, Hume denies that reason can evaluate the ends people set themselves; only passions can select ends, and reason cannot evaluate passions."  (§3)

> "a deduction that seems to Hume “altogether inconceivable” (T3.1.1.27)."  (§5)

> "Few passages in Hume’s work have generated more interpretive controversy."  (§5)

> "According to the dominant twentieth-century interpretation, Hume says here that no ought-judgment may be correctly inferred from a set of premises expressed only in terms of ‘is,’ and the vulgar systems of morality commit this logical fallacy."  (§5)

> "A number of present-day philosophers, including R. M. Hare, endorse this putative thesis of logic, calling it “Hume’s Law.”"  (§5)

> "(As Francis Snare observes, on this reading Hume must simply assume that no purely factual propositions are themselves evaluative, as he does not argue for this.)"  (§5)

> "Some interpreters think Hume commits himself here to a non-propositional or noncognitivist view of moral judgment — the view that moral judgments do not state facts and are not truth-evaluable."  (§5)

> "Other interpreters — the more cognitivist ones — see the paragraph about ‘is’ and ‘ought’ as doing none of the above."  (§5)

> "Some read it as simply providing further support for Hume’s extensive argument that moral properties are not discernible by demonstrative reason, leaving open whether ethical evaluations may be conclusions of cogent probable arguments."  (§5)

> "They point out that Hume himself makes such inferences frequently in his writings."  (§5)

> "Four main interpretations have significant textual support."  (§6)

> "either it gives vent to a feeling, or it is itself a feeling (Flew, Blackburn, Snare, Bricke)."  (§6)

Relied on for: Cohon's four theses; the moral-sense side and the
predecessors (Hobbes, Hutcheson); the instrumentalist vs sceptical readings
of "slave of the passions"; the dominant twentieth-century ("Hume's Law",
Hare) and cognitivist readings of T 3.1.1.27, with Snare's observation; the
four readings of moral evaluation in §6.
Context: the four theses are (1) reason alone cannot motivate, "slave of the
passions"; (2) moral distinctions not derived from reason; (3) derived from
moral sentiments of spectators; (4) some virtues natural, others, including
justice, artificial. §6 names the four readings as nonpropositional,
subjective description, dispositional (Mackie), and a two-state view
(Capaldi; Cohon's own "moral sensing" version).

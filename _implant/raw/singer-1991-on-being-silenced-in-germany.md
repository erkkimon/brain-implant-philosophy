> "For the 1989/1990 winter semester, Dr. Hartmut Kliemt, a professor of philosophy at the University of Duisburg, a small town in the north of Germany, offered a course in which my book Practical Ethics was the principal text assigned to the class."  (opening)

> "Kliemt’s course, however, was subjected to organized and repeated disruption by protesters objecting to the use of the book on the grounds that in one of its ten chapters it advocates active euthanasia for severely disabled newborn infants."  (opening)

> "There will be no Wittgenstein Symposium in 1991."  (opening)

> "It stems from an invitation I received to speak, in June 1989, at a European Symposium on “Bioengineering, Ethics, and Mental Disability,”"  (Marburg)

> "My intention in these lectures was to defend a view for which I have argued in several previously published works: that the parents of severely disabled newborn infants should be able to decide, together with their physician, whether their infant should live or die."  (Marburg)

> "Just a day or two before I was due to leave for Germany, my invitation to speak at the Marburg conference was abruptly withdrawn."  (Marburg)

> "Shortly before the symposium was due to open, Lebenshilfe and the Bishop Bekkers Institute canceled the entire event."  (Marburg)

> "I sent a brief reply in which I pointed out that I was advocating euthanasia not for anyone like themselves, but for severely disabled newborn infants, and that it was crucial to my defense of euthanasia that these infants would never have been capable of grasping that they are living beings with a past and a future."  (Der Spiegel)

> "In contrast to the Nazi ideology that the state should decide who was worthy of life, my view was designed to reduce the power of the state and allow parents to make crucial life and death decisions, both for themselves and, in consultation with their doctors, for their newborn infants."  (Saarbrücken)

> "When I rose to speak in Saarbrücken I was greeted by a chorus of whistles and shouts from a minority of the audience determined to prevent me from speaking."  (Saarbrücken)

> "he and others in the audience were taken aback when I told them that I was the child of Austrian-Jewish refugees, and that three of my grandparents had died in Nazi concentration camps."  (Saarbrücken)

> "while I hold that some lives are so severely blighted from the beginning that they are better not continued, I also believe that once a life has been allowed to develop, then in every case everything should be done to make that life as satisfying and rich as possible."  (Saarbrücken)

> "Before it began, a group of disabled people in wheelchairs, who had been admitted to the flat area at the front of the lecture theater, staged a brief protest in which they said that, while it was all the same to them whether or not I lectured on the topic of animal rights, they objected to the fact that the University of Zurich had invited such a notorious advocate of euthanasia to discuss ethical issues that also concerned the disabled."  (Zurich, May 1991)

> "At that point one of the protesters came up behind me and tore my glasses from my face, throwing them on the floor and breaking them."  (Zurich, May 1991)

Relied on for: the protests against Singer in Germany, Austria and
Switzerland, 1989–1991, as Singer himself reports them (one-sided: the
protesters' own statements are given only as he reports them), and his
replies (the view he meant to defend; the Nazi comparison rejected; family
history; support for the lives of disabled people once begun).
Context: the essay opens with "Some scenes from academic life in Germany and
Austria today"; the Wittgenstein Symposium in Kirchberg was cancelled after
protests against invited speakers including Singer, Meggle and R. M. Hare.
Singer also reports that Der Spiegel did not print his reply to an article
by Franz Christoph. The essay's wording "mental retardation" and "normal
child" is his 1991 wording.

> "The 1970s saw the ascent of a second rationale, autonomy, as the predominant justification of informed consent, through influential work by Ruth Faden, Tom Beauchamp, James Childress, and a U.S. President’s Commission"  (§2.2)

> "Likewise, for bioethicists Beauchamp and Childress, “The autonomous individual acts freely in accordance with a self-chosen plan, analogous to the way an independent government manages its territories and establishes its policies” (Beauchamp and Childress 2008, 99–100)."  (§2.2)

> "As the authors emphasize (Beauchamp and Childress 2008, 100–1), autonomy so understood differs from autonomy as having the will that one wants to have (Frankfurt 1988)."  (§2.2)

> "And if they did, then it would be absurd to claim, as proponents of the autonomy rationale do (Beauchamp and Childress 2008, e.g. pp. 12, 14), that informed consent and the principle of autonomy are distinct from, sometimes conflict with, and often supersede, the principle of beneficence."  (§2.2)

Relied on for: the rise of autonomy as the predominant rationale of informed
  consent in the 1970s; Beauchamp and Childress's characterisation of the
  autonomous individual, as Eyal quotes it (2008, 99–100); their distinction
  from Frankfurt's autonomy; Eyal's objection to the well-being version of
  the autonomy rationale.
Context: §2.1 gives the first rationale, protection; §2.2 reports
  instrumental and intrinsic-value defences of autonomy in health care, then
  doubts that informed consent or autonomous choice "invariably makes a
  patient's life better" (Buchanan and Brock 1989), then a Kantian version on
  which "autonomy should command our awe and reverence, whether or not it is
  good for us (Hill 1991, 43)".

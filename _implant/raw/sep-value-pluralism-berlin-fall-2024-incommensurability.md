From (a), Mason:

> "This entry is concerned with moral pluralism—the view that there are many different moral values."  (preamble)
> "Moral value pluralism should be distinguished from political pluralism."  (preamble)
> "Pluralists have argued that there are incommensurabilities and discontinuities in value comparisons, value remainders (or residues) when choices are made, and complexities in appropriate responses to value."  (§2)
> "The most extreme form of discontinuity is incommensurability or incomparability, when two values cannot be ranked at all."  (§2.1)
> "Griffin denies that pluralism entails incommensurability (Griffin uses the term incomparability) whereas other pluralists embrace incommensurability, but deny that it entails that rational choice is impossible."  (§2.1)
> "Irreducible plurality appears to imply incommensurability—that is to say, that there is no common measure which can be used to compare two different values."  (§4)
> "This assumption is at once very powerful and utterly baseless."  (§4, quoting Williams 1985, p. 17)
> "Making a similar point, Ruth Chang points out that incommensurability is often conflated with incomparability."  (§4)
> "She provides clear definitions of each: incommensurability is the lack of a common unit of value by which precise comparisons can be made. Two items are incomparable, if there is no possible relation of comparison, such as ‘better than’, or ‘as good as’ (1997, Introduction)."  (§4)
> "Chang points out that incommensurability is often thought to entail incomparability, but it does not."  (§4)
> "…it does not follow from there being no super-value that there is no super-scale."  (§4.2, quoting Griffin 1986, p. 90)
> "Joseph Raz takes the line that we can explain choice between irreducibly plural goods by talking about basic preferences."  (§4.3)
> "The classical conception, by contrast, “regards reasons as rendering options eligible” (Raz 1999, p. 47)."  (§4.3)
> "When I choose one tin of soup from a row of identical tins in the shop, it would be wrong and misleading to say that I wanted that tin rather than, or in preference to, the others."  (§4.3, quoting Raz 1999, p. 48)
> "One philosopher who is happy to accept that there may be situations where we just cannot make reasoned choices between plural values is Isaiah Berlin, who claimed that goods such as liberty and equality conflict at the fundamental level."  (§4.4)
> "Berlin is primarily concerned with political pluralism, and with defending political liberalism, but his views about incomparability have been very influential in discussions on moral pluralism."  (§4.4)

From (b), Cherniss & Hardy:

> "In speaking of the incommensurability of values, Berlin seems to have meant that there is no common measure, no ‘common currency’ in terms of which the relative importance of any two values can be established in the abstract."  (§4.1)
> "One can make a three-way distinction, between weak incommensurability, moderate incommensurability and radical incommensurability."  (§4.4)
> "Berlin goes further than this, but it is not clear whether he presents a moderate or a radical version of incommensurability."  (§4.4)
> "Berlin does sometimes offer more starkly dramatic accounts of incommensurability, which make it hard to rule out the more radical interpretation of the concept, according to which incommensurability is more or less synonymous with incomparability."  (§4.4)
> "As a result, choices among values cannot be based on (objectively valid) evaluative comparisons, but only on personal preference, or on an act of radical, arbitrary choice, which Berlin sometimes calls ‘plumping’."  (§4.4)

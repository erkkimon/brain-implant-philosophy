# SEP "Moral Dilemmas" (McConnell) — §6 moral residue (Williams, Marcus), §8 Walzer's politician and "the dirty hands problem"

Source: Terrance McConnell, "Moral Dilemmas", The Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri Nodelman (eds.);
  first published 2002-04-15, substantive revision 2022-07-25. §§6, 8.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/moral-dilemmas/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (HTML of the Fall 2024 archive page read)

> "Michael Walzer (1973) claims that the political ruler, qua political ruler, ought to do what is best for the state; that is his principal role-related obligation. But he also ought to abide by the general obligations incumbent on all."  (§8)

> "Since each of these requirements is binding, Walzer believes that the politician faces a genuine moral dilemma, though, strangely, he also thinks that the politician should choose the good of the community rather than abide by the general moral norms."  (§8)

> "Such a situation is sometimes called “the dirty hands problem.” The expression, “dirty hands,” is taken from the title of a play by Sartre (1946). The idea is that no one can rule without becoming morally tainted."  (§8)

> "C.A.J. Coady (2008) has suggested that this reveals a “messy morality.”"  (§8)

> "For opponents of moral dilemmas, the problem of dirty hands represents both a challenge and an opportunity."  (§8)

> "(The best known proponents of arguments for dilemmas that appeal to moral residue are Williams 1965 and Marcus 1980; for a more recent contribution, see Tessman 2015, especially Chapter 2.)"  (§6)

> "The first objection, in effect, suggests that the argument is question-begging (McConnell 1978 and Conee 1982); the second objection challenges the assumption that remorse and guilt are appropriate only when the agent has done wrong."  (§6)

Relied on for: McConnell's report that Walzer treats the politician's case
as a genuine moral dilemma while also holding the politician should choose
the community (with McConnell's word "strangely", attributed); the origin of
the expression in Sartre's play; Williams 1965 and Marcus 1980 as the best
known proponents of the moral-residue argument, per McConnell; the two
objections to that argument; dirty hands as "challenge and opportunity" for
opponents of dilemmas.
Context: §8 is titled "Multiple Moralities" and treats conflicts between
general and role-related obligations; Walzer's examples McConnell lists are
the deal with a dishonest ward boss and authorising torture to uncover a
bomb plot. McConnell dates Sartre's play 1946; Reynolds (SEP "Sartre", Fall
2024 ed., rev. 2022) lists "1948c [1949], Les mains sales: pièce en sept
tableaux, Paris: Gallimard. First produced 1948. Translated as Dirty Hands,
in No Exit, and Three Other Plays, New York: Vintage Books, 1949." and in the
entry's opening: "plays like No Exit (1947), Flies (1947), and Dirty Hands
(1948)". The play's text was not read.

## Justinian, Institutes I.1 pr. (Moyle 1913; Latin)

> "Justice is the set and constant purpose which gives to every man his due."  (Inst. 1.1 pr., Moyle; line break in the Gutenberg text joined)

> "Iustitia est constans et perpetua voluntas ius suum cuique tribuens."  (Inst. 1.1 pr., Latin Library)

## Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V (Ross)

> "The just, then, is the lawful and the fair, the unjust the unlawful and the unfair."  (V.1)

> "This form of justice, then, is complete virtue, but not absolutely, but in relation to our neighbour."  (V.1)

> "Of particular justice and that which is just in the corresponding sense, (A) one kind is that which is manifested in distributions of honour or money or the other things that fall to be divided among those who have a share in the constitution (for in these it is possible for one man to have a share either unequal or equal to that of another), and (B) one is that which plays a rectifying part in transactions between man and man."  (V.2)

> "This, then, is what the just is-the proportional; the unjust is what violates the proportion."  (V.3; hyphen as on the page)

> "But the justice in transactions between man and man is a sort of equality indeed, and the injustice a sort of inequality; not according to that kind of proportion, however, but according to arithmetical proportion."  (V.4)

> "Of political justice part is natural, part legal, natural, that which everywhere has the same force and does not exist by people's thinking this or that; legal, that which is originally indifferent, but when it has been laid down is not indifferent,"  (V.7)

> "And this is the nature of the equitable, a correction of law where it is defective owing to its universality."  (V.10)

## Hume, Treatise 3.2.2

> "and may easily conclude, that if men were supplied with every thing in the same abundance, or if every one had the same affection and tender regard for every one as for himself; justice and injustice would be equally unknown among mankind."  (T 3.2.2; line breaks in the Gutenberg text joined)

> "Here then is a proposition, which, I think, may be regarded as certain, that it is only from the selfishness and confined generosity of men, along with the scanty provision nature has made for his wants, that justice derives its origin."  (T 3.2.2; line breaks joined)

Context: (1) The definition opens Title I; the next sentence defines
jurisprudence as "the science of the just and the unjust". (2) V.1
distinguishes justice as lawfulness (complete virtue towards others) from
justice as fairness/equality; V.2–4 divide particular justice into
distributive (geometrical proportion, "according to merit") and
rectificatory (arithmetical proportion, the judge equalizing gain and
loss); V.7 divides political justice; V.10 treats equity as a correction
of law. (3) Hume argues that the rules of justice are established "by the
artifice of men" (section heading); the abundance-and-benevolence
sentence follows his remark that friends and married people "are
unacquainted with the mine and thine".

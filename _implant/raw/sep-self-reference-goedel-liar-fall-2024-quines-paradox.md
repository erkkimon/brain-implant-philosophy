## A. Self-Reference and Paradox (Bolander)

Source: Thomas Bolander, "Self-Reference and Paradox", The Stanford
  Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri
  Nodelman (eds.); first published 2008-07-15, substantive revision
  2024-07-19. §1 intro, §1.6, §2.1, §2.4, §3.1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/self-reference/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-02 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped,
  line breaks joined, grepped; LaTeX left as in the HTML source)

> "A paradox is a seemingly sound piece of reasoning, based on apparently true assumptions, that still leads to a contradiction (Quine, 1976)."  (§1 intro)

> "Most classical paradoxes of self-reference involve direct self-reference as in the liar paradox where a sentence refers directly to itself."  (§1.6)

> "it is easy to construct paradoxes that only employ indirect self-reference, i.e., sentences that refer to other sentences that refer to yet other sentences in such a way as to form a loop back to the original sentence."  (§1.6)

> "In 1985, Yablo succeeded in constructing a semantic paradox that does not involve self-reference at all, not even indirect self-reference."  (§1.6)

> "Tarski showed that the liar paradox is formalisable in any formal theory containing his schema T, and thus any such theory must be inconsistent. This result is often referred to as Tarski’s theorem on the undefinability of truth."  (§2.1)

> "In order to construct such a formalisation it is necessary to be able to formulate self-referential sentences (like the liar sentence) within first-order arithmetic. This ability is provided by the diagonal lemma."  (§2.1)

> "Let \(S\) be a theory extending first-order arithmetic. For every formula \(\phi(x)\) there is a sentence \(\psi\) such that \(S \vdash \psi \leftrightarrow \phi \langle \psi \rangle\)."  (§2.1, "Diagonal lemma.")

> "The reader not familiar with Gödel codings (also known as Gödel numberings) can just think of the mapping \(\langle \cdot \rangle\) as a naming device or quotation mechanism for formulae—just like quotation marks in natural language."  (§2.1)

> "is of course not self-referential in a strict sense, but mathematically it behaves like one. It is therefore possible to use sentences generated by the diagonal lemma to formalise paradoxes based on self-referential sentences, like the liar."  (§2.1, of the sentence the lemma yields)

> "The diagonal lemma is sometimes called the fixed-point lemma"  (§2.1)

> "In the proof above we reduced Gödel’s incompleteness theorem to an application of Tarski’s theorem in order to show the close link between the two (this version of the proof is due to Bolander, 2002). Gödel was well aware of this link, and indeed it seems that Gödel even proved Tarski’s theorem before Tarski himself did (Feferman, 1984)."  (§2.4)

> "This hierarchy effectively blocks the liar paradox, since now a sentence can only express the truth or untruth of sentences at lower levels, and thus a sentence such as the liar that expresses its own untruth cannot be formed."  (§3.1, on Tarski's hierarchy of languages)

> "Building an explicit (well-founded) hierarchy to solve the paradoxes is today by most considered an overly drastic and heavy-handed approach."  (§3.1)

> "even though the paradoxes do indeed disappear, so do all non-paradoxical occurrences of self-reference."  (§3.1, followed by Kripke's 1975 Nixon/Jones example)

Relied on for: direct vs indirect self-reference; the diagonal lemma and
the "quotation mechanism" reading of Gödel codes; Tarski's theorem;
Bolander's reduction of Gödel's theorem to Tarski's; Tarski's hierarchy and
Bolander's report of how it is regarded.
Context: Bolander nowhere names Quine's quotation sentence; Quine appears
in the entry for the definition of paradox (1976) and for New Foundations.
The link to Quine's paradox is drawn on the page from Quine's own essay,
not from Bolander.

## B. Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems (Raatikainen)

Source: Panu Raatikainen, "Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems", The Stanford
  Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition), Edward N. Zalta & Uri
  Nodelman (eds.); first published 2013-11-11, substantive revision
  2020-04-02. §2.4, §5.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/goedel-incompleteness/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only)
Retrieved: 2026-10-02 (archive HTML fetched with curl, tags stripped, grepped)

> "In the literature, this lemma is sometimes also called “the self-referential lemma” or “the fixed point lemma”."  (§2.4, of the Diagonalization Lemma)

> "It is often said that given a property denoted by \(A(x)\), the sentence \(D\) is a self-referential sentence which “says of itself” that it has the property \(A\). Such figures of speech may be heuristically useful, but they are also easily misleading and suggest too much."  (§2.4)

> "does not claim any sort of sameness of meaning."  (§2.4, of the lemma's material equivalence)

> "As to the Diagonalization Lemma, actually Gödel himself originally demonstrated only a special case of it, that is, only for the provability predicate. The general lemma was apparently first discovered by Carnap 1934 (see Gödel 1934, 1935)."  (§5)

> "Gödel soon faced various paradoxes (such as the Liar paradox), and had to conclude that arithmetical truth cannot be defined in arithmetic."  (§5)

Relied on for: the lemma's names; Raatikainen's caution about "says of
itself"; Carnap's priority for the general lemma.
Context: §2.4 states the lemma as F proves D if and only if A applied to
the code of D, then applies it to the negated provability predicate to get
the Gödel sentence (§2.5).

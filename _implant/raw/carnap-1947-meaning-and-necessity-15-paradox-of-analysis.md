> "The concept of intensional structure may also help in clarifying a puzzling situation that has been called “the paradox of analysis”. It was recently stated by G. E. Moore, and then discussed by C. H. Langford, Max Black, and Morton White."  (p. 63; footnote markers dropped)

> "‘The concept Brother is identical with the concept Male Sibling.’"  (p. 63)

> "Black tries to show that the two sentences do not express the same proposition;"  (p. 63)

> "White replies that this is not a sufficient reason for the assertion. None of the four authors states his criterion for the identity of “meaning”, “statement”, or “proposition”; this seems the chief cause for the inconclusiveness of the whole discussion."  (p. 63)

> "The difference between the two expressions, and, consequently, between the two sentences is a difference in intensional structure, which exists in spite of the identity of intension."  (p. 64)

> "It seems to me that this cognitive equivalence is explicated by our concept of L-equivalence and that the synonymity, which does not hold for these expressions, is explicated by intensional isomorphism."  (p. 64)

Relied on for: Carnap's list of the early discussants (Moore, Langford,
  Black, White); his diagnosis of the debate; his own solution by
  intensional structure (L-equivalence vs. intensional isomorphism).
Context: Carnap's footnotes cite Moore in Schilpp (1942), pp. 660–67;
  Langford, ibid., pp. 321–42; Black, Mind LIII (1944), 263–67 and LIV
  (1945), 272 f.; White, Mind LIV (1945), 71 f. and 357–61. Between the
  quotations Carnap reports Black's paraphrase via the relation Conjunct
  and says that on L-equivalence as the identity condition for
  propositions "White is certainly right", while Black "feels correctly,
  like Moore and Langford, that there is an important difference in
  meaning" (Carnap's assessments).

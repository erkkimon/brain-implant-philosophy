# Butler 1726 — Fifteen Sermons, Sermon XI: immoderate self-love "may be so prevalent as to disappoint itself"

Source: Joseph Butler, *Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel*
  (1726), Sermon XI, "Upon the Love of Our Neighbour" (Romans xiii. 9).
Original: Project Gutenberg eBook #3150, *Human Nature, and Other Sermons*
  (released 2002-03-01), transcribed from the 1887 Cassell & Co. edition:
  https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3150 (public domain). Dietz (2021)
  cites the same passage as Butler (1726: 364–373, 367) in D. D. Raphael
  (ed.), *British Moralists 1650–1800*, vol. 1 (Hackett, 1991).
Retrieved: 2026-10-01 (plain text fetched from
  https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3150/pg3150.txt; sermon read from
  its heading; italics marked with underscores in the source are dropped)

> "Happiness or satisfaction consists only in the enjoyment of those objects which are by nature suited to our several particular appetites, passions, and affections."  (Sermon XI)

> "So that if self-love wholly engrosses us, and leaves no room for any other principle, there can be absolutely no such thing at all as happiness or enjoyment of any kind whatever; since happiness consists in the gratification of particular passions, which supposes the having of them."  (Sermon XI)

> "Disengagement is absolutely necessary to enjoyment; and a person may have so steady and fixed an eye upon his own interest, whatever he places it in, as may hinder him from attending to many gratifications within his reach, which others have their minds free and open to."  (Sermon XI)

> "Immoderate self-love does very ill consult its own interest: and, how much soever a paradox it may appear, it is certainly true that even from self-love we should endeavour to get over all inordinate regard to and consideration of ourselves."  (Sermon XI)

> "the contracted affection may be so prevalent as to disappoint itself, and even contradict its own and private good."  (Sermon XI)

Relied on for: Butler's statement that self-love which engrosses the mind
  defeats its own end, his ground for it (happiness consists in gratifying
  particular passions), and his own use of the word "paradox".
Context: the sermon argues that self-love and benevolence are not opposed;
  the passage leads into the claim that benevolence is no more at odds with
  self-love than other particular affections are.

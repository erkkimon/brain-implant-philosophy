> "Alan Dershowitz reported in 2002 that “[d]uring numerous public appearances since September 11, 2001, I have asked audiences for a show of hands as to how many would support the use of nonlethal torture in a ticking-bomb case. Virtually every hand is raised.”"  (p. 1426, quoting Dershowitz, Why Terrorism Works 150 (2002); line-break hyphens removed)

> "Second, I will criticize the liberal ideology of torture and suggest that ticking-bomb stories are built on a set of assumptions that amount to intellectual fraud (Parts III and IV). Ticking-bomb stories depict torture as an emergency exception, but use intuitions based on the exceptional case to justify institutionalized practices and procedures of torture."  (p. 1427)

> "Suppose the bomb is planted somewhere in the crowded heart of an American city, and you have custody of the man who planted it. He won’t talk. Surely, the hypothetical suggests, we shouldn’t be too squeamish to torture the information out of him and save hundreds of lives. Consequences count, and abstract moral prohibitions must yield to the calculus of consequences."  (p. 1440)

> "The Schlesinger Report on Abu Ghraib notes that “[f]or the U.S., most cases for permitting harsh treatment of detainees on moral grounds begin with variants of the ‘ticking time-bomb’ scenario.”"  (p. 1440, quoting the Schlesinger Report at 908, 974, per Luban's n. 42)

> "The ticking time bomb is proffered against liberals who believe in an absolute prohibition against torture. The idea is to force the liberal prohibitionist to admit that yes, even he or even she would agree to torture in at least this one situation."  (p. 1440)

> "I don’t mean that the time-bomb scenario is completely unreal."  (p. 1441)

> "The ticking-bomb scenario cheats its way around these difficulties by stipulating that the bomb is there, ticking away, and that officials know it and know they have the man who planted it. Those conditions will seldom be met."  (p. 1442)

> "A one-percent chance of saving a thousand lives yields ten statistical lives. Does that mean that you can torture up to nine people on a one-percent chance of finding crucial information?"  (p. 1443)

> "The real debate is not between one guilty man’s pain and hundreds of innocent lives. It is the debate between the certainty of anguish and the mere possibility of learning something vital and saving lives."  (p. 1444)

> "This is why Alan Dershowitz has argued that judges, not torturers, should oversee the permission to torture, which in his view must be regulated by warrants."  (p. 1452)

> "Politicians pick judges, and if the politicians accept torture, the judges will as well. Once we create a torture culture, only the naive would suppose that judges will provide a safeguard. Judges do not fight their culture—they reflect it."  (p. 1452)

> "For all these reasons, the ticking-bomb scenario is an intellectual fraud."  (p. 1452)

Footnote 46 (p. 1442), verbatim excerpt: "Gross reminds us, however, that
the catastrophic case can actually occur. Id. at 1503–04." (citing Oren
Gross, "Are Torture Warrants Warranted? Pragmatic Absolutism and Official
Disobedience", 88 Minn. L. Rev. 1481 (2004)).

Relied on for: Luban's statement of the scenario and of its dialectical
purpose; "intellectual fraud" (Luban's verdict, attributed); the stipulation
objection; the one-percent arithmetic (checked on the page); Luban's
reframing of "the real debate"; his report of Dershowitz's warrants and of
Dershowitz's show-of-hands claim; Gross's point that the case "can actually
occur", as Luban reports it.
Context: Luban's essay argues that liberal culture both abhors torture and,
through the ticking-bomb story, builds a "torture culture"; p. 1441 gives a
1995 Philippine case (torture of a bomb-maker that preceded the discovery of
an airliner plot) as a "real-life counterpart", then argues that the
officials did not know of the plot when they began. Part V is a case study of
executive-branch lawyers' memoranda. Line-break hyphenation in the PDF text
has been rejoined in the quotations.

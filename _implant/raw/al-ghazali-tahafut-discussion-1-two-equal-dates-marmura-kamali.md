The philosophers' objection, as al-Ghazali states it (Marmura, §43):

> "Indeed, if in front of a thirsty person there are two glasses of water that are similar in every respect in relation to his purpose [of wanting to drink], it would be impossible for him to take either."  (§43, Marmura p. 22)

> "Otherwise, differentiating something from its like is in no circumstance conceivable."  (§43, Marmura p. 23)

Al-Ghazali's reply (Marmura, §46):

> "For we will suppose that there are two equal dates in front of someone gazing longingly at them, unable, however, to take both together. He will inevitably take one of them through an attribute whose function is to render a thing specific, [differentiating it] from its like."  (§46, Marmura p. 23)

> "All the specifying things you have mentioned by way of goodness, proximity, and ease of taking we can suppose to be absent, the possibility of taking [one of the two] yet remaining."  (§46, Marmura p. 23)

> "You could either say that equality in relation to the individual’s purpose is utterly inconceivable, which is sheer foolishness, the supposition [of this equality] being possible; or else, that if the equality is supposed, the man yearning [for the dates] would ever remain undecided, looking at them but taking neither through pure will and choice that [according to you] are dissociated from the objective [of taking a specific one]. This also is impossible, its falsity known by [rational] necessity."  (§46, Marmura pp. 23–24; OCR hyphenation at "dissoci-ated" joined, footnote number 10 after "possible;" removed)

The same passage in Kamali's translation:

> "Let us suppose that there are two equal dates before a man who is fond of them, but who cannot take both of them at once. So he will take only one of them ; and this, obviously, will be done—by an attribute of which the function is to distinguish something from its like!"  (Problem I, Kamali pp. 26–27)

Relied on for: the two equal dates as al-Ghazali's example; that it
answers the philosophers' two-glasses objection; the structure of his
reply (either equality is inconceivable, or the man stays undecided
forever; both rejected), hence an attribute (will) that specifies one of
two like things; that the setting is the dispute over God's will and the
world's origination (the First Discussion).
Context: The philosophers argue that an eternal will cannot single out one
moment of creation from others like it; the Ash'arite reply is that will
is by definition "an attribute whose function ... is to differentiate a
thing from its similar" (§41). §45 argues that human will is a false
analogy for the divine; §46 adds that even in the human case the choice
between similars is conceivable; §47 turns the objection back on the
philosophers' own cosmology.

> "In its broadest sense, “value theory” is a catch-all label used to encompass all branches of moral philosophy, social and political philosophy, aesthetics, and sometimes feminist philosophy and the philosophy of religion — whatever areas of philosophy are deemed to encompass some “evaluative” aspect."  (preamble)

> "In its narrowest sense, “value theory” is used for a relatively narrow area of normative ethical theory particularly, but not exclusively, of concern to consequentialists."  (preamble)

> "In this narrow sense, “value theory” is roughly synonymous with “axiology”."  (preamble)

> "Of course, the central question philosophers have been interested in, is that of what is of intrinsic value, which is taken to contrast with instrumental value."  (§2.1.1)

> "Paradigmatically, money is supposed to be good, but not intrinsically good: it is supposed to be good because it leads to other good things: HD TV’s and houses in desirable school districts and vanilla lattes, for example."  (§2.1.1)

> "Philosophers’ adoption of the term “intrinsic” for this distinction reflects a common theory, according to which whatever is non-instrumentally good must be good in virtue of its intrinsic properties."  (§2.1.1)

> "Consequently, sometimes the term “intrinsic” is reserved for what is good in virtue of its intrinsic properties, or for the view that goodness itself is an intrinsic property, and non-instrumental value is instead called “telic” or “final” (Korsgaard [1983])."  (§2.1.1)

> "Instrumental value is also sometimes contrasted with “constitutive” value."  (§2.1.1)

> "The idea behind this distinction is that instrumental values lead causally to intrinsic values, while constitutive values amount to intrinsic values."  (§2.1.1)

> "And those things, in turn, may be good only for what they lead to, but eventually, it is argued, something must be good, and not just for what it leads to."  (§2.1.1)

> "It does not make sense, for example, to say that something is a good can opener, but only instrumentally, or that Sue is a good dancer, but only instrumentally."  (§2.1.2)

> "For example, as important as he held the value of knowledge to be, Mill was committed to holding that its value is instrumental, not intrinsic."  (§2.2)

> "Moore disagreed, holding that knowledge is indeed a value, but an intrinsic one, and this expanded Moore’s list of basic values."  (§2.2)

> "Kant’s thesis that rational agents have a dignity and not a price is often taken to be a thesis about a kind of incommensurability, as well."  (§2.3.2)

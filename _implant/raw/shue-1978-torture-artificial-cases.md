> "Nevertheless, it cannot be denied that there are imaginable cases in which the harm that could be prevented by a rare instance of pure interrogational torture would be so enormous as to outweigh the cruelty of the torture itself and, possibly, the enormous potential harm which would result if what was intended to be a rare instance was actually the breaching of the dam which would lead to a torrent of torture."  (p. 141)

> "There is a standard philosopher's example which someone always invokes: suppose a fanatic, perfectly willing to die rather than collaborate in the thwarting of his own scheme, has set a hidden nuclear device to explode in the heart of Paris. There is no time to evacuate the innocent people or even the movable art treasures—the only hope of preventing tragedy is to torture the perpetrator, find the device, and deactivate it."  (p. 141)

> "I can see no way to deny the permissibility of torture in a case just like this."  (p. 141; "just like this" italic in the original)

> "But there is a saying in jurisprudence that hard cases make bad law, and there might well be one in philosophy that artificial cases make bad ethics. If the example is made sufficiently extraordinary, the conclusion that the torture is permissible is secure. But one cannot easily draw conclusions for ordinary cases from extraordinary ones, and as the situations described become more likely, the conclusion that the torture is permissible becomes more debatable."  (pp. 141–142)

> "Notice how unlike the circumstances of an actual choice about torture the philosopher's example is."  (p. 142)

> "Most important, such incidents do not continue to happen."  (p. 142)

> "Any judgment that torture could be sanctioned in an isolated case without seriously weakening existing inhibitions against the more general use of torture rests on empirical hypotheses about the psychology and politics of torture. There is considerable evidence of all torture's metastatic tendency."  (pp. 142–143; "is" in "There is considerable" italic in the original)

> "Does the possibility that torture might be justifiable in some of the rarefied situations which can be imagined provide any reason to consider relaxing the legal prohibitions against it? Absolutely not."  (p. 143)

> "An act of torture ought to remain illegal so that anyone who sincerely believes such an act to be the least available evil is placed in the position of needing to justify his or her act morally in order to defend himself or herself legally."  (p. 143)

> "If the situation approximates those in the imaginary examples in which torture seems possible to justify, a judge can surely be expected to suspend the sentence."  (p. 143)

Relied on for: Shue's 1978 statement of the Paris case as "a standard
philosopher's example"; his concession that he can "see no way to deny" its
permissibility; "artificial cases make bad ethics" and the gap between the
example and "an actual choice"; "metastatic tendency"; keep torture illegal,
suspended sentence.
Context: the passage closes a paper that first argues torture violates the
prohibition on assault upon the defenceless (pp. 124–136) and distinguishes
"terroristic" from "interrogational" torture. Earlier on p. 141 Shue says a
complete argument for interrogational torture would need "a full
specification of all necessary conditions" and warns of torture becoming "a
standard operating procedure". Between the two p. 142 quotations he lists the
example's stipulations (the victim "is the perpetrator", "the device will
destroy the city", a priest and doctor present). The sentence "Most
important, such incidents do not continue to happen." introduces a further
assumption of the philosopher's example (that the torture does not spread),
not a report about the world; the page does not use it as a factual claim. Coady (SEP "Dirty Hands",
Fall 2024, §7; `sep-dirty-hands-fall-2024-coady-map.md`) reports that Shue
later "criticises his own admission" (Shue 2006, "Torture in Dreamland");
the 2006 paper was not read.

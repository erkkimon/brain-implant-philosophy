> "There are three different interpretations of the kind of contradiction Kant has (or ought to have) in mind found in the literature."  (ms. p. 2)

> "if the maxim were universalized, the action or policy that it proposes would be inconceivable."  (ms. p. 2, the Logical Contradiction Interpretation)

> "it would be contradictory to will your maxim as a law for a system of nature teleologically conceived"  (ms. p. 2, the Teleological Contradiction Interpretation)

> "the contradiction is that your maxim would be self-defeating if universalized: your action would become ineffectual for the achievement of your purpose if everyone (tried to) use it for that purpose."  (ms. p. 3, the Practical Contradiction Interpretation)

> "All he shows is that the consequences of their universal adoption would be such as no one would choose to incur."  (ms. p. 5, quoting Mill, Utilitarianism ch. I; checked also against Gutenberg #11224, where "consequences" is italic)

> "Mill thinks that Kant's view really amounts to an appeal to utility, to what we would now call rule-utilitarianism."  (ms. p. 5)

> "Versions of a Logical Contradiction Interpretation have been"  (ms. p. 6; continues, after a note number, "defended by Dietrichson, Kemp, and Wood, among others.")

> "We can make the maxim one of killing children that tend to cry at night more than average, in order to get enough sleep."  (ms. p. 8)

> "Either Dietrichson's maxim or mine could clearly be a universal law without a logical contradiction."  (ms. p. 8)

> "In my view, it is the difficulty about natural actions which is most damaging to the Logical Contradiction Interpretation."  (ms. p. 12)

> "I will call these the Hegelian objections, since they were originally put forward by Hegel and promulgated by Bradley and others."  (ms. p. 12)

> "One of the Hegelian objections is that universal law test is empty."  (ms. p. 12)

> "The interesting question, Hegel goes on, is just why there should be property, and about this Kant says nothing."  (ms. p. 12, Korsgaard's paraphrase of Hegel 1803 after H. B. Acton)

> "The other Hegelian objection pulls in the opposite direction: instead of showing the test to be empty, it shows it to be too strong."  (ms. p. 13)

> "In short, every duty which presupposes something to be negated is no duty; it is an immoral rule, because self-contradictory."  (ms. p. 13, quoting Bradley)

> "The argument that shows this seems to me to be an almost decisive one in favor of this interpretation."  (ms. p. 21, of the Practical Contradiction Interpretation)

> "it shows even more clearly why those objections miss the moral point of a universalization test."  (ms. p. 23)

> "But on the Practical Contradiction Interpretation it is the failure of efficacy, not the non-existence, that really matters."  (ms. p. 26)

> "His debate with Benjamin Constant about whether one may lie to the murderer whose victim is hidden in your house, and his insistence that there is never a right to revolution, are infamous examples of cases in which his view seems to forbid us to try to prevent or to set right the wrongs committed by others."  (ms. p. 30)

Relied on for: the three readings of the contradiction in conception and their named
holders; Mill's objection as Korsgaard quotes and glosses it; the Dietrichson counterexample
(a wrongful maxim that passes a logical-contradiction test); the two "Hegelian objections"
(empty, too strong) and Korsgaard's reply; her own preference for the Practical
Contradiction Interpretation.
Context: the Teleological Contradiction Interpretation is the second of the three (ms. p. 2);
Korsgaard says the practical reading answers the Hegelian objections "Like the Logical
Contradiction Interpretation" (ms. p. 23). The ms. p. 30 sentence opens her closing remark
that the view handles best "the temptation to make" an exception for oneself; she adds "I do
not say that Kant is unable to give us an account of these cases."

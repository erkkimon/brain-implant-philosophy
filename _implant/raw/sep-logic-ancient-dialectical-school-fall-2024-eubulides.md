> "Eubulides of Miletus (mid-4th c.) is on record as the inventor of both the Liar and the Sorites paradox."  (§1)

> "problematic—an insight also documented by the discovery of the Liar paradox by Eubulides of Miletus (mid-4th c. BCE) shortly thereafter."  (Plato section, after Sophist and Cratylus on truth)

> "Besides the Liar, Eubulides is said to have been the originator of several other logical paradoxes, including the Sorites."  (fallacies and paradoxes)

> "sometimes referred to as dialecticians (see entry ‘Dialectical School’) and possibly influenced by Eubulides, conceived of logic as a logic of propositions."  (§4)

> "The Stoics recognized the importance of both the Liar and the Sorites paradoxes (Cicero Acad. 2.95–8, Plut. Comm.Not. 1059D–E, Chrys. Log. Zet. col.IX)."  (§5.5)

> "for the paradoxes of presupposition, known in antiquity as the Horned One, they produced a Russellian-type solution based on a hidden scope ambiguity of negation (cf. Bobzien 2012)"  (§5.5, of the Stoics)

## B. Dialectical School

Source: Susanne Bobzien & Matthew Duncombe, "Dialectical School", The
  Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2024 Edition); first published
  2004-08-27, substantive revision 2023-08-09. Preamble, §§1, 6.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/dialectical-school/
  (copyrighted; excerpts only). The brief's slug "megarians" and the
  linked "megaric-school" both returned "Document Not Found" in the Fall
  2024 archive on this date.
Retrieved: 2026-09-27 (archive HTML fetched, converted to text, grepped)

> "The ‘Dialectical School’ denotes a group of early Hellenistic philosophers that were loosely connected by philosophizing in the — Socratic — tradition of Eubulides of Miletus and by their interest in logical paradoxes, propositional logic and dialectical expertise."  (preamble)

> "Those later reports give Clinomachus of Thurii, a pupil of Eubulides of Miletus, as founder of the school, but may have been given this title only in hindsight (DL 1.19 and 2.112, Sedley 1977 n.16)."  (§1)

> "Euphantus of Olyntus, another pupil of Eubulides, probably born before 349 BC, may have been the earliest member of the school."  (§1)

> "DL 7.44 lists the Reaper alongside the Veiled Man, the Horned Man and the No Man, and DL 2.111 gives Diodorus as the author of the Veiled and Horned arguments."  (§6)

## C. Sorites Paradox, §1 (supplement to sep-sorites-and-liar-fall-2024-paradox-formulations-and-responses.md)

Source: Dominic Hyde & Diana Raffman, "Sorites Paradox", SEP (Fall 2024
  Edition); substantive revision 2018-03-26. §1.
Original: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/sorites-paradox/
Retrieved: 2026-09-27

> "Although we don’t know his motivations for introducing it (along with several other legendary puzzles), the paradox was later used by Greek philosophers as a dialectical weapon, most notably by the Sceptics against the Stoics’ claims to knowledge."  (§1)

> "‘Bald’, ‘heap’, ‘tall’, ‘old’, and ‘blue’ are prime examples of vague terms"  (preamble)

> "For example, since a man with 1 hair on his head is bald, and since, for any number of hairs n, if a man with n hairs is bald then so is a man with n+1 hairs, every number n is such that a man with n hairs on his head is bald."  (§2, the inductive form)

Relied on for: Eubulides' date (mid-4th c. BCE), the SEP authors'
attributions of the Liar and Sorites to him and their hedges ("on record",
"said to have been", "usually credited"); his pupils and the Dialectical
School's descent from him; the Stoic treatment of the Liar, Sorites and
Horned One; the competing Diodorus attribution as the SEP reads DL.
Context: Bobzien's §1 sentence continues "But logic as a fully systematic
discipline begins with Aristotle". The Dialectical School §6 passage is
about the Reaper argument, whose Diodorean authorship the authors discuss.

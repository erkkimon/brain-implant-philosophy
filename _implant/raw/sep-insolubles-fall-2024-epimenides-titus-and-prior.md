# SEP "Insolubles" (Spade & Read) — St. Paul's reference to Epimenides (§1.2) and why it is said not to be a strict liar (note 5, citing Prior 1958)

> "One initially plausible stimulus for the medieval discussions would appear to be the Epistle to Titus 1:12: “One of themselves, even a prophet of their own, said, The Cretians [= Cretans] are always liars, evil beasts, slow bellies”. The Cretan in question is traditionally said to have been the sixth-century BCE thinker Epimenides. For this reason, the Liar Paradox is nowadays sometimes referred to as the “Epimenides”."  (§1.2)

> "Yet, blatant as the paradox is here, and authoritative as the Epistle was taken to be, not a single medieval author is known to have discussed or even acknowledged the logical and semantic problems this text poses."  (§1.2)

> "It is not known who was the first to link this text with the Liar Paradox."  (§1.2)

> "It is sometimes observed that Epimenides’s statement is not really a paradox of the Liar type at all."  (note 5)

> "If he did say “The Cretans [i.e., all Cretans] are always liars, evil beasts, slow bellies”, then his statement is true only if it is false, since he himself is a Cretan and so always lying. Thus it cannot be true. But if it is false, all that follows is that some Cretans are not always liars and evil beasts and slow bellies. It does not follow that Epimenides’s own remark is not a lie, and so it does not follow that it is not false."  (note 5)

> "Hence there is no real paradox at all (or at least, no contradiction; the paradox or puzzle, as Prior 1958 pointed out, is that we can infer from his statement the surprising fact that at least one Cretan utterance is true); Epimenides’s statement is just false. This much is correct about what Epimenides said."  (note 5)

> "But St. Paul (or whoever wrote the epistle) goes on to say in the very next verse (Titus 1:13) “This witness is true”. Hence there was ample opportunity for a reader of this text to be introduced to the Liar Paradox, even if he did not already know about it."  (note 5)

Relied on for: the Titus attribution and its medieval silence; the
consistency argument (true only if false, but falsity does not yield
truth); Prior 1958's point as Spade & Read report it; the role of Titus 1:13.
Context: §1.2 sits between §1.1 (Cicero and other ancient passages never
cited in the insolubilia-literature) and §1.3 (Aristotle's Sophistical
Refutations 25, which the authors say did frame the medieval discussion).
Note 5 opens "For a partial list of such discussions, see Spade 1973,
p. 296 n. 24." The bibliography lists "Prior, A.N., 1958, “Epimenides the
Cretan”, The Journal of Symbolic Logic, 23(3): 261–66."

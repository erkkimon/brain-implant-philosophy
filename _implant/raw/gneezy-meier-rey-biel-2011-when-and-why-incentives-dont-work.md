# Gneezy, Meier & Rey-Biel 2011 — when and why incentives (don't) work: price effect vs psychological effect, Titmuss, nuclear waste, day-care, blood

Source: Uri Gneezy, Stephan Meier and Pedro Rey-Biel, "When and Why Incentives (Don't) Work to Modify Behavior", Journal of Economic Perspectives 25(4), Fall 2011, pp. 191–210.
Original: doi:10.1257/jep.25.4.191 (DOI content negotiation checked; free to read at https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.25.4.191)

> "Monetary incentives have two kinds of effects: the standard direct price effect, which makes the incentivized behavior more attractive, and an indirect psychological effect. In some cases, the psychological effect works in an opposite direction to the price effect and can crowd out the incentivized behavior."  (p. 192)

> "Alternatively, offering incentives could signal that the principal does not trust the agent’s intrinsic motivation."  (p. 192)

> "Economists’ interest in crowding out started around the same time as in psychology due to Titmuss (1970), who argued that paying people to donate blood broke established social norms about voluntary contribution and could result in a reduction of the fraction of people who wish to donate."  (p. 193)

> "For example, Frey and Oberholzer-Gee (1997) show that in offering members of a community a large monetary compensation for a nuclear waste site’s presence, the principal signals that the risks involved are high, and thus community members may be less willing to accept the plant."  (p. 193)

> "In their experiment, a daycare began charging late-coming parents a small fine of 10 New Israeli shekels (about $3 at the time)."  (p. 194)

> "One interpretation of this result is information: the parents did not initially know how important it was to arrive on time."  (p. 194)

> "The relatively small fine signaled to parents that arriving late was not that important."  (p. 194)

> "Of course the crowding-out evidence discussed does not mean that using incentives to obtain behavioral changes will always be counterproductive."  (p. 194)

> "In their daycare study, Gneezy and Rustichini (2000b) argue that a possible explanation of the behavior change in the long run is a change in the social norm."  (pp. 200–201)

> "Although recent evidence is mixed, it seems to show that noncash material incentives do not have detrimental effects on blood supply."  (p. 202)

> "However, the drop in blood supply is only statistically significant for women, decreasing from 52 to 30 percent when only cash incentives are offered."  (p. 202; reporting Mellström & Johannesson 2008)

Relied on for: the price-effect/psychological-effect statement; the information/signalling reading of Frey & Oberholzer-Gee 1997 and of the day-care study; the social-norm reading; the blood-donation evidence as these authors summarise it; the caveat that crowding-out evidence does not show incentives always backfire.
Context: Gneezy is a co-author of the day-care study. The paper reviews education, public goods and lifestyle (exercise, smoking) incentives and frames its aim as identifying "when incentives do and do not work".

> "Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) is the central figure in modern philosophy."  (preamble)

> "The fundamental idea of Kant’s “critical philosophy” – especially in his three Critiques: the Critique of Pure Reason (1781, 1787), the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), and the Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) – is human autonomy."  (preamble)

> "Immanuel Kant was born April 22, 1724 in Königsberg, near the southeastern shore of the Baltic Sea."  (§1)

> "Kant’s parents were Pietist and he attended a Pietist school, the Collegium Fridericianum, from ages eight through fifteen."  (§1)

> "Kant’s favorite teacher was Martin Knutzen (1713–1751), a Pietist who was heavily influenced by both Wolff and the English philosopher John Locke (1632–1704)."  (§1)

> "These ideas often stemmed from British sentimentalist philosophers such as David Hume (1711–1776) and Francis Hutcheson (1694–1747), some of whose texts were translated into German in the mid-1750s; and from the Swiss philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778), who published a flurry of works in the early 1760s."  (§1)

> "He finally returned to Königsberg in 1754 and began teaching at the Albertina the following year."  (§1)

> "In 1770, at the age of forty-six, Kant was appointed to the chair in logic and metaphysics at the Albertina, after teaching for fifteen years as an unsalaried lecturer and working since 1766 as a sublibrarian to supplement his income."  (§1)

> "In 1794 his chair at Jena passed to J. G. Fichte, who had visited the master in Königsberg and whose first book, Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation (1792), was published anonymously and initially mistaken for a work by Kant himself."  (§1; "his chair" is K. L. Reinhold's)

> "This catapulted Fichte to fame, but soon he too moved away from Kant and developed an original position quite at odds with Kant’s, which Kant finally repudiated publicly in 1799 (12:370–371)."  (§1)

> "Kant retired from teaching in 1796."  (§1)

> "Kant died February 12, 1804, just short of his eightieth birthday."  (§1)

> "Kant argues that we can comply with our duty to promote the highest good only if we believe in the immortality of the soul and the existence of God."  (§6.2)

"which drew a censure from the Prussian King"), Toward Perpetual Peace (1795) and the
Metaphysics of Morals (1797) as "Kant's most mature work in moral philosophy" (of the Doctrine
of Virtue). §6.2 adds that both postulate arguments are "subjective" in the sense of showing how
we must conceive the highest good, while Kant claims they also have "an objective basis".

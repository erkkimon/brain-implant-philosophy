# Appeal to authority

Family: authority. Written under
[Reporting, not endorsing](../../conventions/reporting-not-endorsing.md):
each classification below belongs to the theory that makes it, and the
implant makes none. Sources are excerpted in `raw/` under the names given.

## Also known as

- *Argumentum ad verecundiam* — Locke's coinage (Latin *verecundia*,
  modesty): "This I think may be called ARGUMENTUM AD VERECUNDIAM"
  ([Locke, *Essay* IV.xvii.19](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10616);
  excerpt: `raw/locke-essay-4-17-19-22-four-arguments.md`). The name
  points at the *audience's* modesty in declining to contradict an
  established reputation, not at the authority's expertise.
- *Argument from expert opinion*, *appeal to expertise* — the names used in
  argumentation-scheme theory
  ([Walton, Reed & Macagno 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511802034);
  the SEP entry on fallacies, [Hansen, Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/),
  §3.6).
- *Ethos* — in classical rhetoric, persuasion "by moral character" of the
  speaker, one of Aristotle's three proofs furnished by the speech
  ([*Rhetoric* I.2.3–4, 1356a1–13](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0060:book=1:chapter=2),
  Freese; excerpt: `raw/aristotle-rhetoric-1-2-1356a-three-proofs.md`).
- *Authority principle* — in the social-influence literature, one of the
  compliance principles reviewed by
  [Cialdini & Goldstein 2004](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142015).

## Mechanism

As described by named sources, in their own vocabulary:

- **Rhetorical.** Aristotle: "The orator persuades by moral character when
  his speech is delivered in such a manner as to render him worthy of
  confidence; for we feel confidence in a greater degree and more readily
  in persons of worth in regard to everything in general, but where there
  is no certainty and there is room for doubt, our confidence is
  absolute" (*Rhetoric* I.2.4, 1356a5–8, Freese). He adds that the
  confidence "must be due to the speech itself, not to any preconceived
  idea of the speaker's character", and reports — and rejects — a view of
  earlier handbook writers that the speaker's worth contributes nothing.
- **Social.** Locke: the appeal works because "it is thought a breach of
  modesty for others to derogate any way from" an established dignity, and
  "it is looked upon as insolence, for a man to set up and adhere to his
  own opinion against the current stream of antiquity"
  (*Essay* IV.xvii.19).
- **Epistemic.** In the scheme reconstruction the move is an inference:
  "E is an expert in subject area S; E asserts p based on E's knowledge of
  S; So, p" — which, when its critical questions are answered, "creates a
  presumption for the conclusion—but not a guarantee, for the reasoning
  is defeasible" ([Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/),
  §3.6, reporting Walton; excerpt: `raw/sep-fallacies-fall-2024-excerpts.md`).
- **Behavioural.** [Milgram 1963](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040525)
  reports that a majority of participants continued administering what
  they believed were painful shocks when instructed by an experimenter;
  the study is about obedience to an authority's *instruction* rather
  than assent to an authority's *claim*, and is cited here only because
  the social-influence literature files both under one principle
  ([Cialdini & Goldstein 2004](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142015)).

## Example

Constructed (not a quotation): *"The Surgeon General's report concludes
that the exposure is safe at this level, so the exposure is safe at this
level."* The same sentence is an instance of the scheme in §3.6 of the SEP
entry (E = the office, S = public health, p = the safety claim), of Locke's
*ad verecundiam* (the audience is invited to defer), and of Aristotelian
ethos if the speaker is the office-holder. Which description applies is a
question about the theory, not the sentence.

## Classified as a fallacy by

- **The standard treatment** (the textbook tradition, named as such by
  [Hamblin 1970](https://doi.org/10.2307/2218351) — the DOI resolves to a
  contemporary review; the book itself is *Fallacies*, Methuen, 1970).
  The SEP entry's own list describes it thus: "The ad verecundiam fallacy
  concerns appeals to authority or expertise. Fundamentally, the fallacy
  involves accepting as evidence for a proposition the pronouncement of
  someone who is taken to be an authority but is either not really an
  authority or a relevant authority" ([Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §1).
  The criterion: the authority is absent, irrelevant, or one side of a
  divided field.
- **Pragma-dialectics** ([van Eemeren & Grootendorst 1987](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00136779);
  [2004](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511616389)): a fallacy is any move
  that violates a rule of the ideal critical discussion — "every violation
  of any of the rules of the discussion procedure for conducting a critical
  discussion" is one (2004, p. 175, as quoted by
  [Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §3.5).
  This theory classes an appeal to authority as fallacious when it is used
  to *evade* the burden of proof or to *close* a discussion, and not
  otherwise. The criterion is procedural, not about the authority.
- **Walton's scheme theory** — conditionally. If the critical questions
  cannot be answered and the argument still has "a semblance of
  correctness about it in [the] context, and poses a serious obstacle to
  the realization of the goal of the dialog", it is a fallacy
  ([Walton 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-009-9657-y), "Defeasible
  reasoning and informal fallacies", *Synthese* 179, p. 380, as quoted in
  [Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §3.6).

## Classified as legitimate by

- **Aristotle**: character is not merely permitted but, in Freese's
  rendering, "constitutes the most effective means of proof"
  (*Rhetoric* I.2.4, 1356a13).
- **Locke** — who classes it as an *argument*, not a fallacy. The SEP entry notes: "Locke
  did not speak of ad-arguments as fallacies—that was left to others to do
  later—but rather as kinds of arguments" (§2.4). Locke does, in the same
  passage, rank the four: only *ad judicium*, "the using of proofs drawn
  from any of the foundations of knowledge or probability", "brings true
  instruction with it" (IV.xvii.22). So Locke reports the appeal as
  effective at securing assent and as not advancing knowledge — both
  claims are his.
- **Argumentation-scheme theory**: "Schemes do not identify fallacies but
  rather argument kinds that are sometimes used fairly, and, other times,
  fallaciously" ([Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §3.6).
  The scheme is presumptive and defeasible; its use is proper when the
  critical questions are satisfied
  ([Walton, Reed & Macagno 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511802034)).
- **Social epistemology**: [Goldman 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x)
  takes it as given that "novices ought to turn to experts for intellectual
  guidance" and asks the *novice/expert problem* — how a novice can tell
  which putative expert to trust — listing five evidential sources: the
  contending experts' arguments; agreement from further putative experts;
  appraisals by "meta-experts" including credentials; evidence of the
  experts' interests and biases; and their track records (Goldman 2001,
  p. 93, as reported in [Frances & Matheson, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/disagreement/),
  §4; excerpt: `raw/sep-disagreement-fall-2024-goldman-novice-expert.md`).
  Hardwig (1985, 1991) is reported in the same place as holding that a
  novice's reliance on an expert "must necessarily be blind".

## Critique

Published objections, on both sides, attributed:

- **Of the fallacy label.** Hamblin's charge against the standard
  treatment as a whole — "as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as
  could be imagined—incredibly tradition bound, yet lacking in logic and
  in historical sense" (Hamblin 1970, ch. 1, as quoted in
  [Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §2.10)
  — is the origin of the post-1970 literature that reconstructs the appeal
  as a scheme rather than a fallacy.
- **Of the scheme's optimism.** Goldman's novice/expert problem is itself a
  critique of any appeal whose critical questions the audience is in no
  position to answer: the questions "Is E really an expert?" and "Did E
  say p in a professional capacity?" presuppose access the novice may lack
  ([Goldman 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x)).
- **Of the audience's competence to resist.** Locke's mechanism — modesty
  before reputation — predicts that the appeal works regardless of whether
  the authority is relevant; the behavioural literature on obedience and
  compliance ([Milgram 1963](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040525);
  [Cialdini & Goldstein 2004](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142015))
  is read by that literature as evidence of the same.
- **A structural note the implant adds**, and owns as its own comparison
  ([manifest G4](../../vision/manifest.md)): the five theories above do not
  disagree about what the move *is*; they disagree about what question to
  ask of it — does it persuade (Aristotle), does it advance knowledge
  (Locke), does the inference hold (Walton), does it respect the discussion
  (pragma-dialectics), can the hearer evaluate it (Goldman).

## How to spot it

Observable markers, from the sources:

- A claim supported by *who* holds it — a title, a credential, an office,
  an institution's name, "experts agree", "the science says" — with no
  reason given beyond the holder (the scheme's second premise present, its
  grounds absent; [Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §3.6).
- Authority outside its field — the SEP list's own example is "when
  celebrities endorse commercial products or social movements" (§1).
- Appeal to one side of a divided field: "when there is controversy, and
  authorities are divided, it is an error to base one's view on the
  authority of just some of them" (§1) — the marker is that the
  disagreement is not mentioned.
- Character-work in the speech itself — a speaker establishing their own
  trustworthiness before or instead of the argument — which is ethos in
  Aristotle's sense (1356a4–13) and is the form the
  [write-persuasively](../../skills/write-persuasively.md) skill offers
  under this page when the user allows it.

## How to answer it

Counter-moves the literature describes:

- **Ask the critical questions** of the scheme — is E an expert in S; was
  p asserted in E's professional capacity; and, in the fuller list used by
  Walton, Reed & Macagno, is p consistent with what other experts say, and
  is E's assertion based on evidence
  ([Walton, Reed & Macagno 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511802034);
  the two-question form is in [Hansen, SEP Fall 2024](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/fallacies/), §3.6).
  On the scheme account, "answering No to either of the two questions
  cancels the presumption for the conclusion".
- **Apply Goldman's five sources** when one cannot assess the expertise
  directly — the contending experts' arguments, further expert agreement,
  meta-expert appraisal, interests and biases, track record
  ([Goldman 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x), p. 93).
- **Ask for the *ad judicium*** — Locke's fourth kind, "proofs drawn from
  any of the foundations of knowledge or probability" (IV.xvii.22): the
  request that the authority's reasons, not the authority, be put on the
  table.
- **Under pragma-dialectics**, point to the rule being violated: if the
  appeal is used to shift the burden of proof or to end the discussion,
  name that as the move
  ([van Eemeren & Grootendorst 1987](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00136779)).
